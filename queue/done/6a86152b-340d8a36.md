## Result

\### Register delta

LIKED-era prompts are not terse or vague: their median is 73 words overall and 87 words on non-deltas; 71% specify a camera/vantage, 45% say `wide`, and 17% use a close/detail register. They lead with a recognizable world, one readable arrangement, and the camera—70% begin with scene framing, only 29% with a cast slug. FRESH is shorter, more cast/chain-led, and leaves cameras implicit more often. At dispatch, FRESH also places an 80-word style recipe after the scene, whereas the actual liked-run forge appended no suffix. The actionable doctrine is **scene-and-camera first, payload last**: specify concrete world objects and one decisive camera/scale relation, remove authoring rationale from pixel prose, and let the shot’s visual payload—not the house-style recipe—own the provider-weighted final position.

\### Top five findings

1. **The actual liked dispatch did not use its stored suffix.** Current forge appends an 80-word/535-character suffix after every payload. This is the strongest unisolated engine-input difference.

2. **FRESH overcorrected v2’s standalone churn into held-chain dominance.** FRESH has 109 deltas and 29 standalones; LIKED has 73 and 74. A2 has only 2 FRESH standalones versus 9 LIKED, reducing independent compositional resets.

3. **The depiction vocabulary moved away from LIKED.** LIKED has 6 literal shots versus FRESH’s 43, but far more symbolic stand-ins, reaction shots, and number-object compositions. FRESH concentrates 59 ironic counterpoints.

4. **The reset genuinely fixed v2’s file-level machinery.** Figureless share rose from 10.2% to 22.4%; crowd shots fell from 72 to 55; `cropped` fell from 197 to 2; row/lane/aisle wording fell from 45 to 0; real cadence outliers fell from 115 to 6.

5. **The LIKED camera and palette distribution remains unrecovered.** Explicit vantage appears in 63/245 FRESH prompts versus 152/214 LIKED; A4 is only 6/69 versus 39/57. FRESH also remains concentrated around `cream` and `charcoal`, while LIKED distributes color and temperature more broadly.
