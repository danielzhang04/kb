# The Second Take — Storytelling Grammar

**What this is:** the channel's single source of truth for *how a video is told* — the voice, the story
shape, the staging. It is a **generative grammar**: kinds of move to invent from, never a template to
clone. Read it every run; where it conflicts with generic doctrine, this doc wins for The Second Take.

**The North Star:** a smart, funny friend telling you an unbelievable true story — excited to tell it,
reacting to it as they go, explaining it with comparisons you instantly picture. Not a writer crafting
prose, not a finance teacher. **Raw and a little messy beats polished and bland.**

**The voice reference:** the approved excerpts in `channels/the-second-take/example-scripts.md`, source
of every inline example below. Absorb it before writing: a register, not a quarry and not a minefield.

---

## 1. The voice

**1.1 — Casual friend, plain words.** No literary phrasing, and no term a normal person wouldn't say out
loud ("sovereign debt," "underwritten," "yield") — say the plain version or cut it. **When a term or
mechanism the viewer may not know has to stay, contextualize it in the same breath:** a plain gloss or
an analogy pitched at someone who has never thought about it, never an assumption of knowledge
("computers run on these things called hard drives, which are basically where your computer keeps
everything it remembers"). This is a money-*story*, not a finance tutorial: deliver the amazing fact,
skip the mechanics lesson. **Spoken grammar beats written grammar every time:** "none of them are" is
what a person says out loud, so it is what gets written. Contractions are the default ("That's one
year," never "That is one year" as a dramatic punch), and the default reach is the stock idiom a friend
already has ("fell off a cliff," "raking it in," "head on the chopping block"); a fresh image survives
only where it parses instantly as talk, and a phrase that sounds written ("everything went into
reverse," "this bleeding thing") is the defect. *Test: would you actually say this line to a friend?*
(Formatting: no em/en dashes, ever — an AI tell the viewer can't hear. Breath is periods, commas, and
colons. `script.md` is pure voiceover prose: no cues, no brackets, no stage directions.)
> ✗ "underwritten by a respectable banking house, in notes of a hundred"
> ✓ "MacGregor, seeing that people believed him, then convinces the Bank of Scotland's official printer
> to make him 5,000 custom Poyais dollar notes."

**Every line parses on the first listen, and names the thing instead of the idea of it.** There is no
rewind on a voiceover: a sentence whose meaning waits on the viewer resolving a pronoun ("That's it
doing that") is gone by the time they get there. Worse is **concept-prose**: naming the *shape* of what
happened instead of what happened ("everything after this is people trying to make the world's version
match his target"). Say who did what, to what, and what came of it, in the order it happened.
> "They rented a warehouse, and went shopping. They bought 26,000 bricks from the Colorado Brick
> Company, handpicked so that a box with a brick felt and weighed exactly like a box of hard drives."

**Paragraphs are idea blocks, four to five sentences on average.** A block carries one idea to its end
and stops; the next block starts the next idea. Short sentences are how a speaker lands a hit, and they
land *inside* a block, powered by the longer sentences around them.
> "The company was MiniScribe, a hard drive manufacturer out of Colorado, founded in 1980 by a guy
> named Terry Johnson. And they were HOT. By 1984 they were pulling in 125 million dollars a year
> selling hard drives to IBM."

A sentence alone as its own paragraph is the defect, and a page of them is the staccato monotone this
pipeline defaults to: every line gets identical weight, so nothing lands. Block length varies; short
sentences *inside* a block are how people talk at speed ("Peru. Chile. Argentina.").

**Tense: past is the storytelling default.** The story already happened, so tell it in the past ("They
bought 26,000 bricks from the Colorado Brick Company"). Present tense is for framing the viewer into a
period ("We're in the 1980s"), for mechanics still true today, and, where the energy wants it, for a
scheme's hottest stretch. What is banned is the present-tense still life: a scene with nobody doing
anything in it. Put people in the sentence, doing something.

**1.2 — Land the point inside the telling; no grandeur buttons.** The recurring failure is ending a beat
on a summary or profound line ("the mania did the work"). Land the point where it happens and keep
going. **The mechanical check:** read the last sentence of every block. If it sums up or moralizes
instead of landing on a fact, an action, or the push into what comes next, fold it forward or cut it. A
dry *factual* aside is the good kind ("Or so they said."). Chopping a button into a fragment fixes
nothing: the defect is the summarizing, not the length. The block ends on something that happened.

**1.3 — The wit is abundant, warm, and rides the facts.** Jokes, metaphors, and asides are not seasoning
for a few beats; they run **throughout**. One or two pulls or reactions per block is the normal running
density, and two consecutive blocks with neither is register drift: the pipeline's signature failure in
a script's back half. **The register is warm, irreverent, and loaded with pop culture:** a narrator who
is plainly enjoying this. Dry irony is welcome where it lands, but smirking distance as the house
register is out: it reads as somebody who thinks the story is beneath him. Not everything is a joke, and
the flat sentence between two funny ones is what makes them funny. **Profanity is in bounds at the "ass"
and "shit" grade and stops there** ("random shit lying around"); the f-word is not the channel. Every
piece of wit still rides a fact: delete the joke and the fact survives; delete the fact and the joke
collapses.
> "Yeah, that's his real name." · "That's a chunk of land the size of Maryland for a bar tab and a
> necklace." · "My Peloton bike is a scam but these guys? Next level."

**1.4 — Analogies are the explanatory engine.** The strongest tool this channel has: a modern comparison
the viewer instantly pictures IS the explanation, so reach for one wherever a mechanism, a scale, or an
absurdity would need a lecture. Runs are welcome, and a good one can recur as a callback. **The pulls
skew heavily to named cultural references** (Ramsay, Doctor Strange, Samsung against Apple, Ocean's
Eleven); generic situational comparisons are the minority, and the drawn-out multi-sentence generic
metaphor is the defect. **Setup-then-apply is licensed:** hand the viewer the reference first, then
apply it ("You know that Gordon Ramsay show where he goes around reinventing failing restaurants? Q.T.
Wiles was the Gordon Ramsay of the business world.").
> Scale: "Accounting for inflation, they were making as much money as Reddit."
> Mechanism: "It's like reading a textbook, you know? You see that many pages and you just skim through
> and believe the pretty pictures."
> Character: "MacGregor was like the Bernie Madoff of the 1820s, except Madoff sold securities and
> MacGregor sold a fake country."
> Situation: "This was Ocean's Eleven level stuff."
> Era: "It was the dot-com bubble of the 1820s."

Cut a comparison only when it repeats a point already landed, derails the story, or makes the fact
harder to understand. **The universality bar governs every pull, not just the analogies:** a joke, a
metaphor, a turn of phrase, an era anchor, each has to be something a general viewer pictures instantly.
Franchises and memes with staying power are the right material: Doctor Strange's sanctums, Ocean's
Eleven, Megamind, Jordan Belfort, a Peloton, what Reddit makes in a year. A pull fails the bar when it
fades fast, or when a general viewer would need it explained, never because it is popular. The approved
excerpts are the calibration for where that bar sits.

**1.5 — The narrator is present and reacting.** The energy comes from a narrator who is IN the telling:
direct questions answered casually ("After all, who would move to a new country without local
currency?"), conversational joints ("So anyway," "you know," "See?"), honest reactions ("I'd move to a
country with golden rivers too, you know," "The balls on this guy, huh?"), casual repetition when
momentum wants it, and first-person asides. Generic "you" is normal speech and casual address is the
voice, but *staging* the viewer as a participant is dead ("So put yourself in that room. You are a
middle manager…" is scratched whole): talk to the viewer, never cast them. Where the research supports a
genuine multi-move scheme, the narrator may announce **Step N** ("**Step 1: Create the Fake Country.**")
— it exposes a mechanism, never bare chronology, and runs as a complete sequential set.

**1.6 — State the amazing thing flat; never pad credibility.** Banned: "that part is real," "he actually
did," "he really did," "seriously," "the maddening thing," "genuinely audacious." State the insane fact
plainly and let the *viewer* be the one amazed. The identity aside is exactly "Yeah, that's his real
name." ("Real" survives where it contrasts real money, land, or a real name with the fake.) **Heat is
carried by the delivery, not by insistence:** capitals on a word the voice would lean on ("And they were
HOT.") and the knowing stock phrase that builds somebody up ("they brought in the man, the myth, the
legend: Q.T. Wiles") are correct: that is how the line is *said*. What stays dead is every written move
that manufactures heat on the page: trailer drama, the clipped fragment carrying no joke and no new fact
("Everything changed."), the dramatic uncontracted punch ("That is one year."), and the paired parallel
aphorism ("The 550 is the number people remember. The 128 is the number that got paid."). A single dry
ironic capper stays correct ("Or so they said.").

## 2. The story

**2.1 — The hook gets them in the door; it does not tell the story.** Its one job is intrigue: leave the
viewer holding exactly one question the story now owes them. **Open on somebody doing something, in a
time and place the viewer already pictures, with the names on the page:** an actor, an event, a familiar
anchor. The **shape is open**. Two that work: the **era drop-in**, which sets the viewer down in a
period on anchors everyone pictures and lets the narrator say plainly what kind of story is coming; and
the **paradox cold-open**, which states the settled, impossible-sounding fact and withholds the
mechanism. Concrete stakes (a number, a count, a fortune) go wherever they land hardest.
> Era drop-in: "We're in the 1980s. Home to big hair, Pac-Man, and one of the funniest corporate scams
> that you've never heard of."
>
> Paradox cold-open: "And one of those companies packaged 26,000... well, the title gives it away...
> red clay bricks into little boxes labelled 'hard drive', and sold them to customers around the world."

The reveal is one sentence and a plain doorway: itemizing the scheme's details (serial numbers, labels,
customers) spends the caper before the story starts. What stays dead is outlining the beats ("first he
did X, then Y"), "he was not some nobody," "welcome back," and the still life: a scene with nobody in
it, a company unnamed to manufacture mystery, a fact so abstract nothing happens in the sentence. Naming
the company, the man, and the year in the first breath buys the viewer something to hold.

**Person-led packaging → person-led hook:** when the title/thumbnail promise a person, open ON that
person and name them within two sentences (teasing an unknown name buys nothing); the stakes beat moves
to the turn, it does not vanish.
> "So there was this guy, Gregor MacGregor. Yeah, that's his real name."

**2.2 — Select by story value; develop what you keep.** `research.md` is a pool, not a checklist. Cut
the beats that don't carry the story; give the ones that do their real color: the scene, the concrete
detail, the telling number. Three inert research paragraphs collapse to one beat, but a beat that
carries the story earns more than a hurried sentence, and racing through it is as real a miss as
stuffing in facts because they're sourced.
> The beat that carries the story gets the room instead: "They bought 26,000 bricks from the Colorado
> Brick Company, handpicked so that a box with a brick felt and weighed exactly like a box of hard
> drives. Then each box got a serial number, because hard drives have serial numbers, and the whole
> thing was shrink wrapped onto pallets and sent out."

**A fact enters when the story needs it, and not one beat earlier.** Sourced material has no claim on a
position; put each fact where it does work. **Scale is the exception worth planning for: it lands early,
and it lands as a comparison**, because a revenue figure means nothing on its own and a company the
viewer has a feel for means everything. **The detail budget is one number per beat**, rounded the way a
friend rounds ("over half a billion dollars"), with precision kept for where precision is the story
(26,000 bricks); résumé lines, model numbers, and logistics enter only when load-bearing.
> "And at their peak in 1988, they were making over 600 million dollars a year. Accounting for
> inflation, they were making as much money as Reddit. Or so they said."

**2.3 — Let the story set the length inside the 8–10 minute band, natural center near the top of it.**
Word target follows the **channel voice's measured wpm** (in `dna.md`; e.g. Miles runs ~175 gross with
~18% pause), a center of gravity within the band, not a universal constant. That rate is gross, so it
already carries the narrator's pausing: runtime is words divided by it, and `lint_script.py` computes
the number. Landing well under means beats are under-developed (starved of color), not that filler is
missing; running long means dwell (§2.5). Add length only as a genuine cut *thread*, never padding.

**2.4 — Motifs and callbacks carry the telling.** Plant a phrase and reuse it so it pays off ("eight
million acres of land" → "those eight million acres was swampland"). A recurring analogy is a callback
too: the comparison planted in the opening can come back at the payoff with no new setup. **The plant is
invisible.** The line does its whole job the first time and simply comes back later; the moment the
script tells the viewer to hold onto something, the payoff is spent and the machinery is showing. Serial
numbers on boxes of bricks are planted by being *mentioned*, never flagged.

**2.5 — Color, not dwell.** *Color* is a new, concrete, specific detail that makes a beat vivid: the
bricks came from the Colorado Brick Company; each box got its own serial number; the guidebook ran 355
pages. A color detail earns its place by becoming a pull or a joke ("ran it from LA" survives as the
Doctor Strange bit); the same fact told as bare logistics is clutter. *Dwell* is restating a point after
it has landed. **The test: does the line add a new fact or image, or re-emphasize one already said?**
Add the first, cut the second. Lean toward color: the miss we correct most is a draft gone too terse.

**2.6 — Design for retention before drafting.** These are outline decisions, not patches: open on the
intrigue in the very first lines (no logo, no throat-clearing); give a real reason to stay inside the
first ~15 seconds; keep new stimulus arriving beat to beat; on longer stories re-arm the tension around
the middle (a new question, a turn, a second scheme); and hold the best fact for the final stretch.

**2.7 — A company story tells the rise before the fall, and motivates every step out loud.** The
collapse only hurts if the viewer watched the climb first: who they were, how fast they got big, and
how big "big" was, before anything goes wrong.
> "The company was MiniScribe, a hard drive manufacturer out of Colorado, founded in 1980 by a guy
> named Terry Johnson. And they were HOT."

From there, every escalation says out loud what caused it, once, at the beat where it happens, and then
trusts it: the chain is spoken, not implied (pressure produced a hire, the hire produced impossible
targets, the targets produced fear, the fear produced the plan), and re-deriving the stakes at every
later beat is bloat. **The title object keeps returning as itself, escalating** ("These bricks weren't a
one quarter thing. They were the start to a strategy that ran quarter after quarter.").
> "By 1985, MiniScribe was struggling. IBM had slashed its orders, and the competition was closing in.
> So they brought in the man, the myth, the legend: Q.T. Wiles."
>
> "Wiles set impossible sales targets, and missing them meant your head was on the chopping block. So
> the managers put their heads together, and hatched a brilliant plan."

A beat that merely happens next, with no because, is where the viewer quietly stops caring.

## 3. The structure

**3.1 — Impose a shape; refuse flat chronology.** Design the sequence in the outline before drafting —
sequence to land hardest, not by the calendar.

**3.2 — Paradox-hook, then rewind.** Open on the sharpest contradiction as a settled fact with the
mechanism withheld ("one of those companies packaged 26,000... red clay bricks into little boxes
labelled 'hard drive', and sold them to customers around the world"), then reset into the story ("The
company was MiniScribe, a hard drive manufacturer out of Colorado..."). Composes with the person-led
hook (§2.1) and runs straight into the rise beat (§2.7).

**3.3 — Cross-cut, but consolidate — don't ping-pong.** Weave parallel threads, but finish a thread
cleanly before cutting, with a casual signpost ("Meanwhile, back in London…"). One clean cut beats four
choppy ones. For a pre-spoiled ending, the **irony cross-cut** — obliviousness against the gathering
disaster — is the main engine (§3.6).

**3.4 — Casual transitions, question turns, and forward-promise seams.** The question-answered-casually
move ("So how does a warehouse full of bricks pass an audit? Well…") is the spoken connective tissue.
What is dead is the literary connector ("which brings us to," "little did they know") and the announced
category ("here's the strange part," "the uncomfortable question"): **deliver the moment, don't announce
its category**. A plain spoken doorway ("Here is the story of that company.") is normal speech. A beat
exits on an open loop the viewer stays to collect, and escalation is honest ("it gets worse" is followed
by something worse).

**3.5 — The ending is a tone, not a formula.** Stop the way a friend stops telling a story: casual,
brisk, unceremonious, usually landing an ironic observation or one last laugh. Settle what happened to
everybody in a line and get out.
> "In the end, MiniScribe went bankrupt in 1990, their investors were screwed, and Q.T. Wiles lost his
> Fix It reputation. The big winner was probably the Colorado Brick Company, who scored a major payday
> selling bricks to a tech company."

That is one ending that works; another stops on a fate, a joke, or a plain last fact, and a second
approved shape is the counterfactual last laugh, wondering out loud how long it would have run if one
small thing had gone differently. What they share is that nobody is being taught anything. Banned: the
essay conclusion ("here's what it means"), the moral, and the crafted profundity written to sound
resonant ("That's a workday"), because a line reaching for meaning drains everything the story just
earned. The insight is woven into the body.

**3.6 — Pre-spoiled tension.** Our titles usually give away the ending, so carry tension on something
other than the outcome: dramatic irony, comic dread (make them wait for the doom you can both see
coming), and how-badly / how-exactly assembly.

**3.7 — Non-linear, but followable.** Jumping around is the craft; the bar is that a first-time viewer
can follow the causal through-line. Introduce a person, place, or company before the story leans on it;
establish a connection before a beat depends on it. The line is *withheld* versus *unestablished*: a
question the story raises and answers later is suspense; a connection used as if already understood is a
hole. Structural confusion gets a structural fix, resequencing, never an explaining sentence pasted on.

## 4. Staging & facts

- **Casual is not loose with facts.** The friendly voice stays leashed to `research.md`. Correct what's
  wrong, and never invent to fill a hole. **The color license:** a witnessed scene or behavior carried
  by one credible source is tellable flat, the ledger marking it reported while the narration never
  hedges it; numbers and load-bearing plot facts keep the strict leash.
- **Hedging happens in fact selection, never in the narration.** Deciding which version of a contested
  fact is solid enough to tell is the writer's job, done before the line exists. A claim either goes in
  as the strongest version the ledger supports, stated flat, or it does not go in. "By one account,"
  "sources disagree," "reportedly," "the record doesn't support" and every cousin of theirs are banned
  from the voiceover: the viewer hears a narrator apologising for his own story, and the beat dies. **A
  myth is busted by telling the documented version flat**, never by staging the correction: "the version
  of this story you usually hear" is dead with them.
- **A genuine gap in the record gets transparent speculation, in the narrator's voice.** When nobody
  knows why something happened and the story needs a why, say plainly that nobody knows, float the live
  possibilities as guesses, and move on inside the same breath.
  > "Don't ask me why. Maybe Wiles was just that charismatic, or maybe there was some under-the-table
  > dealing going on. Either way, the same twenty six thousand bricks..."

  The viewer can hear that this is a guess, which is the point: nothing is passed off as fact, and
  nobody sits through a confession about sourcing. It is the only sanctioned way a research hole reaches
  the audio.
- **Reported speech is the default telling mode.** Dramatized beats land as the narrator
  *characterizing* what was said, with attitude; a paraphrase never adds a detail no source supports.
- **Defamation discipline:** present documented facts and let the conclusion be the viewer's inference,
  never the channel's accusation. Dramatize the *act of checking*, and land the payoff on the *system*.
- **Staging craft:** open the villain with documented motive so the con reads as one person's obsession,
  and use the analogy-that-indicts (explains the mechanism *and* exposes its flaw).

## 5. The before → after bank

The taste defects the pipeline reliably produces, as transformations (what critics scan for):

| The recurring AI-tell | The fix |
| --- | --- |
| Clipped one-liner monotone: sentence after sentence as its own paragraph | Idea blocks of four to five sentences; the short line lands inside one (§1.1) |
| Concept-prose ("make the world's version match his target"), an unparseable line, or a present-tense still life | Say who did what, to what, and what came of it, in past tense, with people in the sentence (§1.1) |
| Audible hedge ("by one account," "sources disagree") | State the strongest sourced version flat, or cut the claim (§4) |
| Historiography framing ("the version of this story you usually hear") | Tell the documented version flat; never stage the correction (§4) |
| Visible payoff-plant ("hold onto that, it matters later") | Mention the fact where it does work and let the callback land cold (§2.4) |
| Literary/essayist phrasing, the uncontracted punch ("That is one year."), the paired parallel aphorism | Plain and short, the way it would be said aloud; heat lives in capitals and delivery (§1.6) |
| Grandeur button ending a beat, or an essay/moral close ("That's a workday") | End on the fact/action; stop the way a friend stops: the fates, then a last laugh (§1.2, §3.5) |
| Dwell: restating a landed point, re-restating the premise, or a triple-build of one idea | Say it once, on the sharpest image, then move (§2.5) |
| Drawn-out generic metaphor where a named pull would land | One named cultural reference, applied in a line (§1.4) |
| Viewer-staging ("put yourself in that room. You are a middle manager") | Talk to the viewer, never cast them (§1.5) |
| Number pile-up: several figures in one beat, unrounded | One number per beat, rounded the way a friend rounds (§2.2) |
| Outline-then-retell fluff ("he was not some nobody"), literary connector, or announced category | Cut it and open on the content itself; a casual signpost or a question turn (§3.4) |
| Cramming every sourced fact in | Select and compress to one vivid beat (§2.2) |
| Too terse — compressing away the vivid detail | Keep the specific color (§2.5) |
| Credibility padding ("he actually did") | State the fact directly (§1.6) |

## 6. The joke toolbox (reach for these; never force them)

All fact-riding, and used **often** (§1.3 — abundance is the default, flatness is the defect):

- **Anachronistic analogy that teaches the mechanism** (the workhorse): "the Bernie Madoff of the
  1820s, except Madoff sold securities and MacGregor sold a fake country." The same move closes a beat
  as a kicker: "This guy might even put Jordan Belfort to shame."
- **Deadpan undercut / bathos:** "And what does he get in return for all his efforts? 250 customers."
- **Ironic re-label:** "he's turned their savings into his homemade magic money."
- **Comic false precision** (exact numbers for deadpan authority): "a chunk of land the size of
  Maryland for a bar tab and a necklace."
- **Deflate-the-powerful** (the mastermind reduced to an ordinary man): "It's not even that MacGregor
  was Megamind, you know. He wasn't some super intelligent con man; people just didn't care."
- **Viewer-solidarity aside** (the narrator admits they'd fall for it too): "I'd move to a country with
  golden rivers too, you know."
- **Playing out the mark's inner monologue:** "You see that many pages and you skim through and believe
  the pretty pictures like 'wow, that looks so real. Somebody must know what they're talking about.'"
- **Irreverent reaction** (says out loud what the viewer is thinking): "The balls on this guy, huh?"
- **Franchise pull that carries the fact:** "like Doctor Strange with his many sanctums, Doctor Fix It
  ran several other companies at the same time." Setup-then-apply is the larger version: hand the viewer
  the reference first, then apply it ("You know that Gordon Ramsay show where he goes around reinventing
  failing restaurants? Q.T. Wiles was the Gordon Ramsay of the business world.").
- **The knowing build-up before the fall:** "they brought in the man, the myth, the legend: Q.T. Wiles."
- **Scale by modern comparison:** "Accounting for inflation, they were making as much money as Reddit."
- **Narrator at his own expense:** "My Peloton bike is a scam but these guys? Next level."
- **Genre re-label** (name the movie the story has turned into): "This was Ocean's Eleven level stuff."
- **Historical irony where the real fact is the punchline:** the bricks had serial numbers.

**The humor bar:** a joke ships only if it clears the universality bar (§1.4) and the words are plain.
It also has to actually land: warm or dry, a swing that misses costs energy and returns nothing, so cut
it rather than keep it for texture. Punch up at power and absurdity, never at the mark or the viewer.
Profanity rides along at the "shit" grade and no further.
