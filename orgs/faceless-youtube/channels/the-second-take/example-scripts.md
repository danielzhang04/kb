# The Second Take: approved script excerpts

**What this is:** the channel's **voice bar**. Every passage below is script-level prose Daniel has
approved as hitting the target energy. Writers match the energy, never the content: nothing here is a
rewrite order, and no line is meant to be lifted into a new script. Critics and judges judge a draft
against these, and the storytelling grammar draws its inline examples only from them. The file grows;
each excerpt Daniel approves lands here with a short note on what it demonstrates. Openings, mid-story
beats, and endings all live here, so the bar covers the whole arc of a script and not just its first
minute.

Raw and a little messy is preferable to polished and bland. Preserve contractions, direct questions, slang,
repeated conversational joints, and a run of useful comparisons. Keep every claim within the fact leash.

Every excerpt is what the narrator says and nothing else, because `script.md` is pure voiceover prose.

## MacGregor / the Poyais pitch

> So there was this guy, Gregor MacGregor. Yeah, that's his real name. MacGregor was like the Bernie Madoff of the 1820s, except Madoff sold securities and MacGregor sold a fake country.
>
> So the year's 1821 and 34-year-old MacGregor had just come back to Britain from fighting in South America as a general. This guy was like Scottish George Washington before he turned to the dark side.
>
> While he was out there, MacGregor cut an insane land deal with a local king. All he had to do was fork over some rum and jewellery in exchange for eight million acres of land. That's a chunk of land the size of Maryland for a bar tab and a necklace. It was the louisiana purchase on crack. 

> MacGregor had just one small problem... those eight million acres was swampland. Uninhabitable and unproductive. So he thinks, and schemes, and finally, he hatches a plot.
>
> **Step 1: Create the Fake Country.**
>
> So MacGregor decides to invent a country. The balls on this guy, huh? He calls this beautiful new country of his Poyais, and he crowns himself the Prince of Poyias. He calls upon a Captain of his, Captain Thomas Strangeways, to author a gorgeous 355-page book: the Guidebook to Poyais. It was like Harry Potter in real life.

> According to the book, there's a magical city across the Atlantic with beautiful palaces, twenty thousand people, and rivers overflowing with gold. Strangeways, like Poyais, was also fake; invented by MacGregor to add credibility to his story.

> And the guidebook was 355 pages long. It's like reading a textbook, you know? You see that many pages and you just skim through and believe the pretty pictures like "wow, that looks so real. Somebody must know what they're talking about." And people bought it.
>
> **Step 2: Print Its Money.**
>
> It's not even that MacGregor was Megamind, you know. He wasn't some super intelligent con man; people just didn't care. Spanish rule had just broken in South America, and hot new countries were popping up left and right. Peru. Chile. Argentina. It was the dot-com bubble of the 1820's. The hottest new thing sold, and boy was MacGregor hot. I'd move to a country with golden rivers too, you know.

> MacGregor, seeing that people believed him, then convinces the Bank of Scotland's official printer to make him 5,000 custom Poyais dollar notes. And what does he get in return for all his efforts? 250 customers. MacGregor convinced 250 people to sail for Poyais.

> Before they leave, MacGregor also has them swap out their gold and savings for his Poyais dollar notes. After all, who would move to a new country without local currency? Before the ships have even left Britain, he's turned their savings into his homemade magic money. This guy might even put Jordan Belfort to shame.

**What this demonstrates.** A narrator who is inside the telling and reacting to it as he goes ("The
balls on this guy, huh?"), with analogy as the explanatory engine rather than decoration: several
comparisons deep, each one a modern figure or moment the viewer already holds. The irreverence bar
sits where the narrator mocks the scheme, the era, and the man, never the marks. The universality bar
sits where every pull (Madoff, Megamind, Harry Potter, the dot-com bubble) is something a general
viewer pictures instantly.

## Bricks / the era drop-in intro

> We're in the 1980s. Home to big hair, Pac-Man, and one of the funniest corporate scams that you've never heard of.
>
> It's 1983, and personal computers had just hit the market a few years earlier. They were all the rage. Anybody who had money was buying themselves a computer. It was like when Apple released the iPhone.
>
> Well, computers run on these things called hard drives, which are basically where your computer keeps everything it remembers. Your files, your photos, your games: all of it lives on the hard drive. So while the computer companies fought for the spotlight, the hard drive guys quietly made a killing. They were the people selling picks and shovels in the gold rush.
>
> And one of those companies packaged 26,000... well, the title gives it away... red clay bricks into little boxes labelled "hard drive", and sold them to customers around the world. Here is the story of that company.
>
> MiniScribe was founded in...

**What this demonstrates.** The era drop-in hook: set the viewer down in a period on anchors everyone
already pictures, then say plainly what kind of story is coming. The everyman gloss explains a hard
drive in the same breath it names one, pitched to someone who has never thought about it. The aside
about the title leans into an ending the packaging already gave away, so the narrator and the viewer
are in on it together, and the doorway line simply opens the story without announcing a transition.

## Bricks / the company's rise

> The company was MiniScribe, a hard drive manufacturer out of Colorado, founded in 1980 by a guy named Terry Johnson. And they were HOT. By 1984 they were pulling in 125 million dollars a year selling hard drives to IBM. And at their peak in 1988, they were selling to giants like Compaq and making over 600 million dollars a year. Accounting for inflation, that's almost as much money as Reddit makes today. Or so they said.

**What this demonstrates.** The rise told before the fall, inside one idea block: who they were, how
fast they got big, and how big "big" actually was. Scale lands as a comparison to a company the viewer
already has a feel for, never as a bare figure. The short sentences live inside the block and get their
punch from the long ones around them, and the capitals carry the heat the way a voice would. The last
four words turn the whole rise ironic without announcing that they are doing it.

## Bricks / meet the fixer

> By 1985, MiniScribe was struggling. IBM had slashed its orders, and the competition was closing in. So they brought in the man, the myth, the legend: Q.T. Wiles. Q.T. Wiles was an investment banker nicknamed "Doctor Fix It". He was the mechanic for dying companies, and he built a reputation off of turning dying companies back into successful ones. And, like Doctor Strange with his many sanctums, Doctor Fix It ran several other companies at the same time.

**What this demonstrates.** A character introduced together with the cause that produced him, so the
escalation is motivated out loud: competition squeezed them, so they went and got the fixer. The
knowing stock phrase is exactly how a friend builds somebody up before knocking them down. The
franchise pull does the explaining rather than decorating it, turning one man running several companies
at once into a picture the viewer already holds.

## Bricks / the caper

> Wiles set impossible sales targets, and missing them meant your head was on the chopping block. So the managers put their heads together, and hatched a brilliant plan. They rented a warehouse, and went shopping. They bought 26,000 bricks from the Colorado Brick Company, handpicked so that a box with a brick felt and weighed exactly like a box of hard drives. Then each box got a serial number, because hard drives have serial numbers, and the whole thing was shrink wrapped onto pallets and sent out. These guys even put defective drives, scraps, and random shit lying around in too. My Peloton bike is a scam but these guys? Next level. This was Ocean's Eleven level stuff.

**What this demonstrates.** A whole caper carried in one block, with every step chained to the one
before it: the targets made the fear, the fear made the plan, the plan needed a warehouse, the bricks
needed serial numbers because real drives have them. Facts arrive at the moment the story needs them
and nothing is planted for later. The register runs warm and loud at the end, profanity included, and
the heist comparison tells the viewer how to feel about what was just described.

## Bricks / speaking to a gap in the record

> Don't ask me why. Maybe Wiles was just that charismatic, or maybe there was some under-the-table dealing going on. Either way, the same twenty six thousand bricks...

**What this demonstrates.** What a hole in the research sounds like when it stays inside the narrator's
voice. He says out loud that nobody knows, floats the two live possibilities as guesses the viewer can
hear are guesses, and is moving again by the end of the third sentence. No apology, no discussion of
sources, no stall in the story.

## Bricks / the ending

> In the end, MiniScribe went bankrupt in 1990, their investors were screwed, and Q.T. Wiles lost his Fix It reputation. The big winner was probably the Colorado Brick Company, who scored a major payday selling bricks to a tech company.

**What this demonstrates.** How a friend stops telling a story: everybody's fate in one quick line,
then a last laugh at the one party nobody was watching. It settles rather than concludes. Nothing gets
drawn out into a meaning, because the irony is already sitting in the fact. This is one ending that
works, not the shape every ending takes.
