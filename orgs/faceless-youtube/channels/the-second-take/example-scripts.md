# The Second Take: approved script excerpts

**What this is:** the channel's **voice bar**. Every passage below is script-level prose Daniel has
approved as hitting the target energy. It is a **register**: writers absorb it before drafting, for
language, tone, joke grade, pull species, and density. It is neither a quarry to quote nor a minefield
to avoid; critics judge a draft's paragraphs against it, never their ancestry. The storytelling grammar
draws nearly all of its inline examples from these excerpts. The file grows; each excerpt Daniel approves lands
here with a short note on what it demonstrates. Openings, mid-story beats, and endings all live here,
so the bar covers the whole arc of a script and not just its first minute.

Raw and a little messy is preferable to polished and bland. Preserve contractions, direct questions, slang,
repeated conversational joints, and a run of useful comparisons. Keep every claim within the fact leash.

Every excerpt is what the narrator says and nothing else, because `script.md` is pure voiceover prose.

## MacGregor / the Poyais pitch

> So there was this guy, Gregor MacGregor. Yeah, that's his real name. MacGregor was like the Bernie Madoff of the 1820s, except Madoff sold securities and MacGregor sold a fake country.
>
> So the year's 1821 and 34-year-old MacGregor had just come back to Britain from fighting in South America as a general. This guy was like Scottish George Washington before he turned to the dark side.
>
> While he was out there, MacGregor cut an insane land deal with a local king. All he had to do was fork over some rum and jewellery in exchange for eight million acres of land. That's a chunk of land the size of Maryland for a bar tab and a necklace. It was the louisiana purchase on crack. 

> MacGregor had just one small problem... those eight million acres was swampland. Uninhabitable and unproductive. So he thinks, and schemes, and finally, he hatches a plot.
>
> **Step 1: Create the Fake Country.**
>
> So MacGregor decides to invent a country. The balls on this guy, huh? He calls this beautiful new country of his Poyais, and he crowns himself the Prince of Poyias. He calls upon a Captain of his, Captain Thomas Strangeways, to author a gorgeous 355-page book: the Guidebook to Poyais. It was like Harry Potter in real life.

> According to the book, there's a magical city across the Atlantic with beautiful palaces, twenty thousand people, and rivers overflowing with gold. Strangeways, like Poyais, was also fake; invented by MacGregor to add credibility to his story.

> And the guidebook was 355 pages long. It's like reading a textbook, you know? You see that many pages and you just skim through and believe the pretty pictures like "wow, that looks so real. Somebody must know what they're talking about." And people bought it.
>
> **Step 2: Print Its Money.**
>
> It's not even that MacGregor was Megamind, you know. He wasn't some super intelligent con man; people just didn't care. Spanish rule had just broken in South America, and hot new countries were popping up left and right. Peru. Chile. Argentina. It was the dot-com bubble of the 1820's. The hottest new thing sold, and boy was MacGregor hot. I'd move to a country with golden rivers too, you know.

> MacGregor, seeing that people believed him, then convinces the Bank of Scotland's official printer to make him 5,000 custom Poyais dollar notes. And what does he get in return for all his efforts? 250 customers. MacGregor convinced 250 people to sail for Poyais.

> Before they leave, MacGregor also has them swap out their gold and savings for his Poyais dollar notes. After all, who would move to a new country without local currency? Before the ships have even left Britain, he's turned their savings into his homemade magic money. This guy might even put Jordan Belfort to shame.

**What this demonstrates.** A narrator who is inside the telling and reacting to it as he goes ("The
balls on this guy, huh?"), with analogy as the explanatory engine rather than decoration: several
comparisons deep, each one a modern figure or moment the viewer already holds. The irreverence bar
sits where the narrator mocks the scheme, the era, and the man, never the marks. The universality bar
sits where every pull (Madoff, Megamind, Harry Potter, the dot-com bubble) is something a general
viewer pictures instantly.

## Bricks / the era drop-in intro

> We're in the 1980s. Home to big hair, Pac-Man, and one of the funniest corporate scams that you've never heard of.
>
> It's 1983, and the personal computer had only been invented a few years earlier. They were all the craze. Anybody with money was buying one, and they were flying off the shelves. It was like when Apple released the iPhone.
>
> Well, computers run on these things called hard drives. A hard drive is the part of your computer that remembers things after you switch it off: your files, your applications, and your private collections. Every computer needs one. So while the computer companies were out front fighting like Samsung and Apple fight over the phone market, the hard drive manufacturers were quietly raking it in. They were the people selling picks and shovels in the gold rush.
>
> And one of those companies packed 26,000... well, the title gives it away... red clay bricks into little boxes labelled hard drive, and sold them to customers around the world. Here is the story of that company.

**What this demonstrates.** The era drop-in hook: anchors everyone already pictures, then a plain word
about what kind of story is coming. The everyman gloss explains a hard drive in the same breath it names
one and winks at what else lives on there ("private collections"), while the pull does the rest of the
explaining: who was quietly getting rich arrives as Samsung against Apple. The title aside puts narrator
and viewer in on it together, and the doorway line simply opens the story.

## Bricks / the company's rise, then the rewind

> The company was MiniScribe, a hard drive manufacturer out of Colorado, founded in 1980 by a guy named Terry Johnson. And they were HOT. IBM picked MiniScribe to supply hard drives for their PCs, and within four years the company was making 125 million dollars a year. And at their peak in 1988, they were selling to giants like Compaq and making over 600 million dollars a year. Accounting for inflation, that's almost as much money as Reddit makes today. Or so they said.

> Now rewind four years. At the end of 1984, IBM slashed its orders, and MiniScribe fell off a cliff. They let a quarter of their people go, and Terry Johnson was out the door.

**What this demonstrates.** The peak-first rewind, the shape a company rise-and-fall gets instead of the
calendar. The whole climb is told once, in one idea block, all the way to its peak: who they were, how
fast they got big, and how big "big" actually was, with scale arriving as a comparison to a company the
viewer already has a feel for and four ironic words turning the rise over without announcing it. Then
the story rewinds to the cause of the fall in three sentences of plain stock idiom. Because the climb
was spent at its peak, nothing later needs to re-climb it.

## Bricks / meet the fixer

> By 1985 the company was in real trouble, so their bankers at Hambrecht & Quist put about 20 million dollars in and sent their own turnaround guy to run it. They brought in the man, the myth, the legend: Q.T. Wiles. You know that Gordon Ramsay show where he goes around reinventing failing restaurants? Wiles was the Gordon Ramsay of the business world, an investment banker they called Doctor Fix It. Instead of shitting on your risotto, he's reading your quarterly statements. And, like Doctor Strange with his many sanctums, Doctor Fix It ran several other companies at the same time, this one from an office out in Los Angeles. Full remote, forty years early.

**What this demonstrates.** Setup then apply: hand the viewer the reference first, then drop the man
into it, so the pull carries the job description instead of decorating it. The arrival is motivated out
loud, the bankers were in trouble so they sent a fixer, and the knowing stock phrase is how a friend
builds somebody up before knocking him down. The risotto line keeps the block running warm, and one
sourced logistical detail (the LA office) earns its place by turning into the closing joke.

## Bricks / the fear regime

> Wiles ran MiniScribe with fear. He set sales targets. Hit them, and you got a fat bonus. Miss them, and your head was on the chopping block. And when you hit them, he raised them, so the next quarter was even harder. He once made two managers stand up in the middle of a team meeting and fired them on the spot, just to show everyone who was in control. What a dick.

**What this demonstrates.** A regime told in the idioms a friend actually reaches for (a fat bonus, your
head on the chopping block), then one witnessed scene doing the work that abstraction would have done
badly, capped by the narrator's honest three-word reaction. The scene rests on a single source and is
told flat anyway, no hedge, because it is color and not a number.

## Bricks / the pressure and the corner

> Now, the targets Wiles handed out didn't care much what the company could actually build and sell. So four times a year, a room full of middle managers in Colorado sat there needing a number they didn't have, and wrote one down anyway. Then in January 1987, MiniScribe's outside accountants came in to audit the books, which means somebody counts what your books say is sitting in the warehouse. So the accountants set up in the building, everybody started counting, and all those numbers the managers had been writing down finally got held up against what was actually on the shelves. Four million dollars of product that was supposed to be sitting there, wasn't. Awkward.

**What this demonstrates.** The two staged beats that have to land before any scheme exists: the
pressure (a number nobody can hit, four times a year) and the corner (the gap, found). The audit gets a
gloss in the same breath it is named, and the gloss says only what an audit IS, not how the counting
works, so the mechanism is still unspent and available later as the punchline. The block ends on the
fact plus a one-word reaction, never a summary.

## Bricks / the decision and the act

> And coming up short like that meant somebody was getting fired, so instead of reporting it, they wrote down a better number. The real sheets were locked in the accountants' own boxes, so they popped the boxes open with Allen wrenches and paper clips, took the real count out and put the fake one in, and blamed the whole gap on a typo. The audit came back clean. Not exactly Ocean's Eleven level, but pretty close.

**What this demonstrates.** The decision arrives motivated out loud (coming up short meant somebody was
getting fired, SO they wrote a better number), and the act runs on chained verbs: popped, took, put,
blamed. The one detail that survives is the funny one, Allen wrenches and paper clips, and the named
pull closes the block while planting the Ocean's Eleven callback the next caper cashes in.

## Bricks / the caper

> So the managers put their heads together and came up with a brilliant plan. They rented a warehouse near headquarters, and went shopping at a local company: the Colorado Brick Company. They bought 26,000 bricks, handpicked to match the weight and size of a real boxed hard drive. They put the bricks in boxes, gave each one a serial number, because hard drives have serial numbers, and shrink wrapped them onto pallets, premium Colorado clay sitting on the books as inventory. Perfectly indistinguishable. My Peloton bike is a scam, but these guys? Next level. This was Ocean's Eleven level stuff.

**What this demonstrates.** A whole caper carried on chained verbs: rented, went shopping, bought,
boxed, numbered, wrapped. The beat that carries the story gets the room, so the serial numbers arrive
with the reason they are funny attached. The two-word verdict lands the absurdity faster than a sentence
could, and the two pulls tell the viewer how to feel about what was just described.

## Bricks / speaking to a gap in the record

> Don't ask me why. Maybe Wiles was just that charismatic, or maybe there was some under-the-table dealing going on. Either way, the same twenty six thousand bricks...

> Either Wiles was one charismatic guy, or there was money changing hands under the table. Either way, the same bricks counted as a sale in one quarter, came home, and got counted as inventory in the next.

**What this demonstrates.** What a hole in the research sounds like when it stays inside the narrator's
voice. He says out loud that nobody knows, floats the two live possibilities as guesses the viewer can
hear are guesses, and is moving again by the end of the third sentence. No apology, no discussion of
sources, no stall in the story. The second is the accepted script's tighter run at the same beat: the
admission compresses into the shape of the guess itself, and the guess reaches only for a motive, never
for a scene nobody recorded.

## Bricks / how it passed the audit

> So how does a warehouse full of bricks pass an audit? Well, MiniScribe's outside accounting firm was Coopers & Lybrand, one of the giants, and nobody there is opening all those boxes. What they do instead is called a test count. They pull a sample, count it, and if the sample matches the paperwork, everything else in the building gets a pass. It's the TSA. And a box with a brick in it weighs exactly what a box with a hard drive in it weighs.

**What this demonstrates.** The mechanism arriving AFTER the act, as the answer to the question the
viewer is already holding. The question turn is asked once the bricks are on the pallets and never
before them; the named institutional pull carries the entire explanation in three words; and the
punchline is a plain physical fact rather than a joke bolted onto one.

## Bricks / it kept going

> And this wasn't a one time thing. Each quarter, Wiles raised sales targets and management came up with better ways to meet them. They started padding their inventory with broken drives and factory scrap, and whatever else was lying around, and the junk padding grew to more than twice what the bricks were worth. One of the executives even brought his own family in at night to help pack the boxes. The original bring your kid to work. And in 1988, right in the middle of all this, MiniScribe was named the best managed company in the disk drive industry. The irony.

**What this demonstrates.** The namesake coming back as itself and escalating: the bricks stop being an
incident and become a strategy. The ratchet is restated as a cause (targets up, so the methods got
worse) rather than re-climbing the revenue, and the fraud widens past its own icon with two short
cappers riding real facts.

## Bricks / the ending

> In the end MiniScribe was gone, a rival drive maker called Maxtor bought what was left, Wiles lost his Doctor Fix It title forever and was sentenced to three years in prison, and the investors collected about a quarter of what a jury had promised them. The big winner was probably the Colorado Brick Company, who scored a major payday selling bricks to a tech company.

> And the scheme itself was never caught. It beat the audit, it beat the count sheets. What it didn't survive was HR. If MiniScribe had just kept a few more people on the payroll through Christmas, who knows how many more years they would've gone on selling those bricks.

**What this demonstrates.** How a friend stops telling a story: everybody's fate in one quick line,
then a last laugh at the one party nobody was watching. The brick company is the payoff of a plant laid
back at the caper, mentioned plainly there and never flagged, which is how a minor character earns the
final joke. Nothing gets drawn out into a meaning, because the irony is already sitting in the fact.
The second is a different approved shape, the counterfactual last laugh, running the story one step
past its end into what almost happened. Both settle rather than conclude, and neither is the shape
every ending takes.
