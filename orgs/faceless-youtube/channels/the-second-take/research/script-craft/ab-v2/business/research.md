## Fact ledger  ← the scriptwriter may state ONLY what appears here

- **[F-01]** Ty Warner launched Beanie Babies in 1993 (first nine designs, led by "Legs the Frog"), priced
  at about $5 retail. — *Src:* [S3][S4], 1993 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the ~$5 price is the anchor for every later contrast.
- **[F-02]** The toys were deliberately *under-stuffed* with PVC pellets ("beans"), making them soft and
  posable — a choice that also kept them cheap to make. — *Src:* [S1][S3] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-03]** Beanie Babies were manufactured overseas (South Korea early, then China) at a small fraction of
  retail — commonly described as cents per unit. — *Src:* [S1][S3] · *Conf:* low · *Note:* the "≈25–65¢ each"
  figure is widely repeated but not tied to a primary; treat as "pennies-to-about-a-dollar," not a hard number.
- **[F-04]** Warner's core scarcity tactic — "retiring" individual Beanie Babies to manufacture the illusion
  of limited supply — began around 1995. — *Src:* [S2][S5] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-05]** A 1996 Forbes article described the strategy explicitly: "the strategy here is empty shelves —
  the deliberate creation of scarcity, which pumps up word-of-mouth demand to a frenzied level." — *Src:* [S2]
  (Forbes, 1996, quoted) · *Conf:* high · *Note:* on-record contemporaneous primary framing; strong vindication payload.
- **[F-06]** Ty refused to sell to big-box chains, distributing only through independent gift/specialty
  stores — so no single store had many, making them *feel* rare regardless of true volume. — *Src:* [S2][S5] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-07]** Ty limited retailers to 36 of any one Beanie Baby per order. — *Src:* [S2][S5] · *Conf:* med ·
  *Note:* "36 per order" recurs across secondary sources; not traced to a Ty document.
- **[F-08]** A collector-run secondary-market price guide assigned dollar "values" to each Beanie Baby —
  numbers based on guesswork, not underlying worth — and the resale market treated those figures as real
  prices, reframing a toy as an "investment." — *Src:* [S3][S5] · *Conf:* med.
- **[F-09]** On eBay, Beanie Babies resold for an average of about $30 — roughly six times the ~$5 retail —
  and were among the site's most-traded items in the late 1990s. — *Src:* [S3][S4] · *Conf:* med · *Note:*
  the "~10% of all eBay sales" share is repeated widely but not sourced to eBay/primary data; use the $30-avg framing.
- **[F-10]** Rare Beanies commanded extreme resale prices at the peak — sought-after items reportedly
  changing hands for $5,000+ apiece. — *Src:* [S3][S4] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* thin-market prices; frame as "sold for," not "worth."
- **[F-11]** Ty Inc. reported about $280 million in revenue in 1996. — *Src:* [S5][S6] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-12]** Ty Inc. revenue was roughly $400 million in 1997 and surpassed ~$1.4 billion in 1998. — *Src:*
  [S5][S6] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* official Ty sales, excluding all secondary-market resale.
- **[F-13]** In spring 1997 Ty partnered with McDonald's on "Teenie Beanies" in Happy Meals; the promotion
  distributed on the order of 100 million units in ~10 days and triggered nationwide shortages. — *Src:* [S7][S8] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-14]** The mania produced real crime — thefts, store incidents, hijacked shipments, and arrests tied to
  Beanie Babies and Teenie Beanies. — *Src:* [S8][S9] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-15]** The value proposition was structurally bubble/Ponzi-adjacent: an existing collection only rose in
  value while *new* buyers kept entering, so holders were incentivized to recruit more buyers — the toy itself
  produced no income. — *Src:* [S3][S5] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* frame as mechanism/analysis, not a legal "Ponzi scheme" accusation.
- **[F-16]** In September 1999 Ty appended a notice to a press release: "On December 31, 1999-11:59 p.m. (CST)
  All Beanies will be retired," announcing the entire line would end. — *Src:* [S10][S11] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-17]** Ty then staged a public vote on whether Beanie Babies should continue; voting cost 50¢, proceeds
  went to the Elizabeth Glaser Pediatric AIDS Foundation, and about 90% voted to continue — after which Ty
  released a new line ("The Beginning" bear) in 2000. — *Src:* [S10][S11] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* widely read
  as a manufactured-scarcity stunt, not a real shutdown.
- **[F-18]** By 1999 the market was oversupplied — Ty had produced Beanies in enormous volume and demand had
  stopped growing — and prices began falling as new buyers dried up. — *Src:* [S5][S9] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-19]** In the collapse, most Beanie Babies fell to roughly 1% of their original resale value; bulk lots
  went for ~20–40¢ per toy, and most are worth only ~$5–$20 today. — *Src:* [S3][S12] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-20]** Even the most-hyped item — the Princess Diana memorial bear — is typically worth far less than
  collectors paid: it retailed ~$5–$7, spiked to ~$350–$400 at kiosks in Dec 1997, and today most versions
  sell for ~$5–$100. — *Src:* [S12][S13] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the "$25,000 Princess bear" is a rare-variant outlier, not the norm.
- **[F-21]** Ty Warner became a billionaire from Ty Inc. and remained one long after the bubble. — *Src:* [S1][S4] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-22]** On/around Jan 31, 1996, Warner flew to Zurich and deposited about $80 million at UBS AG,
  instructing that no account statements be sent to him in the U.S., and kept the account secret until 2007.
  — *Src:* [S14] (DOJ release, 2013, quoted) · *Conf:* high · *Note:* stated as what the DOJ charge/plea alleges.
- **[F-23]** Warner failed to report at least $24.4 million in interest income on the offshore account and
  evaded at least $5.6 million in U.S. taxes; the balance reached ~$93.6 million when he moved it to Zürcher
  Kantonalbank in late 2002. — *Src:* [S14] (DOJ, 2013) · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-24]** Warner pleaded guilty to one count of tax evasion in October 2013, agreeing to pay ~$16 million
  in back taxes/interest and a $53.5 million civil penalty. — *Src:* [S14][S15] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-25]** He was sentenced to two years' probation, community service, and ~$100,000 in fines/costs — no
  prison — against an advisory guideline range of 46–57 months; the 7th Circuit upheld the no-jail sentence in
  2015. — *Src:* [S15] (7th Cir., 2015)[S16] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* judge cited Warner's charitable giving;
  critics noted it was a small share of his net worth — attribute as commentary, not fact.
- **[F-26]** Warner was, per those who worked with him, an obsessive perfectionist who personally trimmed and
  brushed the plush and dictated store lighting and shelf arrangement — hand-finishing details long after toys
  shipped. — *Src:* [S17] (Bissonnette, 2015; Vice/Kirkus summaries quoting it) · *Conf:* med-high · *Note:*
  reported through a journalist's interviews with associates; attribute to the book, not asserted independently.
- **[F-27]** Associates told Bissonnette that what Forbes framed in 1999 as deliberate "mystique marketing" was
  in their view not a strategy but the behaviour of "an obsessive compulsive guy" — i.e., the control looked
  like genius from outside and compulsion from inside. — *Src:* [S17] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* characterization
  from named-book reporting; frame as what associates said, not a clinical claim.
- **[F-28]** Lina Trivedi, an early Ty employee, wrote the first 136 Beanie Baby poems and built Ty's early
  consumer website; she left the company in 1998 feeling uncredited, and in a 2023 interview Warner minimized
  her authorship ("wrote many poems, but so did several other employees"). — *Src:* [S18] (Wikipedia; Yahoo/NY
  Post interview, 2023) · *Conf:* med-high · *Note:* the credit-erasure is the on-record control tic; the "punch
  to the gut" reaction is Trivedi's own words.
- **[F-29]** On Nov 5, 1999, Clark County (Las Vegas) Family Court Judge Gerald Hardcastle ordered divorcing
  couple Frances and Harold Mountain to divide their Beanie Baby collection (valued ~$2,500–$5,000) by picking
  toys one at a time on the courtroom floor under his supervision; "Maple the Bear" was chosen first. — *Src:*
  [S19] (Las Vegas Sun, Nov 5 1999; Reuters photo by Aaron Mayes) · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-30]** In October 1999, Jeffrey White (29) shot and killed Harry Simmons (63), a security guard, at a
  lumberyard in Elkins, West Virginia; police cited a job-loss grievance, and reporting noted the two had also
  disputed over several hundred dollars' worth of Beanie Babies Simmons had lent White to start a trading
  business. — *Src:* [S20] (Baltimore Sun, 2004) · *Conf:* med · *Note:* Beanie Babies were *a* factor in a
  documented killing, not the sole motive — phrase exactly that way; defamation-safe (White confessed, per report).
- **[F-31]** Beanie Baby counterfeiting grew large enough to draw the U.S. Postal Inspection Service and produce
  several federal indictments. — *Src:* [S20] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* no individual named in the source; keep aggregate.

## Cast, motive & human-cost  ← the story fuel a fact-ledger prunes; REQUIRED, all sourced

- **Villain / protagonist — Ty Warner:** a reclusive, controlling perfectionist who personally hand-brushed the
  plush and micromanaged store lighting and shelving [F-26]; associates said the celebrated "mystique marketing"
  was, up close, obsessive-compulsion rather than a plan [F-27]. The through-line the writer can open a character
  beat on: **the same compulsion that made the toy feel special (obsessive control of every detail) is the
  compulsion that manufactured its fake scarcity** — he wasn't playing a con so much as running his temperament
  at national scale, and it happened to mint a bubble. Control tic on record: he erased the people who built the
  magic — Lina Trivedi wrote the first 136 poems and Ty's first website, left uncredited in 1998, and decades
  later he still minimized her [F-28]. Refused interviews, refused to pay for marketing [S18]. *Interiority, not
  mood: this IS the engine of the con.*
- **Named human-cost / victim:** **Frances and Harold Mountain** — a real divorcing Las Vegas couple who, on
  Nov 5, 1999, had to kneel on a courtroom floor and split their Beanie collection toy-by-toy in front of a
  judge, a bailiff, a court reporter, and invited press [F-29]. The loss isn't money; it's dignity — two adults
  treating stuffed animals as the contested marital asset. **Second, harder victim:** **Harry Simmons**, 63, a
  security guard shot dead in Elkins, WV in Oct 1999 by Jeffrey White, in a dispute that included the Beanie
  Babies Simmons had lent White to start a trading business [F-30] — the mania as backdrop to a real killing.
  (Use Simmons carefully per the [F-30] note: Beanies were a factor, not the whole motive.)
- **Witnessed-absurd / telling details (3):** (1) the billionaire who **brushed the hair on the toys by hand**
  before they shipped [F-26]; (2) a judge telling a divorcing couple, over a pile of plush on his courtroom
  floor, **"This isn't about toys. It's about control"** [F-29][Q-02]; (3) a **50-cent pay-to-vote** on whether
  a toy line should "come back to life," proceeds to charity, ~90% voting yes [F-17] — a manufactured resurrection
  dressed as democracy.

2. **The product was real; the scarcity was manufactured** — under-stuffed, genuinely nicer toy, then the lever:
   "retiring" them + small-stores-only + 36-per-order. — [F-02][F-04][F-06][F-07][Q-01]
3. **How a toy became an "asset"** — collector checklists, a made-up price guide, then eBay quoting a "market
   price." — [F-08][F-09]
4. **The machine runs hot** — 1996 $280M → 1998 $1.4B; McDonald's 100M Teenie Beanies in 10 days; the crime wave

5. **Mid-video re-arm (the turn):** it was never the bear — it was the next buyer. Warner sold *scarcity*,
   collectors bought *belief*; the man's obsessive control WAS the scarcity machine. — [F-15][F-05][F-27]
6. **The break** — oversupply + the Sept-1999 "all retired" notice + the 50¢ vote nobody believed; demand stalls,

## Verification chain (vindication lever — how the case is PROVEN)

- **What is proven / uncontested:** the scarcity was DELIBERATE and documented in real time — Forbes printed
  Ty's "empty shelves" method in 1996 [Q-01][F-05], and the distribution levers (no big-box, per-order caps,
  "retirements") are corroborated across contemporaneous reporting [F-04][F-06][F-07]. The collapse to ~1% of
  peak is uncontested [F-19]. The tax evasion is a guilty plea on the federal record [F-24]. — [F-05][F-19][F-24]
- **What stays soft / contested:** the exact per-unit manufacturing cost [F-03] and eBay's precise sales share
  [F-09] are not primary-sourced — hold them loose. Warner's *psychology* [F-26][F-27] is reported through one
  journalist's interviews, not clinical fact — stage it as "people who worked with him said," not diagnosis.
- **Corroboration (independence-tagged):** the scarcity-was-deliberate core is backed by a 1996 contemporaneous
  Forbes piece AND a 2015 reported book working from separate interviews (Bissonnette) [S2],[S17] — two
  independent vantage points, decades apart, agreeing. The tax figures are internally consistent across the DOJ
  release, the 7th Circuit opinion, and Forbes [S14],[S15],[S16].
- **Exoneration (what the obvious villain did NOT do):** Warner did not *lie about the product* — the toys were
  real, arguably better-made than rivals [F-02][F-26]; and he did not run an illegal securities scheme — the
  bubble was structurally Ponzi-*adjacent* [F-15], not a charged fraud. Clearing those keeps the real
  indictment clean: the con wasn't a lie about the bear, it was the engineered *belief* that a bear was money.

## Analogies that teach AND indict (§5b candidates)

- "Retiring" a Beanie is like a bakery announcing it will *never* bake your favorite loaf again — while a factory
  two towns over stamps out a million a day. (grounds [F-04][F-03])
- The price guide was a scoreboard with no game underneath — numbers everyone agreed to believe, backed by
  nothing but the next person agreeing too. (grounds [F-08][F-15])
- It worked like musical chairs where the music is "new buyers": you profit only while someone keeps arriving to
  take the seat you're leaving. (grounds [F-15][F-18])

## Myths / misconceptions to bust

- **Myth:** Beanie Babies were rare. → **Actually:** mass-produced by the tens of millions; the rarity was
  engineered through distribution and "retirement." ([F-03][F-04][F-06]) — strong vindication payload.
- **Myth:** your Princess Diana bear is worth a fortune. → **Actually:** most sell for ~$5–$100 today; only rare
  first-run variants fetch more. ([F-20]) — the honest gut-punch.
- **Myth:** it was a spontaneous craze. → **Actually:** the scarcity was a documented, deliberate strategy
  (Forbes, 1996). ([F-05][Q-01])

## Why this matters (universality / second person) — the spine line

- **The rhyme:** manufactured-scarcity manias rhyme across centuries — tulip mania (1630s) → the dot-com bubble
  (1999–2000) → NFTs (2021) — the same shape as Beanie Babies: an object with little or no cashflow whose price
  is entirely the next buyer's belief. *(General-knowledge historical rhyme, flagged as such — not a sourced
  ledger claim.)*
- **The spottable tell:** the asset produces no income and can't be *used up* — its whole value is "someone
  later will pay more," and the supply is controlled by the person selling you the scarcity. When the pitch is
  "it's rare and it only goes up," the rarity is usually the product being sold to you [F-15][F-05].
- **Why it's the viewer's problem:** you are always somebody's "next buyer." The Mountains weren't stupid and
  the eBay bidders weren't stupid [F-29][F-09] — they were early enough to believe and late enough to lose.
  The bear didn't change; the belief did. The question the video hands the viewer: *in whatever you're holding
  now, are you the one selling scarcity, or the one buying belief?*
