# The Beanie Baby Fortune That Was Never Real

[B-ROLL: a soft toy sitting where a gold bar should be]
A stuffed frog cost pennies to make. It sold for five dollars. And at the peak, a rare one changed hands for more than five thousand.
[PAUSE]
The man who made them built a billion-dollar company on the feeling that they were rare. They never were.
[B-ROLL: identical toys stamped out by the million, off to the horizon]

[B-ROLL: the word RARE stenciled on a factory crate]
Here's the part the Beanie Baby story usually skips. The toys were real: soft, posable, a genuinely good little product. That part was never the con. The con was the shortage, and the shortage is the whole engine.
Nobody lied about the toy. They manufactured the feeling that there weren't enough to go around, on a product pouring out of overseas factories by the tens of millions.
[BEAT] Sell the sense of not-enough instead of an actual short supply, and a five-dollar plush animal starts trading like a stock.
(One note before we go: this is a story, not investment advice. Nobody here is telling you what to buy.)
Stick around. The moment it broke is the moment the whole thing makes sense.

[B-ROLL: 1993, a shelf of the first nine bears]
Start in 1993. Ty Warner launches nine designs, and the first one's a floppy frog named Legs. About five dollars each. They're understuffed on purpose, filled with plastic pellets instead of packed tight, so they flop and pose like real animals. Kids love them. So far, it's just a good toy.
Then, around 1995, Warner does the thing that changes everything. He starts retiring them.
[B-ROLL: a bear stamped RETIRED, a factory still running behind it]
He announces that a given Beanie Baby will never be made again, while his factories can stamp out a million more of almost anything he wants. [BEAT] It's a bakery swearing it will never bake your favorite loaf again, standing in front of an oven that never shuts off.
He stacks two more levers on top. He refuses to sell to the big chains, only small independent gift shops. So no single store ever has a pile of them, and they feel rare whether or not they are.
[B-ROLL: a shop counter, a hand-lettered limit sign]
And the supply gets pinched at the source: stores were reportedly capped at thirty-six of any one bear per order. Demand always runs a step ahead of the shelf, by design.
None of this was a secret. In 1996, Forbes wrote it down in plain English: the strategy here is empty shelves, the deliberate creation of scarcity, which pumps up word-of-mouth demand to a frenzied level. That's not a critic guessing after the fact. That's the playbook, described while it was working.

[B-ROLL: a bear's price tag, crossed out and rewritten higher, again and again]
A toy feeling rare is one thing. A toy becoming an investment is another, and that took a number.
The number came from a price guide. Collectors started publishing checklists that put a dollar value on every Beanie Baby: this one's worth ten, that one two hundred. Those figures weren't based on anything the toy did. No earnings, no dividend. A bear on a shelf makes you exactly nothing. The value was somebody's guess, printed in a booklet.
[BEAT] But once it's printed, people treat it as the price.
[B-ROLL: a scoreboard lit up over an empty, gameless field]
It's a scoreboard with no game underneath it: a number everyone agrees to believe, backed by nothing except the next person agreeing too.
Then eBay showed up and poured gasoline on it. Beanie Babies became one of the most-traded things on the entire site, going for around thirty dollars on average, six times the register price. Now that guessed-at number had a live feed. The booklet said thirty, eBay showed someone paying thirty, so the bear was worth thirty, and around it spins. The toy had a quoted market price. On paper, it had become an asset.

[B-ROLL: a revenue bar shooting past the top of the frame]
And the money underneath was real. Ty Inc., Warner's company, selling the actual toys, did about two hundred eighty million dollars in 1996. Around four hundred million in 1997. In 1998, it cleared one point four billion. [BEAT] That's just Ty selling toys to stores. None of the resale, none of the eBay flipping. That's a separate ocean of money sitting on top.
[B-ROLL: a Happy Meal box overflowing with tiny bears]
In the spring of 1997 came the accelerant: McDonald's. The Teenie Beanies promotion dropped miniature Beanie Babies into Happy Meals and moved something like a hundred million of them in about ten days, and set off shortages across the country.
Then it got dark. The mania bred real crime: thefts, store robberies, even hijacked shipments, all over stuffed animals. [BEAT] Sit with that. People are committing armed robbery over a toy a factory is churning out by the million. The gap between what these things cost and what people would do to get one? That's the bubble, fully inflated.

[B-ROLL: a bear passed hand to hand to hand down a line]
So here's the turn. If a Beanie Baby earns nothing, why does holding one make you money?
It doesn't. Your collection only climbs as long as new buyers keep showing up behind you, willing to pay more than you did. The toy was never the asset. The next person's belief was the asset.
[B-ROLL: a ring of chairs, one always being pulled away]
It runs on the logic of musical chairs, where the music is new buyers. You come out ahead only while someone keeps arriving to take the seat you're leaving. The second the crowd stops growing, whoever's holding a bear is just holding a bear.
[PAUSE]
And that changes what Warner was actually selling. He wasn't selling a rare collectible. He was selling the feeling of rarity itself, which he could manufacture more of whenever he liked. The collectors thought they were buying an investment. They were buying belief, and paying the one man who controlled the supply of it. He'd dealt himself the only guaranteed seat at the table.

[B-ROLL: warehouse shelves sagging under identical unsold bears]
By 1999, the trick stopped working, because you can't fake a shortage while flooding the market. Ty had made Beanies in enormous volume, demand had flattened, and prices began sliding as the new buyers dried up.
[B-ROLL: a press release with one line stapled to the bottom]
Warner's response was his biggest rarity stunt yet. In September 1999, he pinned a notice to a press release: on December 31st, at 11:59 p.m., all Beanies will be retired. The entire line, every last bear, gone for good.
[BEAT] Almost nobody believed him. Retiring things was the oldest move in his book.
Then came the strangest twist in the whole story. He staged a public vote on whether Beanie Babies should even continue. Voting cost fifty cents, officially to stop people voting twice, with the proceeds going to a children's AIDS charity. About ninety percent voted to keep them. And Warner, apparently shocked, revived the line with a new bear named, with a straight face, The Beginning.
[BEAT] Manufactured scarcity, all the way down. Announce the ending, sell the panic, then let the crowd beg you to reverse it. But the crowd wasn't growing anymore, and no vote could fix that.

[B-ROLL: a bin of bears marked 20 cents each]
So what happened to all those bears people were guarding like gold bars?
Most lost about ninety-nine percent of their peak value. They didn't drift down. They fell off a cliff. Bulk lots got dumped for twenty to forty cents apiece, and most Beanie Babies today sell for somewhere between five and twenty dollars.
[B-ROLL: the Princess Diana bear on a velvet cushion, then in a yard-sale box]
And the crown jewel, the purple Princess Diana memorial bear, the one people were certain would cover a kid's tuition? It retailed for around five or six dollars, spiked to three or four hundred at the peak, and today most of them sell for about five to a hundred. A few rare first-run versions reach into the thousands. Yours, almost certainly, is not one of them.
[PAUSE]
Through all of it, exactly one person was guaranteed to come out ahead. It wasn't the collectors betting on the bears. It was the man who sold them the shortage. He made his billion on the way up, and he kept it.

[B-ROLL: a numbered Swiss bank door]
About that billion. In January 1996, just as the mania caught fire, Warner flew to Zurich and, according to the Justice Department, deposited around eighty million dollars at a Swiss bank, instructed them to mail no statements to the U.S., and kept the account secret until 2007.
The record says he never reported at least twenty-four million dollars in interest from that account, and evaded at least five point six million in taxes. In 2013, he pleaded guilty to a single count of tax evasion, and agreed to pay about sixteen million in back taxes plus a civil penalty of fifty-three and a half million.
[B-ROLL: a courthouse door, quiet]
The sentence for hiding tens of millions offshore? Two years of probation, community service, and no prison time. The guidelines called for something like four to five years behind bars. An appeals court let the no-jail sentence stand.
[PAUSE]
The man who sold the shortage walked. The collectors, the ones who believed the bears were rare, got to keep the bears.
