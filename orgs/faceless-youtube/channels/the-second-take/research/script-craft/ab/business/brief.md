## Subject (identified from reference only; researched independently)

The **Beanie Baby speculative bubble** — Ty Warner and Ty Inc. A ~$5 understuffed plush toy line
launched 1993 that, over ~1995–1999, became a mass get-rich collectible mania in which ordinary
adults treated stuffed animals as appreciating financial assets, resold them for hundreds to
thousands of dollars, and then watched the value evaporate to near zero. Same story the reference
covers; our facts and framing are our own.

## Angle / POV (our original take)

Not "the crazy Beanie Baby craze" (the saturated nostalgia retread everyone's told). Our angle:
**Beanie Babies were a manufactured-scarcity machine, and the "investment" was structurally a bubble
whose fuel was new buyers** — a con built not on lying about the product but on *engineering the
perception of rarity* (retirement announcements, small-store-only distribution, order caps) around a
toy being stamped out by the tens of millions for pennies. The vindication payload: the thing that
made you money was never the bear — it was the next person's belief that a bear was money. When Ty
stopped controlling scarcity, the whole thing was exposed as ordinary supply and demand.

## Hook promise (the <5s curiosity gap the video pays off)

A stuffed animal that cost about a dollar to make, sold for five, and got resold for tens of
thousands — and the man who ran it became a billionaire while telling everyone the bears were rare.
Promise: by the end you'll see the exact lever he pulled, and why it *had* to collapse.

## Payload promise (what the viewer walks away actually knowing)

The concrete mechanism of a manufactured-scarcity collectible bubble: (1) how artificial "retirement"
and refusal to sell to big-box chains created fake rarity; (2) how a secondary-market price guide +
eBay turned a toy into a quoted "asset" with no underlying value; (3) the Ponzi-adjacent dynamic —
existing holders profit only while new buyers keep entering — and the precise moment/decision that
broke it (over-production + the "retire everything" stunt that nobody believed). Ends on the part the
system never spells out: the winner was the manufacturer selling scarcity, not the collectors buying it.

## Key questions the video must answer

1. What did a Beanie Baby actually cost to manufacture, and what did the rare ones peak at on resale?
   (the absurd-number contrast)
2. What were the *specific* scarcity levers Ty used — "retiring" toys, small-store-only distribution,
   per-store order caps — and roughly when did each start?
3. Where did the "these are investments" belief actually come from? (secondary-market price guides,
   collector checklists, magazines, eBay's share of Beanie sales)
4. In what concrete sense was it bubble/Ponzi-like vs. just a fad — who profits, who's left holding it?
5. What broke it? (over-production ~1998–99, the December-1999 "retire all Beanies" stunt + the 50-cent
   public vote, the ~90% sales collapse)
6. What happened to Ty Warner afterward (net worth; the 2013–14 tax-evasion case + sentence) — traced
   to primary/reputable sources, phrased as what the record says, not our accusation.
7. The honest payoff: are old Beanie Babies worth anything today?
