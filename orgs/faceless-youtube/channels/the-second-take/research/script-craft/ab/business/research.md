## Fact ledger  ← the scriptwriter may state ONLY what appears here

- **[F-01]** Ty Warner launched Beanie Babies in 1993 (first nine designs, led by "Legs the Frog"), priced
  at about $5 retail. — *Src:* [S3][S4], 1993 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the ~$5 point-of-sale price is the anchor for every later contrast.
- **[F-02]** The toys were deliberately *under-stuffed* with PVC pellets ("beans"), making them soft and
  posable — a design choice that also kept them cheap to make. — *Src:* [S1][S3] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-03]** Beanie Babies were manufactured in overseas factories (South Korea early, then China) at a
  small fraction of the retail price — commonly described as cents per unit. — *Src:* [S1][S3] · *Conf:* low ·
  *Note:* the "≈25–65¢ each" figure is widely repeated but I could not tie it to a primary; treat as
  order-of-magnitude ("a toy that cost pennies-to-about-a-dollar to make"), not a hard cited number.
- **[F-04]** Warner's core scarcity tactic — "retiring" individual Beanie Babies to manufacture the
  illusion of limited supply — began around 1995. — *Src:* [S2][S5] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-05]** A 1996 Forbes article described the strategy explicitly: "the strategy here is empty shelves —
  the deliberate creation of scarcity, which pumps up word-of-mouth demand to a frenzied level." — *Src:*
  [S2] (Forbes, 1996, quoted) · *Conf:* high · *Note:* on-record contemporaneous primary framing; strong for the vindication payload.
- **[F-06]** Ty refused to sell to big-box chains, distributing only through independent gift/specialty
  stores — so no single store ever had many, making them *feel* rare regardless of true volume. — *Src:*
  [S2][S5] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-07]** Ty limited retailers to 36 of any one Beanie Baby per order, keeping store supply below
  demand by design. — *Src:* [S2][S5] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* "36 per order/month" recurs across secondary sources; not traced to a Ty document.
- **[F-08]** A collector-run secondary-market price guide assigned dollar "values" to each Beanie Baby —
  numbers based on guesswork, not any underlying worth — and the resale market began treating those figures
  as real prices, which is what reframed a toy as an "investment." — *Src:* [S3][S5] · *Conf:* med.
- **[F-09]** On eBay, Beanie Babies resold for an average of about $30 — roughly six times the ~$5 retail —
  and were among the site's most-traded items in the late 1990s. — *Src:* [S3][S4] · *Conf:* med · *Note:*
  the oft-cited "~10% of all eBay sales" share is repeated widely but I could not source it to eBay/primary
  data; use the $30-avg / "one of the most-traded items" framing, not the 10% stat.
- **[F-10]** Rare Beanies commanded extreme resale prices at the peak — sought-after "#1 Bear"-class items
  reportedly changing hands for $5,000+ apiece. — *Src:* [S3][S4] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* peak anecdote,
  thin-market prices; frame as "sold for" not "worth."
- **[F-11]** Ty Inc. reported about $280 million in revenue in 1996. — *Src:* [S5][S6] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-12]** Ty Inc. revenue was roughly $400 million in 1997 and surpassed ~$1.4 billion in 1998. —
  *Src:* [S5][S6] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* these are official Ty sales, excluding all secondary-market resale.
- **[F-13]** In spring 1997 Ty partnered with McDonald's on "Teenie Beanies" in Happy Meals; the promotion
  distributed on the order of 100 million units in about 10 days and triggered nationwide shortages. —
  *Src:* [S7][S8] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-14]** The mania produced real crime — thefts, store incidents, arrests tied to Beanie Babies and
  Teenie Beanies (including hijacked shipments and armed store thefts). — *Src:* [S8][S9] · *Conf:* high ·
  *Note:* documented category of incidents; keep specific named-defendant claims out unless individually sourced.
- **[F-15]** The value proposition was structurally bubble/Ponzi-adjacent: an existing collection only
  rose in value while *new* buyers kept entering, so holders were incentivized to recruit more buyers — the
  toy itself produced no income. — *Src:* [S3][S5] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* frame as mechanism/analysis, not
  as a legal "Ponzi scheme" accusation.
- **[F-16]** In September 1999 Ty appended a notice to a press release: "On December 31, 1999-11:59 p.m.
  (CST) All Beanies will be retired," announcing the entire line would end. — *Src:* [S10][S11] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-17]** Ty then staged a public vote on whether Beanie Babies should continue; voting cost 50¢ (to
  prevent multiple votes), proceeds went to the Elizabeth Glaser Pediatric AIDS Foundation, and about 90%
  voted to continue — after which Ty released a new line ("The Beginning" bear) in 2000. — *Src:* [S10][S11]
  · *Conf:* high · *Note:* widely read as a manufactured-scarcity marketing stunt, not a real shutdown.
- **[F-18]** By 1999 the market was oversupplied — Ty had produced Beanies in enormous volume and demand
  had stopped growing — and prices began falling as new buyers dried up; the bubble deflated. — *Src:*
  [S5][S9] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-19]** In the collapse, most Beanie Babies fell to roughly 1% of their original resale value; bulk
  lots were offered at ~20–40¢ per toy, and most are worth only about $5–$20 today. — *Src:* [S3][S12] ·
  *Conf:* high.
- **[F-20]** Even the most-hyped item — the Princess Diana memorial bear — is typically worth far less than
  collectors paid: it retailed ~$5–$7, spiked to ~$350–$400 at kiosks in Dec 1997, and today most versions
  sell for about $5–$100 (only rare first-run variants reach the high hundreds/thousands). — *Src:* [S12][S13]
  · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the "$25,000 Princess bear" is a rare-variant outlier, not the norm — the honest payoff beat.
- **[F-21]** Ty Warner became a billionaire from Ty Inc. and remained one long after the bubble. — *Src:*
  [S1][S4] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-22]** On/around Jan 31, 1996, Warner flew to Zurich and deposited about $80 million at UBS AG,
  instructing that no account statements be sent to him in the U.S., and kept the account secret until 2007.
  — *Src:* [S14] (DOJ release, 2013, quoted) · *Conf:* high · *Note:* stated as what the DOJ charge/plea alleges.
- **[F-23]** Warner failed to report at least $24.4 million in interest income on the offshore account and
  evaded at least $5.6 million in U.S. taxes; the account balance reached ~$93.6 million when he moved it to
  Zürcher Kantonalbank in late 2002. — *Src:* [S14] (DOJ, 2013) · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-24]** Warner pleaded guilty to one count of tax evasion in October 2013, agreed to pay ~$16 million
  in back taxes/interest and a $53.5 million civil penalty. — *Src:* [S14][S15] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-25]** He was sentenced to two years' probation, community service, and ~$100,000 in fines/costs —
  no prison — against an advisory guideline range of 46–57 months; the 7th Circuit upheld the no-jail
  sentence in 2015. — *Src:* [S15] (7th Cir., 2015)[S16] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the judge cited Warner's
  charitable giving; critics noted it was a small share of his net worth — attribute as commentary, not fact.

## Narrative spine (recommended section outline — the writer's outline seed)

1. **The absurd contrast (cold open)** — a toy that cost pennies to make, sold for $5, resold for
   thousands, and minted a billionaire. — [F-01][F-03][F-10][F-21]
2. **The product was real; the scarcity was manufactured** — under-stuffed, genuinely nicer toy, then the
   lever: "retiring" them + small-stores-only + 36-per-order. — [F-02][F-04][F-06][F-07]
3. **How a toy became an "asset"** — collector checklists, a made-up price guide, then eBay quoting a
   "market price." — [F-08][F-09]
4. **The machine runs hot** — 1996 $280M → 1998 $1.4B; McDonald's 100M Teenie Beanies in 10 days; the crime
   wave. — [F-11][F-12][F-13][F-14]
5. **Mid-video re-arm (the turn):** it was never the bear — it was the next buyer. The value only existed
   while new people kept entering; Warner sold *scarcity*, collectors bought *belief*. — [F-15][F-05]
6. **The break** — oversupply + the Sept-1999 "all retired Dec 31" notice + the 50¢ vote stunt nobody
   believed; demand stalls, prices fall. — [F-16][F-17][F-18]
7. **The withheld fine print (final 20%)** — most are worth ~1% today; even the Princess Diana bear is
   usually $5–$100; the only guaranteed winner was the manufacturer. — [F-19][F-20]
8. **Dry close** — Warner stayed a billionaire, hid $80M in Switzerland, pleaded guilty to tax evasion, and
   did no prison time. The collectors kept the bears. — [F-21][F-22][F-24][F-25]

- **Cold-open candidate:** a ~$5 toy resold for $5,000+, and the man behind it became a billionaire while
  telling everyone they were rare — [F-10][F-21][F-05].
- **Mid-video re-arm (the turn):** the "asset" was the next buyer's belief, not the toy — a bubble whose
  only fuel was new entrants — [F-15].
- **Withheld "fine print" (final 20%):** almost every Beanie is now worth ~1% of its peak; the scarcity was
  fake all along; the only reliable profit went to the one man selling the scarcity — [F-19][F-20][F-21].

## Analogies that teach AND indict (§5b candidates)

- "Retiring" a Beanie is like a bakery announcing it will *never* bake your favorite loaf again — while a
  factory two towns over stamps out a million of them a day. (grounds [F-04][F-03])
- The price guide was a scoreboard with no game underneath — numbers everyone agreed to believe, backed by
  nothing but the next person agreeing too. (grounds [F-08][F-15])
- It worked like musical chairs where the music is "new buyers": you profit only while someone keeps
  arriving to take the seat you're leaving. (grounds [F-15][F-18])

## Myths / misconceptions to bust

- **Myth:** Beanie Babies were rare. → **Actually:** they were mass-produced by the tens of millions;
  the rarity was engineered through distribution and "retirement," not scarcity of supply. ([F-03][F-04][F-06])
  — strong vindication payload.
- **Myth:** your Princess Diana bear is worth a fortune. → **Actually:** most versions sell for about
  $5–$100 today; only rare first-run variants fetch more. ([F-20]) — the honest gut-punch.
- **Myth:** it was a spontaneous craze. → **Actually:** the scarcity was a documented, deliberate strategy
  (Forbes, 1996). ([F-05])
