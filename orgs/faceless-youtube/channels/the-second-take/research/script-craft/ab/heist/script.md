# Script — The Bank Robbery That Died Over Twenty Dollars

- **Source idea:** ST-AB-heist (test) · **Channel:** The Second Take · **Target:** 8–15 min · **Status:** scripted
- **Voice:** The Second Take narrator — dry-smart insider who tells it like a story
- **Estimated runtime:** ~10:47  *(≈1,618 spoken words ÷ 150 wpm)*  ·  humanized (no em-dashes) · short beats only where they land dry wit or a concrete payoff, no dramatic-portent staccato
- **Leashed to:** `research.md` fact ledger. Hedged per Open-Questions: prior-robbery figures + the $5,000 reward phrased "by most accounts / about" (med-confidence, single-threaded); Bob's Jesse James line as "reportedly wanted"; Emmett's Heck-Thomas motive as "Emmett said years later"; the Condon $1,500 loose-cash figure not stated; the "no insurance so the town fought" motive NOT used (unsourced). Emmett's "never fired a shot" presented only as his own claim.

## LONG-FORM VOICEOVER

[B-ROLL: a quiet main street in a small Kansas town at morning, two brick banks facing each other across it] On the morning of October fifth, eighteen ninety-two, five men rode into a small town in Kansas to rob two banks at once. [B-ROLL: eight plain grave markers standing in a row] By the time the shooting stopped, twelve minutes of gunfire later, eight people were dead. Four of the robbers, and four of the townsmen who tried to stop them. [PAUSE] And then there is the number that makes the whole thing land wrong. When the banks counted up afterward, the money the gang actually cost them came to about twenty dollars.

[B-ROLL: a classic Wild West wanted poster, a generic outlaw glowering] You have heard this kind of story before. The gang, the disguises, the shootout in the dusty street. Jesse James is remembered for exactly this sort of thing. [B-ROLL: the narrator avatar, flat and unimpressed] But the raid on Coffeyville, Kansas, is not really a gunfight story. It is a money story wearing a gunfight's clothes. Because the Dalton gang did not die because they were bad shots. They died because they were bad at math. [PAUSE] Every decision that put them in that street was a bet, and every bet was priced wrong. This is the story of how five men ran the numbers on the biggest score of their lives, got every line of it backwards, and paid for the error in the one town where everybody knew their faces.

[B-ROLL: a steam train crossing open plains, riders on horseback pulling alongside] Before Coffeyville, the Daltons were train robbers. For about a year and a half, across what was then Oklahoma Territory, they hit the railroads. Perry, Orlando, Red Rock, Adair, Pryor Creek, one job after another. [B-ROLL: a canvas mail sack tipped over on a train floor, banknotes spilling out] And the money was real. By most accounts their train jobs pulled in something like ten thousand dollars off the Missouri-Kansas-Texas line, and a robbery at Pryor Creek that July reportedly netted around seventeen thousand more. [BEAT] For eighteen ninety-two, that is a serious amount of money.

But there was a second number, and it is the one the gang could never shake off. [B-ROLL: a reward poster nailed to a post, "$5,000," a Dalton name inked across the bottom] After the train jobs, the law put a price on their heads: about five thousand dollars on each Dalton. [PAUSE] Think about what that does to the cost of doing business. Every successful robbery made the next one harder, because the more they stole, the more armed men were being paid to hunt them down. They were running a company whose only product kept driving up the price of staying in business. The cash added up slowly. The heat added up a great deal faster.

[B-ROLL: two brick banks side by side on a hand-drawn town map, both circled in ink] So Bob Dalton, the leader, came up with a plan to fix all of it in a single morning. Not one bank. Two. At the same time. [B-ROLL: the narrator avatar, one eyebrow slightly raised] Two banks on the same street, robbed simultaneously by the same five men. Why would anyone double the size of the job when they were already the most wanted men in the territory?

Two reasons, and both of them are very human. [B-ROLL: a portrait of Bob Dalton, chin up, hungry for it] The first was reputation. Bob reportedly wanted to beat anything Jesse James had ever done. Rob two banks at once, in broad daylight, and put his name above every outlaw in the country. [PAUSE] The second reason surfaced years later, from his younger brother Emmett. Emmett said the gang was being hunted relentlessly by a deputy US marshal named Heck Thomas, and that one enormous score was meant to fund their exit: enough money to vanish out of the territory for good.

[B-ROLL: a hand pushing two tall stacks of betting chips together into one pile] Put those two reasons together, because the combination is the trap. [B-ROLL: the two circled banks again, the ink circle swelling wider] Robbing two banks at once is doubling your position to win back your losses faster. The upside doubles. That part is true. But so does the thing that can kill you. Twice the men inside, twice the minutes exposed, twice the odds of one clerk reaching under the counter. They stared at the doubled reward and somehow never looked straight at the doubled risk. [BEAT] It is the oldest mistake in money, and they made it on horseback.

[B-ROLL: the familiar storefronts of Coffeyville's main street, homey and specific] There was one more cost in the plan, and it was the one they treated as free. Coffeyville was their hometown. [PAUSE] These were not strangers riding into an anonymous town. People here had watched the Dalton boys grow up. [B-ROLL: false beards and disguises, a shade too obvious to work] The gang knew that, so they showed up in disguises. False beards, that kind of thing. It did not work. [B-ROLL: a townsman on the sidewalk, squinting, the recognition landing on his face] They were spotted almost the moment they arrived, because a fake beard does not hide a walk, or a voice, or a face your neighbors have known for twenty years.

[B-ROLL: the two banks, Bob and Emmett peeling toward one, the other three crossing to the opposite door] They split up the way the plan called for. Bob and Emmett went into the First National Bank. Grat Dalton, Bill Powers, and Dick Broadwell went into the Condon bank across the street. [BEAT] The clock that would decide everything started the second they walked in recognized. They just did not know it was running.

[B-ROLL: inside the Condon bank, three armed robbers, a cashier standing very still behind the counter] Here is where the whole raid turns, and it turns on a single sentence. [B-ROLL: the cashier, hands flat on the counter, thinking fast under the gun] The robbers ordered the Condon cashier, a man named Charley Ball, to open the safe. And Ball, standing at gunpoint, invented something on the spot. He told them the safe was on a time lock. It could not be opened, he said, until about nine forty-five. Roughly ten minutes out.

[PAUSE] It was a lie. [B-ROLL: the safe door, plainly shut but plainly openable, no lock in sight] There was no time lock. The safe would have opened right then. It was not even quite nine forty-five yet. But the robbers did not know that, and standing there with guns drawn, they had no way to check. So Grat Dalton reportedly said the two words that cost him everything. [PAUSE:LONG] "I'll wait."

[B-ROLL: the narrator avatar, leaning in slightly] Look at what they actually did here, because you have met this decision in a hundred quieter forms. Nobody checked. They waited, inside a bank, in their own hometown, on an unverified claim about a number, told to them by the one man in the room with every reason to lie. [PAUSE] It is the same mistake as taking a counterparty's word on a figure you could have checked yourself in five seconds. The whole plan lived or died on speed, and they handed those minutes away because a clerk told them a story about a clock.

[B-ROLL: townsmen slipping into a hardware store, pulling rifles down off the wall rack] And the town used every one of those minutes. Right beside the banks stood a hardware store. While the gang stood around waiting on a lock that did not exist, the men of Coffeyville walked into that store, took the guns and the ammunition off the shelves, and quietly filled the street around them.

[B-ROLL: the street gone still, then the first muzzle flashes, colour draining from the frame] So when the gang finally stepped back out, the street was ready. [PAUSE] What followed lasted about twelve minutes. [B-ROLL: the aftermath, desaturated and stylized, no gore, just stillness] It was less a battle than a trap closing. The townsmen had the cover, the numbers, and the guns from their own hardware store, and they were defending their own homes.

[B-ROLL: four plain grave markers for the gang, side by side] Four of the five robbers died in those twelve minutes. Bob Dalton, Grat Dalton, Bill Powers, and Dick Broadwell. [PAUSE] But four townsmen died too, and they are the part of this story that should never get lost in the arithmetic. [B-ROLL: four names lettered plainly on a board, no music flourish] Marshal Charles Connelly, forty-six, who was also the town schoolteacher. George Cubine, a shoemaker, thirty-six. Charles Brown, a shoemaker, fifty-nine. Lucius Baldwin, a store clerk, twenty-three. Three of them were killed near the hardware store. The marshal was killed in a passage the town would forever after just call Death Alley. [BEAT] A teacher, two shoemakers, and a clerk, dead in their own street on a Wednesday morning.

[B-ROLL: one robber on the ground, badly wounded but breathing] Only one of the five lived. Emmett Dalton, the youngest, survived with something like twenty-three gunshot wounds. [PAUSE] And now we reach the part the legend tends to skip. The fine print at the bottom of the whole disastrous deal.

[B-ROLL: two bank ledgers open on a desk, clerks running their fingers down the columns] Remember, this was all for the money. So how much did the gang actually get? [B-ROLL: the ledger columns settling, totals balancing out to almost nothing] They had grabbed roughly twenty-five thousand dollars between the two banks. Then they lost all of it. [PAUSE] Every dollar was recovered off the dead and out of the street. When the banks balanced their books afterward, the Condon came up about twenty dollars short, and the First National actually came out a dollar and change over. [PAUSE:LONG] That was the entire haul. About twenty dollars, for eight lives. [BEAT] They did not lose the shootout and keep the prize. There was never any prize to keep. A robbery with no way to carry the cash off is a trade with a perfect entry and no exit. The taking was never the hard part. The keeping was, and they had not planned it at all.

[B-ROLL: Emmett in a courtroom, then the iron gate of a penitentiary swinging shut] Emmett lived to answer for it. In eighteen ninety-three he pleaded guilty to second-degree murder and was sentenced to life in the Kansas penitentiary at Lansing. He served around fourteen years. In nineteen oh seven he was paroled, and then pardoned, by the governor of Kansas.

[B-ROLL: a silent-film poster, Emmett Dalton billed as himself] And here is the last turn, the one that is almost too neat to be true. The sole survivor of a robbery that netted twenty dollars spent the rest of his life selling the story of it. [B-ROLL: a stack of books, a reel of film unspooling] Emmett became an author and an actor. He played himself in a nineteen eighteen silent film about the raid, and later moved into construction and real estate out near Los Angeles. He even claimed, for the record, that he had never fired a shot that day. [PAUSE] The one man who survived doing the math wrong found the single part of the whole scheme that ever actually paid. Not the robbery. The telling of it.

[B-ROLL: a single faded reward poster curling off a wall] The Daltons get remembered as unlucky. A good plan spoiled by a mob. [PAUSE] But there was no bad luck in Coffeyville. They robbed the one town that already knew their faces, waited on a lie any of them could have tested, and reached for money that could never leave the street. [PAUSE:LONG] Four robbers, four neighbors, and a set of books that came out twenty dollars short. [BEAT] The only thing they ever really stole was twelve minutes. And most of those, they gave away.
