# Self-diagnosis — does OUR research.md carry story scaffolding, or is it a fact-ledger with citations?

**Verdict: MIXED, leaning fact-ledger-with-scaffolding-pointers.** The dossier is honest, sourced, and
scoped — it will keep a scriptwriter from inventing anything. But if you ask "is this already a *story*, or
is it raw material with a story gestured at in the margins," the honest answer is the latter. The
narrative lives in three thin sections stapled to a fat ledger; it is *indicated*, not *built*.

## The honest read

This is **not a vapor fact-dump** — it has a spine, a designated cold-open, a designated turn, a withheld
payoff, myth-busts, and analogies that indict. By the researcher SKILL's own contract it is doing its job:
the *template forces* scaffolding sections, so scaffolding exists.

But the template also reveals the limit: **the scaffolding is only as deep as a few bullet lines, while
90% of the word-count is the atomic ledger.** The ledger is, correctly and by design, facts-only — each
entry is one checkable sentence with a source and a confidence rating. All the *story* is compressed into
the spine/analogy/myth sections, and those are written as **pointers for the writer** ("draws on
[F-11]"), not as prose that has already found the emotional beat. The characters have motives but no
interiority; the stakes are named but not felt; the ironic details are catalogued but not *timed*.

That is arguably **the correct division of labor** for this pipeline — the researcher's job is the leash
(sourced facts + a defensible arc), and `long-form-writer` is where the beats get *landed* and the
second-person "why this matters" gets written. The dossier deliberately does NOT pre-write the drama,
because pre-written drama on unsourced emotional claims is exactly the "vapor" the payload rule bans (see
how I killed the "no insurance, so the town fought to the death" line — it's the most *dramatically useful*
beat in the whole story and I refused it because it wasn't sourced).

## Where that division of labor could bite

- **The best character is buried.** Charley Ball, the cashier who bluffs an armed gang with a fake time
  lock, is the story's hero and its cleanest irony. He's one clause in [F-10]. A story-first research pass
  would have hunted his name, his exact words, what happened to him — and given the writer a *person* to
  hang the turn on. I noted the mechanism; I didn't develop the character.
- **The emotional "why the town fought" is a flagged gap, not a solved one.** I was disciplined (good) but
  the result is that the single most story-critical *motive on the winning side* is something I told the
  writer to hedge or drop. A story-ready dossier would have gone one more search deep to either source it
  or find the real reason, instead of leaving a hole where the emotional core should be.
- **Motive is thin and self-sourced.** The two load-bearing motive facts ([F-08] Bob's quote, [F-09]
  Emmett's Heck-Thomas account) are both med-confidence and both trace to a self-interested narrator. The
  vindication payload ("they lost on the math, not the luck") rests on motive I've correctly labeled as
  soft. Honest — but it means the *thesis* is better-sourced on the outcome than on the "why."

## One-line diagnosis

**Story-ready-ENOUGH, not story-built:** a sound, sourced, well-scoped ledger with a correct arc and the
right ironic details flagged — but the cast, the stakes, and the "why this matters" are *pointed at* in
three thin sections, not developed, and the pipeline is trusting `long-form-writer` to turn those pointers
into a landed story. For a well-documented heist that's a reasonable bet; the risk is that the most
human, most story-making material (the cashier's bluff, the town's motive) got left as one-line mechanisms
because the ledger rewards atomic facts over narrative texture.
