## Crayon Capital — 5000 Years of Gold

Style baseline: crude flat 2D "stick-comic" line art (South-Park-of-doodles look) — bald egg/oval
heads, dot eyes, thin black outlines, flat fills. Almost everything is delivered as **short
reenacted skits with speech bubbles + handwritten italic captions**, punctuated by **black title
cards**, **fake newspapers/TV/radio**, **crude charts**, and **labeled props**. The literal
narration ("here is what happened") is almost never shown as a floating explainer — it is *acted
out by tiny characters saying the idea out loud*. This is the channel's core trick: abstraction →
dialogue.

### B. Recurring narration→visual patterns (NON-literal moves called out)

1. **Abstraction is almost always ACTED, not diagrammed.** Rarity, inflation, debasement, trust,
   over-printing — each becomes a tiny two-character skit with a speech bubble stating the idea in
   plain words. The narrator explains; the puppets *demonstrate*. [NON-literal: concept → skit]
2. **Nations & institutions are single characters wearing a flag.** A country = one egg-head in a
   suit with that nation's flag as a **necktie** (US, China), or standing next to its flag. An
   empire = its landmark building (Colosseum, Fort Knox bunker). [NON-literal: institution → person/building]
3. **Black one-line title cards** carry the spine: chapter beats ("Rare"), transitions ("So
   governments were like…"), teasers ("something strange in gold vaults"), ironic setups ("It was
   foolproof!"), and the closing quote. Cheap, punchy, rhythm-setting.
4. **Diegetic media deliver historical events** so the narrator doesn't have to: fake NEW YORK
   TIMES front pages (twice), a period radio ("World War 1 begin today"), a vintage TV showing
   Nixon, a stock-monitor showing the gold chart. [NON-literal: event → in-world newspaper/TV/radio]
5. **Numbers live glued to the object they measure.** "110 kilog" beside the sarcophagus, "$35 /
   1971" on the gold stack, "40%" beside the cash pile — the stat and its referent share the frame.
6. **Crude, deliberately-lo-fi charts** for anything quantitative (inflation bars, treasury-gold
   decline, gold-price climb, pound-decline) — often with a **narration question overlaid on top**
   of the chart, turning data into a backdrop.
7. **Recurring bit-characters & callbacks** create continuity: the greedy crowned king, the
   alchemist, the smug sunglasses "gold bull," the flag-tie nation-suits.
8. **Dark-comic literalization of stakes:** "died for it" → little corpses; "cut off his hands" →
   on-screen; wealth → characters swimming in gold piles.

### B2. ABSTRACT-ECONOMIC-CONCEPT → CONCRETE-IMAGE (priority output)

### C. Within-shot motion patterns

- Motion is minimal and cheap — this is near-motion-graphics over illustration. Dominant moves:
  - **Text type-on / fade** on title cards and number callouts (the primary "animation").
  - **Number tick / label pop-in** next to objects (110 kg, $35, 40%).
  - **Element pop-in**: coins appearing on the map; props sliding into a skit.
  - **Chart draw**: bars rising, area/line drawing left→right.
  - **Slow camera push** on establishing icons (Colosseum, Fort Knox).
  - **Idle micro-motion**: sparkle twinkle on gold, flame flicker, subtle character bob.
  - **Character gesture/lean/recoil** carries emotion in the skits (angry king recoil, smug lean-back).
  - Scale transforms (**coin shrinking**) used as the actual explanatory device.
- Almost no walk-cycles, parallax, or complex camera work. The "movement" is really *cutting +
  speech-bubble timing + one accent animation per shot.*

### D. Cut cadence feel

- Fast and skit-driven. Most shots are **2–5 seconds**; a skit beat = one speech bubble then cut.
- Rhythm = **black card → skit → skit → payoff line → black card**. The cards act as breath/reset
  beats and chapter dividers, roughly every 30–60s.
- Dialogue exchanges cut on each line (shot-reverse-ish, but usually just swapping who's talking).
- Data/number moments hold slightly longer to let the figure land.
- Overall: relentless forward motion, no shot outstays ~5s, humor beat roughly every 15–25s to keep
  retention. Never a static "narrator over one B-roll" stretch.

### E. Top 5 transferable lessons for an illustrated FINANCE explainer's visual-prompt-writer

1. **Default to the SKIT, not the diagram.** For any abstract mechanism (inflation, debasement,
   trust, a policy choice), the strongest move is a 2-character mini-scene where the characters *say
   the idea in plain words* and one small action demonstrates it (haggling at a bread stall, a coin
   shrinking, a scale tipping). Prompt-writer rule: "concept → who are the two people arguing about
   it, and what tiny prop/action shows it?" This beats a floating labeled illustration every time.
2. **Personify institutions with a single wearable tag.** A country/central bank/government = one
   recurring character marked by a **flag-as-necktie** (or its landmark building). This lets an
   explainer stage geopolitics and monetary policy as *conversations between characters* and reuse
   a small cast. Give the channel a fixed "cast" of these tagged figures.
3. **Glue every number to its object, and keep charts deliberately crude.** Stats land when the
   figure sits *on/next to the thing it measures* (110 kg on the sarcophagus, $35 on the gold
   stack, 40% on the cash pile). For trends, a simple hand-drawn bar/area chart — often with the
   narration *question overlaid on the chart* — is enough; polish is not the point.
4. **Use diegetic media to deliver history and let black cards carry the spine.** Route "X happened
   in year Y" through a **fake newspaper front page, a period radio, or a TV set** instead of
   narrator-over-b-roll; it's more textured and reads as evidence. Between beats, use **stark
   black one-line cards** for chapters, ironic setups, and teasers ("something strange in gold
   vaults…") — they're the cheapest retention/rhythm tool available.
5. **Find the ONE transformation that IS the explanation, and animate only that.** Motion budget is
   tiny, so spend it on the single move that carries the idea: the coin that shrinks (debasement),
   the scale that tips (over-printing), the chart that draws itself. Everything else stays near-still
   with only text type-on, a pop-in, or a sparkle. One meaningful animated transform per shot >
   lots of ambient motion.
