# Research dossier — Diamonds Are Worthless. One Ad Made Them Forever. (revised: How Diamonds Became Forever)

- **Channel:** The Second Take · **Slug:** 2026-07-30-diamonds · **Source idea:** ST-013
- **Researched:** 2026-07-30 · **Register/lever (from dna):** plain-concrete-specific, witty / vindication
- **Confidence overall:** mixed — the campaign, CSO/stockpile mechanism, and industrial-diamond guilty plea are well documented; an original two-months-salary advertisement and a defensible universal 2026 resale percentage were not retrieved.

## Research plan (what was and wasn't chased)
- **Tier and method:** **LIGHT-plus.** Native `deep-research` was unavailable. Manual pass: eight live web requests (one connectivity probe; seven targeted searches/fetches) on The Atlantic investigation, De Beers’ own history, DOJ/antitrust records, Oxford academic research, an EU competition notice, and lab-grown reporting. The final request was a skeptic pass on “two months’ salary” and “diamonds are worthless.”
- **Questions pursued:** What did the CSO and its stockpile actually do? What did Ayer/Gerety demonstrably create? What does a dated resale test prove rather than an internet resale statistic? What did the 2004 US case cover? What has lab-grown supply changed, as of 2026? What honest material, cutting, retail, and emotional value survives the anti-hype framing?
- **Chase list:** 1938–47 campaign record; Gerety/slogan; CSO sightholders, stockpiles, historical share; the US industrial-diamond plea; a concrete resale example; chemistry and price trend of lab-grown stones.
- **Ignore list:** generic 4Cs buying advice; unsourced “diamond scam” numbers; a full conflict-diamond history; investment advice; claims that every producer, jeweler, or current De Beers practice repeats the historic model.
- **Source-quality bar:** primary government/company records plus academic or reputable primary reporting. Load-bearing supply-share claims have independent corroboration; retail-resale claims stay dated and illustrative; current lab-grown claims are stated **as of 2026** only through their dated source series.
- **Viability check:** retain the supply-control + demand-invention payload. Revise “worthless” to “not reliably worth retail price on resale.” Treat the two-months-salary convention as qualified color until a primary campaign asset is obtained.

- **[F-01]** South African mine discoveries in 1870 sharply increased diamond output, creating a glut risk for a gem whose price depended heavily on scarcity. — *Src:* [S-1] *The Atlantic*, Edward Jay Epstein, February 1982, https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1982/02/have-you-ever-tried-to-sell-a-diamond/304575/ · *Conf:* high · *Note:* “Intrinsic value” is Epstein’s analysis; this is not a claim that diamonds have no material or aesthetic value.
- **[F-02]** De Beers Consolidated Mines Limited was established on 12 March 1888 after Cecil Rhodes and Barney Barnato combined competing diamond operations. — *Src:* [S-2] De Beers Group, accessed 2026-07-30, https://www.debeersgroup.com/about-us/our-history · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Company historical record, consistent with [F-01].
- **[F-03]** De Beers says diamond demand stalled during the Depression and all of its mines closed in 1932. — *Src:* [S-2] De Beers Group, accessed 2026-07-30, https://www.debeersgroup.com/about-us/our-history · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Company statement; context for the later US campaign.
- **[F-04]** In September 1938, Harry Oppenheimer met N. W. Ayer president Gerold M. Lauck in New York to seek a new American image for diamonds after worldwide price declines. — *Src:* [S-1] *The Atlantic*, Edward Jay Epstein, February 1982, https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1982/02/have-you-ever-tried-to-sell-a-diamond/304575/ · *Conf:* med · *Note:* Reputable historical investigation; De Beers dates the first US campaign to 1939 [F-06], so separate 1938 planning from 1939 launch.
- **[F-05]** N. W. Ayer’s reported plan associated diamonds with romance through film stars, society coverage, fashion commentary, and advertising rather than direct brand advertising. — *Src:* [S-1] *The Atlantic*, Edward Jay Epstein, February 1982, https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1982/02/have-you-ever-tried-to-sell-a-diamond/304575/ · *Conf:* med · *Note:* Do not reduce this to “one ad”; it was a multi-year advertising and publicity program.
- **[F-06]** De Beers dates its first US advertising campaign to 1939. — *Src:* [S-2] De Beers Group, accessed 2026-07-30, https://www.debeersgroup.com/about-us/our-history · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Resolves the timeline as 1938 planning / 1939 public campaign.

Ayer copywriter, as the creator of “A Diamond Is Forever” in 1947. — *Src:* [S-2] De Beers Group, accessed 2026-07-30, https://www.debeersgroup.com/about-us/our-history · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Company primary history, independently consistent with [S-1].
- **[F-08]** Epstein reports that “A Diamond Is Forever” became De Beers’ official motto within a year of its creation. — *Src:* [S-1] *The Atlantic*, Edward Jay Epstein, February 1982, https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1982/02/have-you-ever-tried-to-sell-a-diamond/304575/ · *Conf:* med · *Note:* Do not claim the phrase alone invented the engagement-ring convention.
- **[F-09]** N. W. Ayer’s 1947 strategy, as reported by Epstein, described its task as strengthening the diamond engagement-ring tradition into a “psychological necessity.” — *Src:* [S-1] *The Atlantic*, Edward Jay Epstein, February 1982, https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1982/02/have-you-ever-tried-to-sell-a-diamond/304575/ · *Conf:* med · *Note:* Attribute the wording to the reported strategy document; it is evidence of demand-making, not an assertion about private thoughts.
- **[F-10]** Campaign Asia reports that De Beers later promoted a two-months-salary engagement-ring benchmark with the line “How else could two months’ salary last forever?” — *Src:* [S-11] *Campaign Asia*, 2022, https://www.campaignasia.com/article/a-look-back-to-when-de-beers-changed-the-rules-of-engagement/481261 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* Qualified later-campaign convention only; no primary original advertisement was retrieved.
- **[F-11]** An Oxford academic study describes the CSO as distributing rough diamonds through roughly 125 selected “sightholders,” with allocations ten times a year. — *Src:* [S-4] Ben-Shahar and Schneider, *Journal of Legal Analysis*, 2017, https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/9/2/247/4430792 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Independent account of the historical distribution mechanism.
- **[F-12]** The Oxford study says De Beers used the CSO to control how much rough diamond supply was sold each year. — *Src:* [S-4] Ben-Shahar and Schneider, *Journal of Legal Analysis*, 2017, https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/9/2/247/4430792 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Historical mechanism, not a conclusion about every later De Beers practice.
- **[F-13]** The Oxford study says that when supply threatened prices, De Beers acted as a buyer of last resort and stockpiled rough gems to support price stability. — *Src:* [S-4] Ben-Shahar and Schneider, *Journal of Legal Analysis*, 2017, https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/9/2/247/4430792 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Independently consistent with Epstein’s account of stockpile/distribution controls [S-1].
- **[F-14]** The Oxford study reports that De Beers and the CSO controlled more than 80% of diamond supply as recently as 1989. — *Src:* [S-4] Ben-Shahar and Schneider, *Journal of Legal Analysis*, 2017, https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/9/2/247/4430792 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* “More than 80%” refers to supply in this historical source; do not call it “all diamonds” or a current share.
- **[F-15]** A 2002 European Commission notice likewise states that De Beers held more than 80% of worldwide rough-diamond supply for most of the twentieth century and used CSO sales arrangements. — *Src:* [S-5] European Commission, 2002, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:52002XC1109(01) · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Official independent corroboration for [F-14]; share definitions and years vary, so use only “more than 80% of rough supply.”
- **[F-16]** By the late 1990s, the Oxford study says De Beers’ rough-gem inventory grew from under $3 billion to nearly $5 billion as the old stockpiling strategy came under pressure. — *Src:* [S-4] Ben-Shahar and Schneider, *Journal of Legal Analysis*, 2017, https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/9/2/247/4430792 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Historical nominal dollars, not adjusted for inflation.
- **[F-17]** In 2000, De Beers announced it would sell off stockpiles and move into retail, according to the Oxford study. — *Src:* [S-4] Ben-Shahar and Schneider, *Journal of Legal Analysis*, 2017, https://academic.oup.com/jla/article/9/2/247/4430792 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Exonerating timeline: the historic stockpile model changed; do not narrate it as unchanged in 2026.
- **[F-18]** A consumer-magazine experiment reported by Epstein bought a 1.4-carat diamond for £2,595 in 1974 and received a highest offer of £1,000 one week later. — *Src:* [S-1] *The Atlantic*, Edward Jay Epstein, February 1982, https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1982/02/have-you-ever-tried-to-sell-a-diamond/304575/ · *Conf:* med · *Note:* Dated illustrative resale test—not a universal percentage or a 2026 quote.

## Cast, motive & human-cost  ← the story fuel a fact-ledger prunes; all sourced
- **Villain / protagonist:** The historical protagonist is De Beers’ supply-and-demand system: selective CSO distribution and stockpiles managed rough supply [F-11]–[F-17], while the reported Ayer strategy sought to make an engagement ring psychologically necessary [F-09]. The defensible motive is documented price stability/demand construction—not private intent or current wrongdoing.
- **Human cost (light, aggregate):** The sourced harm is resale mismatch, not an invented list of victims: the dated 1974 test in [F-18] shows a retail consumer offered far less one week later, and [F-19] supplies the historical markup mechanism. Do not claim a universal loss rate, people “ruined,” or investment advice.
- **Witnessed-absurd / telling details (2-3):** Rough boxes went to about 125 selected buyers ten times yearly [F-11]; the “forever” object was historically supported by stones deliberately kept off-market [F-13]; and the salary slogan turns romance into payroll arithmetic [F-10]. **Humor hooks:** high—“a luxury market with a bouncer list,” “forever, subject to inventory management,” and “romance got an HR policy”—provided the joke rides on the fact and never targets couples.

## Verification chain (vindication lever — how the case is PROVEN)
- **What is proven / uncontested:** Ayer demand-making is documented in the historical reporting [F-04]–[F-09]; the CSO rationed supply and used stockpiles [F-11]–[F-17]; the 2004 industrial-diamond plea is a primary DOJ record [F-20], [F-21]. **What stays soft / contested:** “the most successful con in history,” a one-ad causal explanation, any universal present resale percentage, and the exact two-months-salary provenance [F-10], [F-18], [F-19].
- **Corroboration:** Campaign author/date align between De Beers history [S-2] and Epstein [S-1]; CSO power aligns between Oxford scholarship [S-4] and the European Commission [S-5]. The DOJ record [S-3] is primary but proves only the industrial case stated there.
- **Exoneration:** The record does **not** prove a present-day retailer, every producer, or everyone who values a diamond is committing fraud. The historical stockpile model changed around 2000 [F-17], and the 2004 plea was for industrial products—not consumer engagement rings [F-20], [F-21].

## Myths / misconceptions to bust
- **Myth:** “Diamonds are literally worthless.” → **Actually:** Their resale quote can be far below retail, but they retain physical, cutting, setting, brand, and emotional value; the provable payload is the historic price-making system and resale gap, not literal zero value ([F-18], [F-19], [F-22]). — strong vindication payload.
- **Myth:** “One ad invented everything overnight.” → **Actually:** The evidence describes planning in 1938, a US campaign in 1939, and continued publicity; Gerety’s 1947 line became its durable symbol ([F-04]–[F-09]).
- **Myth:** “The 2004 guilty plea proves consumer engagement-ring price fixing.” → **Actually:** DOJ records a guilty plea about specified industrial diamond products in 1991–92 ([F-20], [F-21]).
- **Myth:** “Natural and lab-grown diamonds are different chemicals.” → **Actually:** They are chemically identical carbon crystals; provenance, supply, and pricing differ ([F-22]–[F-26]).

## Open questions, gaps & hedges
- No primary archival De Beers/N. W. Ayer advertisement or campaign memo proving the exact move from one to two months’ salary was retrieved. [F-10] is qualified color, not a load-bearing numerical origin story.
- No high-quality standardized 2026 retail-to-resale data set was found. Do not write “you lose X%” or imply every stone resells alike; use the dated example [F-18] and historical mechanism [F-19].
- “Peak market share” varies by rough supply, production, value, volume, and year. The safe wording is “more than 80% of worldwide rough-diamond supply historically / in 1989,” never “90% of all diamonds.”
- Blood-diamond history is not covered beyond the limited 2003 Kimberley Process context [F-27]. A primary/official/NGO pass is required before discussing effectiveness, a current mine, or allegations.
- Lab-grown price direction is corroborated through 2024 reporting, but exact discounts are source-specific and dated. No 2026 retail forecast or purchase advice belongs in the script.

- **Mechanism, escalation, relevance:** **Verified:** demand construction [F-04]–[F-09] + CSO allocations/stockpiles [F-11]–[F-17] + concrete resale mismatch [F-18], [F-19] + dated lab-grown turn [F-22]–[F-26].
