For most of the twentieth century, one diamond system controlled more than 80 percent of the world's rough supply. Then it helped convince millions of people that the finished stone was a permanent symbol of love. Permanent as in forever. Permanent as in please, for the love of romance, never put this thing back on the market.

So how do you build a luxury market where one group manages the supply and the customers volunteer not to resell the product? This is entertainment, not financial advice, by the way. If your proposal is tonight, please don't send me the hotel bill. But the answer starts in South Africa in 1870, when new mines began producing so many diamonds that the market had a very awkward problem: too much treasure.

Diamonds weren't fake, and they weren't worthless. They were stones whose luxury price depended heavily on scarcity, right when miners had found a whole lot more of them. So in 1888, Cecil Rhodes and Barney Barnato combined competing operations into De Beers. The company grew into the center of a system that handled more than 80 percent of the world's rough diamonds, meaning stones before they were cut and polished, for most of the twentieth century. That's not a jewelry company. That's the nightclub, the bouncer, and most of the guest list.

Then the Depression hit, demand stalled, and by 1932 every De Beers mine was closed. Six years later, with prices falling around the world, Harry Oppenheimer went to New York to meet an advertising agency called N. W. Ayer. He didn't need them to improve the diamond. He needed them to improve what Americans felt when they looked at one.

So in 1938, Oppenheimer hired Ayer to launch a campaign that attached diamonds to romance. The agency went after film stars, society pages, fashion writers, and the general human fear that everybody else knows the correct way to get engaged. This wasn't a regular ad saying buy our brand. It was Hollywood and Madison Avenue quietly writing a dress code for love.

That distinction was the whole game. A brand ad has to keep reminding you which company to choose, but a social rule gets repeated by parents, friends, movies, jewelers, and anybody asking when the wedding is. The campaign wasn't trying to win one shopping trip. It was trying to decide what the shopping trip meant before the couple entered the store.

The campaign put diamonds around movie stars, into society coverage, and beside fashion advice. The product kept showing up wherever Americans went to learn what glamorous people wore and what respectable couples did. De Beers wasn't just selling a stone anymore. It was getting the culture to do product placement before product placement had a name.

By 1947, Ayer's own strategy described the job as turning the diamond engagement ring into a psychological necessity. That same year, a copywriter named Frances Gerety wrote four words: "A Diamond Is Forever." Within a year, it became De Beers' official motto. Hallmark had found a vault.

And forever was perfect, because forever doesn't come with a used section. The line made the diamond sound like love itself: permanent, personal, and a little insulting to resell. A later campaign even pushed a two months' salary benchmark with the line, "How else could two months' salary last forever?" Romance had an HR policy now.

Notice what just happened. A geological material had picked up a social rule, a spending rule, and an exit rule. You needed the ring, the ring needed to cost enough to look serious, and selling it made the whole gesture feel less serious. Nobody changed the crystal. They changed the consequences of saying no.

So how did all that advertising help hold up actual prices? This is where the Central Selling Organisation came in, basically the velvet rope between diamond producers and the dealers who could buy rough stones. It distributed boxes to about 125 chosen buyers called sightholders. The rough-diamond business had a Met Gala guest list.

Those sightholders received allocations ten times a year. The point wasn't to open a giant diamond bazaar and let every seller dump whatever it had. The point was to control how much rough supply was sold each year. Even the calendar had a bouncer.

And when too many diamonds threatened prices, De Beers could buy the excess and keep it in a stockpile instead of selling it. Most businesses respond to extra inventory by putting up a SALE sign. This system responded by finding a larger vault. It was Netflix running backward: when the audience wanted more, some of the catalog stayed unavailable.

Put the two halves together and the machine was beautiful. Advertising told customers a diamond was necessary and forever. Distribution decided how many rough stones reached the trade. One side made people want the product, and the other side made sure the market never looked like a clearance aisle. Hallmark with a vault.

By the late 1990s, though, that vault was getting crowded. De Beers' rough-diamond inventory had grown from under 3 billion dollars to nearly 5 billion as the old stockpiling strategy came under pressure. Five billion dollars of forever sitting around waiting for later. The romance department had an inventory problem.

And then there was the small issue of what happened when a customer ignored the word forever. In 1974, a consumer magazine bought a 1.4-carat diamond for 2,595 pounds. One week later, the highest offer it received was 1,000 pounds. The stone hadn't changed in seven days. The honeymoon lasted longer than the resale value.

That test doesn't mean every diamond loses the same amount, because it was one stone in 1974. But it does show the gap between a retail price and what a trade buyer would pay to take the stone back. Historical jewelers used markups of 100 to 200 percent on the diamond and setting, so the checkout price included a whole retail machine the second buyer wasn't paying for. You bought the lighting, the box, and the person saying excellent choice. The next dealer just saw inventory.

By 2000, De Beers announced that it would sell off stockpiles and move into retail. The old buyer-of-last-resort strategy was being retired, which matters because this isn't a story about the company running the exact same machine today. The vault was opening. The slogan, however, had already escaped into the culture.

Then lab-grown diamonds showed up and made the old scarcity story even stranger. Mined and lab-grown stones are exactly the same carbon crystal, though a laboratory can tell them apart through tiny structural signatures. Producers can make the lab stones in chosen sizes and qualities, and falling production costs pushed their prices down. Same sparkle, different birth certificate.

By 2024, lab-grown diamonds were selling at about an 80 percent discount to comparable natural stones. Their average price had fallen while their average size got bigger, which is usually not how luxury is supposed to behave. It was as if the champagne bottles kept growing while the menu price fell. Somewhere, the velvet rope was having a terrible year.

The numbers made it even sillier. In one set of industry data, the average lab-grown diamond fell from almost 3,900 dollars in 2020 to about 2,700 dollars in 2024. Over the same stretch, the average size rose from 1.2 carats to 1.9. Customers were getting more diamond and less invoice. Luxury had accidentally discovered Costco.

None of that makes a natural diamond fake. It can still carry the cutting, the setting, the brand, and whatever a couple decided the moment meant to them. But the lab-grown turn makes the old machine easier to see: a diamond's price was never just the carbon. It was the controlled supply, the retail channel, and a story powerful enough to make payroll sound romantic.

So De Beers changed its stockpile strategy, lab-made stones got bigger and cheaper, and the engagement ring stayed exactly where the campaign wanted it: out of the resale case. The salary benchmark gave you a number to spend. Forever made sure you never asked what number you could get back. Pretty convenient.
