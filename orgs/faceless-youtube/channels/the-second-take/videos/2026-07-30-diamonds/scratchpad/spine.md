# ST-013 story, plot, and locked spine

## The story

One system solved a luxury market's two opposite problems at the same time. De Beers and its distribution system kept excess rough diamonds from flooding the market, while N. W. Ayer made the finished stone feel like a compulsory proof of love that should never come back for resale. The story earns its place through the 1870 output shock, the Depression corner, the 1938 decision to attach diamonds to romance, the long campaign and 1947 slogan, the selective distribution and stockpile machine, one brutal resale test, and the lab-grown turn that exposes how much of the price rests on scarcity and meaning.

## The plot

Shape: paradox cold open, then a hook-tease chronology that stages the demand-making act before revealing the supply mechanism. The hook spoils the contradiction, more than 80 percent of rough supply under one historical system and a message telling buyers never to sell, but withholds how the two halves reinforced each other. The first third establishes the output shock, De Beers' rise, and historical scale once. The middle stages the advertising act in full, then answers the viewer's question with the CSO allocations and stockpiles. The final turn uses the resale test and lab-grown stones to expose the promise's limits, then settles the old model's fate and stops on the salary rule's last laugh.

Retention arc: the opening owes one answer, how a system could control supply and discourage resale at the same time. The 1938 New York meeting opens the human decision loop. Frances Gerety and the 1947 line pay off the cultural act, but the mechanism remains withheld until the campaign has landed. The resale test re-arms the middle with a new question, what happens when somebody ignores "forever" and tries to sell. The lab-grown turn is the withheld modern fine print.

Humor register: HOT on payroll arithmetic, the luxury-market bouncer list, and romance backed by inventory management. Quieter on consumer loss. Candidate pulls: Hollywood writing the social script, Hallmark with a vault, a Met Gala guest list for rough-diamond buyers, Netflix running scarcity backward, and a birth certificate as the visible difference between mined and lab-grown carbon crystals. No joke targets couples or treats emotional value as fake.

## Staged title-scheme card

1. Pressure: new South African mines sharply increase output, threatening the scarcity a luxury price needs.
2. The corner: demand stalls, prices fall, and De Beers says all its mines close in 1932.
3. The decision as a moment: Harry Oppenheimer goes to New York in 1938 and asks N. W. Ayer to give diamonds a new American image.
4. The act with audacious detail: Ayer places diamonds around film stars, society coverage, fashion, and romance; Gerety supplies the 1947 line; a later campaign turns love into two months of payroll.
5. How did nobody notice: the campaign does not merely sell one brand; it makes the purchase look like a public rule, and the rule says the stone should never return to market.
6. Mechanism as punchline: selected sightholders receive rough stones on a schedule, while excess stones wait in stockpiles instead of being released.
7. Escalation: more than 80 percent of historical rough supply, a stockpile nearing 5 billion dollars, a resale offer far below retail, then chemically identical lab-grown stones selling at a widening discount.

## Locked spine

1. Open on the paradox: one historical system handles more than 80 percent of rough diamonds and persuades buyers that selling the finished stone is almost sacrilegious. Facts on hand: historical worldwide rough-supply share and the demand strategy. Carries the "Hallmark with a vault" angle.
2. Drop back to the 1870 South African discoveries, where a flood of output threatens the scarcity story. Facts on hand: sharp output increase and glut risk. Carries a plain supply problem, no mechanism explanation yet.
3. Tell the climb once: competing operations combine into De Beers in 1888, and the De Beers/CSO system later handles more than 80 percent of worldwide rough supply for much of the twentieth century. Facts on hand: formation date and corroborated historical share. Carries the scale inside the first third.
4. Put the company in its corner: Depression demand stalls, mines close, and falling prices send Harry Oppenheimer to N. W. Ayer in New York. Facts on hand: 1932 closures, 1938 meeting, worldwide price declines. Carries the human decision loop.
5. Stage the advertising decision: change what diamonds mean by attaching them to romance rather than changing the crystal. Facts on hand: Ayer plan and motive. Carries the Hollywood social-script pull.
6. Stage the act: film stars, society pages, fashion publicity, the 1939 campaign, Gerety's 1947 line, and the strategy's "psychological necessity." Facts on hand: campaign channels, dates, creator, strategy language. Carries the Hallmark and wedding-rule jokes.
7. Escalate the act with the later salary benchmark, stated as a later campaign move rather than an origin myth. Facts on hand: two-months-salary promotion and exact campaign line. Carries "romance got an HR policy."
8. Ask how the price held, then reveal the mechanism after the act: roughly 125 selected sightholders, ten allocations per year, and excess rough stones kept in stockpiles. Facts on hand: distribution schedule, supply control, buyer-of-last-resort role. Carries the Met Gala guest-list and Netflix-backward pulls.
9. Show the machine at maximum strain: the rough inventory grows to nearly 5 billion dollars by the late 1990s. Facts on hand: historical nominal inventory and pressure on the strategy. Carries "forever, subject to inventory management."
10. Re-arm with the resale test: a magazine buys a 1.4-carat stone in 1974 and gets an offer of 1,000 pounds one week after paying 2,595 pounds; historical retail markup helps explain the gap. Facts on hand: dated test and historical markup. Carries the one-way-door joke without universalizing the loss.
11. Deliver the modern fine print: mined and lab-grown diamonds are chemically identical carbon crystals, labs can tell them apart, and by 2024 the lab-grown discount reaches about 80 percent as production costs and prices fall. Facts on hand: material identity, detectability, price direction, dated discount. Carries the "same crystal, different birth certificate" pull.
12. Settle the fates and stop casually: in 2000 De Beers moves away from the old stockpile model and into retail, but the forever slogan and salary arithmetic outlive that machine. Facts on hand: strategy change and the campaign legacy. Last laugh: a rule built to keep diamonds out of the resale case still fits perfectly in a jewelry drawer.
