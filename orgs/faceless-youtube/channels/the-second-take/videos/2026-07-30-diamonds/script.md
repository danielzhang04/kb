# How Diamonds Became Forever

Source idea: ST-013
Target length: 7:30-9:30
Voice: Chris
Estimated runtime: 8:20 (1,424 words ÷ 171 wpm)

---

For most of the twentieth century, De Beers controlled more than 80 percent of the world's rough-diamond supply. Then it helped convince people that the finished stone was a permanent symbol of love. Permanent as in forever. Permanent as in please, for the love of romance, never put this thing back on the market.

So how do you build a luxury market where one group manages rough supply while advertising ties diamonds to romance? This is entertainment, not financial advice, by the way. If your proposal is tonight, please don't send me the hotel bill. But the answer starts in South Africa in 1870, when new mines began producing so many diamonds that the market had a very awkward problem: too much treasure.

Diamonds weren't fake, and they weren't worthless. They were stones whose luxury price depended heavily on scarcity, right when miners had found a whole lot more of them. So in 1888, Cecil Rhodes and Barney Barnato combined competing operations into De Beers. The company became the center of a system that controlled more than 80 percent of worldwide rough-diamond supply, meaning stones before they were cut and polished, for most of the twentieth century. That's not a jewelry company. That's the nightclub, the bouncer, and most of the guest list.

Then the Depression hit, demand stalled, and by 1932 all of De Beers' mines were closed. Six years later, with prices falling around the world, Harry Oppenheimer went to New York for De Beers to meet an advertising agency called N. W. Ayer. He didn't need them to improve the diamond. He needed them to improve what Americans felt when they looked at one.

So in 1938, Oppenheimer asked Ayer for a campaign that attached diamonds to romance. The public campaign launched in 1939 and ran through years of advertising and publicity, not one ad flipping a switch and inventing engagement rings in an afternoon. The agency used film stars, society coverage, fashion commentary, and advertising to associate diamonds with romance. A regular ad tells you which brand to buy. Hollywood and Madison Avenue were quietly writing a dress code for love.

That was the whole game. A brand ad has to keep reminding you which company to choose, but a social rule gets repeated by parents, friends, movies, jewelers, and anybody asking when the wedding is. Winning the shopping trip was the smaller job. The campaign wanted to decide what the shopping trip meant before the couple entered the store.

The campaign put diamonds around movie stars, into society coverage, and beside fashion advice. The product kept showing up wherever Americans went to learn what glamorous people wore and what respectable couples did. De Beers wasn't just selling a stone anymore. It was getting the culture to do product placement.

By 1947, Ayer's strategy described the job as strengthening the diamond engagement-ring tradition into a psychological necessity. That same year, a copywriter named Frances Gerety wrote four words: "A Diamond Is Forever." Within a year, it became De Beers' official motto. Hallmark had found a vault.

And forever was perfect, because forever doesn't come with a used section. The line made the diamond sound permanent and personal. A later campaign even pushed a two months' salary benchmark with the line, "How else could two months' salary last forever?" Romance had an HR policy now.

The campaign tied diamonds to romance. De Beers also managed rough supply. So how did that part work? The Central Selling Organisation, or CSO, sat between producers and the dealers selected to buy rough stones. It sent boxes to about 125 chosen buyers called sightholders. The rough-diamond business had a Met Gala guest list, and the box only moved if your name was on it.

Getting on the list did not mean shopping whenever you wanted. The sightholders received allocations ten times a year, on the CSO's calendar. Those scheduled sales let De Beers control how much rough supply was sold annually. First the velvet rope decided who got a box. Then the calendar decided when the boxes moved.

Each allocation was a cache of rough stones, not a finished-ring order. The sightholders were buying material before it became the polished product behind glass. So De Beers' supply control happened far from the proposal, where rough stones entered the trade. The boxes moved first; jewelers and customers came later.

When extra supply threatened prices, De Beers stepped in as buyer of last resort. It bought the excess rough gems and held them in a stockpile instead of selling them. Most businesses see inventory pile up and reach for a SALE sign. De Beers reached for a larger vault. It was Netflix running backward: when the catalog got too big, part of it disappeared from the market.

By the late 1990s, the buyer of last resort owned a very large pile. De Beers' rough-gem inventory had grown from under 3 billion dollars to nearly 5 billion as the old stockpiling strategy came under pressure. These were rough gems sitting in company inventory, not finished rings behind store glass. The romance department had acquired a warehouse, and De Beers was still holding the stones.

And then there was the small issue of what happened when a customer ignored the word forever. In 1974, a consumer magazine bought a 1.4-carat diamond for 2,595 pounds. One week later, the highest offer it received was 1,000 pounds. The stone hadn't changed in seven days. The honeymoon lasted longer than the resale value.

That test put the customer through two different doors. On the way in, historical jewelers marked up the diamond and setting enough to at least double the price. On the way out, a trade buyer quoted what it would pay to take the stone back. The same diamond crossed from retail to trade in one week.

By 2000, De Beers announced two changes: it would sell off stockpiles, and it would move into retail. One decision started releasing the inventory the old model had accumulated. The other moved the company toward the customer-facing end of the business. The buyer-of-last-resort strategy was being retired, which matters because this isn't a story about the company running the exact same machine today. De Beers was opening the vault and changing where it sold.

In a separate industrial-products case in 2004, a company called De Beers Centenary pleaded guilty in the United States to conspiring to fix prices for certain industrial diamond products. It paid a criminal fine. Bring that plea to an engagement-ring argument and you've got the wrong case file. Consumer gemstones and engagement rings were outside the case.

The fine was 10 million dollars. De Beers Centenary admitted it agreed to raise list prices worldwide for those products. The agreements covered 1991 and 1992.

Then lab-grown diamonds showed up and made the old scarcity story even stranger. Mined and lab-grown stones are chemically identical carbon crystals, though a laboratory can tell them apart through structural signatures. Producers can make the lab stones in chosen sizes and qualities, and falling production costs pushed their prices down. Same sparkle, different birth certificate.

In 2018, lab-grown diamonds sold at about a 20 percent discount to comparable natural stones. Both stones still had the same carbon-crystal chemistry, but the price gap was about to open like the Red Sea.

After that discount snapshot, lab-grown prices kept falling. De Beers reported that wholesale and retail prices fell throughout 2024, and the drop sped up in the second half.

By 2024, lab-grown diamonds were selling at about an 80 percent discount to comparable natural stones. Their average price had fallen while their average size got bigger. The champagne bottles were getting bigger while the menu price fell. Somewhere, the velvet rope was having a terrible year.

Axios's industry numbers showed the size and price moving in opposite directions. In the earlier data Axios cited, the average lab-grown diamond was smaller. By the later snapshot, the average had grown to 1.9 carats. Bigger rock. The average price moved the other way from a higher earlier figure, falling to 2,657 dollars in the later snapshot.

None of that makes a natural diamond fake. It can still carry the cutting, the setting, the brand, and whatever a couple decided the moment meant to them. But lab-grown diamonds make the old machine easier to see. Carbon was never the whole price. Controlled supply, the retail channel, and a story powerful enough to make payroll sound romantic made up the rest.

De Beers opened the vault. Luxury had accidentally discovered Costco.
