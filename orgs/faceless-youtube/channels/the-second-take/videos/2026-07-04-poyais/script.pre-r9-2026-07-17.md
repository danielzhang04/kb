[B-ROLL: families staking everything on a promised paradise across the ocean]
In 1822, a few hundred people sold everything they owned, packed up their families, and sailed across the Atlantic to go live in a country called Poyais. They'd been told Poyais was a gorgeous young country in Central America, with a capital city, a national bank, its own money, a cathedral, and a prince. There was just one problem. [BEAT] The whole thing was made up. And more than half of the people who sailed for Poyais never came home.

[B-ROLL: the one man behind all of it]
So what happened? It all started with a soldier named Gregor MacGregor.

[B-ROLL: the real war hero, the kernel of truth the con was built on]
MacGregor was a Scottish war hero who had fought his way across Europe and then South America, and earned the rank of general in the wars of independence, fighting alongside Simón Bolívar, the man who freed half the continent. And in 1820, a local king really did give him land. Eight million acres of it, in exchange for some rum and some jewellery.

[B-ROLL: worthless swamp being mentally redrawn as a kingdom]
There was just one problem with the eight million acres. It was empty jungle. There was nothing there. No towns, no farms, nobody. MacGregor probably took one look at that worthless swamp and figured no one in London was ever going to cross an ocean to check. So instead of selling the land, he invented a country to sit on top of it, and made himself the Cazique, the prince of Poyais.

[B-ROLL: a man pinning on himself titles he never earned]
And making things up was nothing new for him. As a young officer, MacGregor was obsessed with uniforms and medals and titles. He was so into it that the men under him supposedly weren't allowed to leave the barracks unless they were in full dress. By the time he got back to Britain, he'd quietly promoted himself. He started signing his name Sir Gregor MacGregor, a knight and a clan chief, none of it real, riding around town in a flashy coach with a foreign medal pinned to his chest.

[B-ROLL: a lavish guidebook to a country that was never built]
So, to sell his country, MacGregor commissioned a Captain Thomas Strangeways to write a three-hundred-and-fifty-page book, a traveller's guide to Poyais. The book described a gorgeous new capital city called St Joseph, with grand houses, an opera house, a cathedral, and twenty thousand people already living there. The soil was so rich a family could feed itself off a single day's work, and the rivers ran with pure gold you could wash straight out of the sand. The ultimate five-star experience. Captain Strangeways, though, like Poyais itself, didn't exist. He was invented by MacGregor and his people to sell eight million acres of swamp.

[B-ROLL: the machinery of the sell: fake money and cheap land]
Then MacGregor used the same printer the Bank of Scotland used to run off completely legit-looking Poyais currency. He opened offices across Europe, selling deeds to Poyais farmland for about two shillings an acre, roughly a day's wages, for one acre of a country that wasn't there. And the more the offices filled up, the higher MacGregor pushed the price, because nothing sells faster than watching other people scramble to buy in. By early 1823, around five hundred people had signed up.

[B-ROLL: hopeful emigrants boarding, their savings already converted]
In September 1822, the first ship, the Honduras Packet, set sail from London with about seventy settlers aboard, doctors, lawyers, a banker, whole families, all bound for their new lives under the green cross flag of Poyais. Did I mention he'd made a flag? A second ship, the Kennersley Castle, followed a few months later out of Scotland, carrying almost two hundred more. And before any of them left, they'd all been told to change their savings into Poyais dollars, so they'd have local money when they arrived. So MacGregor had turned their real, hard-earned savings into his own homemade currency before the ships had even left the dock.

[B-ROLL: a fake nation's government debt landing on a real trading floor]
In the meantime, MacGregor was back in London, planning his next move. He borrowed money in the name of the Poyais government. He issued official government bonds, worth around two hundred thousand pounds, and sold them through a respectable bank, exactly how a real country would raise money abroad. Government debt, for a government that was one guy in a rented office. And it sold. It traded on the floor of the actual London Stock Exchange, right next to the bonds of real countries like Colombia and Peru.

[B-ROLL: a market too euphoric to look closely at what it's buying]
So how does the debt of a country that doesn't exist end up trading next to real ones? Well, MacGregor picked the perfect moment. In 1822, London had completely lost its mind over exactly this kind of thing. Spain's colonies in South America were breaking away, and all at once a whole run of brand-new countries, Colombia, Peru, Chile, showed up in London selling bonds for the first time. Everybody wanted in. Money poured in faster than anyone could check where it was going. It was the dot-com bubble of the 1820s, with everyone throwing cash at the shiny new thing with a good story. MacGregor didn't have to outsmart anyone. He just had to look like one more exciting new country in a year that was filled with them.

[B-ROLL: the bubble bursting, the paper going worthless]
The bond kept selling for months, until the new-country boom finally cracked. The new Latin American bonds collapsed altogether, and Poyais went down with them, worth almost nothing by the end of 1823.

[B-ROLL: the brutal gap between the brochure and the beach]
But by that time, two ships full of settlers had already crossed the Atlantic and stepped off onto the shore of the five-star country they'd paid for. And of course, when they landed, there was nothing there. No city, no port, no cathedral. Just an empty beach and eight million acres of jungle. So they set up camp and waited, politely, for the Poyais government to come and welcome them into their new homes. The only official who ever showed up was the local king, who took one look at the stranded Europeans and cancelled MacGregor's grant on the spot.

[B-ROLL: the human cost, quiet and cold, no music]
Then the rainy season came, and disease came with it. No shelter, no supplies, no help coming, and people started to die. In one camp alone, seven men and three children were dead before the spring was out. One settler, an Edinburgh cobbler who'd been handed the grand title of Official Shoemaker to the Princess of Poyais, walked down to the beach and shot himself.

[B-ROLL: the few who made it back, and what it cost them]
So how did any of them make it home? Eventually, word of the disaster reached the outside world, and the survivors who could still travel were taken off the coast and put on ships back to Britain. But most of them were half-dead already, and plenty more died on the way. Of the roughly two hundred and fifty people who had sailed for Poyais, fewer than fifty ever came home.

[B-ROLL: the con's eerie hold, still gripping its own victims]
And when the survivors finally got home, they didn't come after MacGregor. In October 1823, six of them, including Hastie, signed a sworn statement blaming the officer MacGregor had left in charge on the ground, and insisting MacGregor himself had been treated worse than any man alive. Even after Poyais had cost them nearly everything, some of them still refused to believe the prince had meant them any harm.

[B-ROLL: the architect slipping quietly out of the country, unbothered]
But by the time they got home to sign it, MacGregor was already long gone. After the news broke in London, he'd slipped out of the country, telling people he was taking his wife to Italy for her health. He went to Paris instead. And in Paris, with a completely straight face, he tried to sell Poyais all over again, to French investors this time, with a fresh stack of documents and a brand-new loan. The French arrested him and put him on trial for fraud. And they let him walk. He was never convicted over Poyais, not in France, not in Britain.

[B-ROLL: the paper outlasting the people it ruined]
He kept running Poyais schemes for another decade. Then he moved to Venezuela, where his old war record still counted for something, and they welcomed him back as a hero and handed him back his rank of general. When he died in 1845, they buried him with full military honours, in a cathedral. The people who believed in Poyais are mostly gone, buried on a coast that never had their town on it. But the guidebook is still here. It sits in libraries to this day, and anyone can go and read about the beautiful country that never existed.

[B-ROLL: end card, held on the closing line]
Thanks for watching. And maybe double-check before you buy a country.
