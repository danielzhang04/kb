## Fact ledger  ← the scriptwriter may state ONLY what appears here
<!-- Atomic, sourced, dated, confidence-rated. IDs never reused. NOTHING the video says may lack an entry. -->

- **[F-01]** Gregor MacGregor (born 24 December 1786, Stirlingshire, Scotland; died 4 December 1845, Caracas) was a Scottish soldier and adventurer with a genuine military career, first in the British Army (ensign 1803, captain by 1805, service in Gibraltar and the Peninsular War under Wellington), then as a general in the South American wars of independence under Francisco de Miranda and Simón Bolívar. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the real military record is load-bearing. It is why he was credible.
- **[F-02]** On 29 April 1820, MacGregor obtained a genuine land grant of about 8 million acres (an area larger than Wales) on the Mosquito Coast (modern Honduras/Nicaragua) from King George Frederic Augustus of the Mosquito nation, reportedly in exchange for rum and jewellery. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the "kernel of truth." The grant was real but the land was undeveloped, uninhabitable jungle.
- **[F-03]** Returning to London around mid-1821, MacGregor styled himself "Cazique (Prince) of Poyais" and presented Poyais as a settled, civilized nation with a government, not a wilderness. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-04]** The illusion was documented in a roughly 355-page guidebook, *Sketch of the Mosquito Shore, including the Territory of Poyais* (Edinburgh and London, 1822), published under the pseudonym "Captain Thomas Strangeways," a fictitious aide-de-camp to the Cazique; it was produced by MacGregor himself or his accomplices. — *Src:* [S1],[S3] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-05]** In the guidebook, Poyais's fictional capital St Joseph is described as a flourishing town of wide paved boulevards, colonnaded buildings and mansions, with as many as 20,000 inhabitants, a theatre, an opera house, a domed cathedral, the Bank of Poyais, houses of parliament, and a royal palace. — *Src:* [S1],[S3] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* none of it existed; the site was empty jungle with only long-forgotten colonial foundations and rubble.
- **[F-06]** The guidebook claimed the rivers of Poyais contained "globules of pure gold" that could be washed from the sands. — *Src:* [S1],[S3] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* primary-document claim (the guidebook's own boast); the single most vivid promise of the con. Report as *what the book said*, not asserted true.
- **[F-07]** The guidebook promoted Poyais as having a healthy climate for Europeans and land so fertile and game-rich that a settler could take multiple crops a year and feed a family easily, presenting the territory as free of tropical disease. — *Src:* [S1],[S2],[S3] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the classic guidebook pitch as reproduced across the standard accounts; the deadly irony reads against the disease death toll [F-16]. The specific "three maize harvests a year / a day's hunting feeds a family for a week" wording is widely quoted from the guidebook but was not re-verified against the primary page this pass, so hedge the exact phrasing.
- **[F-08]** MacGregor's operation had 5,000 Bank of Poyais dollar notes printed by the Bank of Scotland's official printer, and settlers were encouraged to exchange their real British gold and savings for this Poyais currency before departing. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the sharpest single "the con took real savings for worthless paper" beat; the real printer's involvement is what made the notes look legitimate.
- **[F-09]** MacGregor opened land/emigration offices selling Poyais land certificates at about 2 shillings 3 pence per acre (roughly a working man's daily wage), raised to 2 shillings 6 pence in July 1822 and gradually up toward 4 shillings per acre as "demand" grew; about 500 people had bought Poyais land by early 1823. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* deliberate scarcity/escalation pricing.
- **[F-10]** MacGregor floated a Poyais government loan on the London market in October 1822: a £200,000 issue underwritten by the reputable London banking house Sir John Perring, Shaw, Barber & Co., in bonds of £100, £200 and £500, offered at a marked-down 80% of face value and paying 6% per annum; the bonds were offered on 23 October 1822. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the £200,000 London issue; a separate, later £300,000 "loan" belongs to the 1825 French scheme, see [Open Q2].
- **[F-11]** The Poyais bonds traded on the real London Stock Exchange alongside genuine new Latin-American sovereign debt (Colombia had floated a £2 million loan in March 1822; Peru and Chile also issued), all paying about 6% against 3% on British consols, so the market treated a fictional country's paper like the real thing. — *Src:* [S1],[S4] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the core "how did this clear?" payload.
- **[F-12]** The scheme rode the 1822 London mania for Latin-American sovereign bonds; when that boom turned to a panic in late 1822 and 1823, Poyais securities collapsed to less than 10% of their face value by late 1823, alongside real Latin-American bonds that also fell or defaulted. — *Src:* [S1],[S4] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the vindication core. The mania, not just MacGregor's charm, made a fake bond look investable.
- **[F-13]** The first emigrant ship, the *Honduras Packet*, departed London on 10 September 1822 with about 70 settlers (including doctors, lawyers and a banker), led by Hector Hall. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-14]** The second emigrant ship, the *Kennersley Castle*, was hired at Leith (near Edinburgh) in October 1822 and sailed from Leith on 22 January 1823 with almost 200 emigrants. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* correction to some retellings that place its departure in October 1822; October 1822 is when it was hired, January 1823 when it sailed.
- **[F-15]** Roughly 250 settlers sailed in total on the two ships; on arrival at the Black River they found no city and no development, only empty coast, untouched jungle, and long-forgotten colonial foundations and rubble, with no Poyaisian authorities to meet them. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-16]** Of the roughly 250 who sailed on the *Honduras Packet* and *Kennersley Castle*, at least 180 perished (disease, exposure, malnutrition on the Honduran coast or during the journey home) and fewer than 50 ever returned to Britain. — *Src:* [S1],[S5] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the featured account states "at least 180 had perished; fewer than 50 ever returned"; it remains an estimate, so script it as "more than half never came home." See [Open Q1].
- **[F-17]** By early May 1823, before most survivors were evacuated, seven men and three children had already died at the Black River camp. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-18]** The settlers set up camp on the shore assuming the Poyaisian authorities would soon contact them and sent search parties inland; one party, guided by locals who recognised the name St Joseph, returned having found only long-forgotten foundations and rubble. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* they waited for a government that did not exist.
- **[F-19]** King George Frederic Augustus, the real Mosquito king, arrived at the Black River settlement around late April/early May 1823 and revoked the grant on the spot, declaring he had never made MacGregor a "Cazique" nor given him any right to sell land or raise loans against it, and telling the settlers they were there illegally. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the on-the-record fact that there was no sovereign authority behind Poyais.
- **[F-20]** MacGregor commissioned Hector Hall, an ex-British-Army officer, as a Poyais lieutenant-colonel titled "Baron Tinto" (with a supposed 12,800-acre estate) to lead the settlers on the ground, and installed a City of London banker named Mauger as head of the Bank of Poyais, handing him the 5,000 dollar notes for distribution when the *Honduras Packet* sailed. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the fake nation had appointed officials with invented titles. A former Royal Marine, George Woodbine, was also named "Vice-Cazique."
- **[F-21]** On 22 October 1823, six survivors, including James Hastie, signed an affidavit insisting the blame lay not with MacGregor but with Hall and other members of the emigrant party, saying MacGregor had been worse used by Hall and his agents than any man before. — *Src:* [S1],[S5] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* do NOT assert this as proof MacGregor was innocent; it is the eerie loyalty a great con manufactures.
- **[F-22]** As news reached London, MacGregor's scheme was exposed and he left Britain for France shortly before the Poyais survivors arrived home in October 1823, telling an associate he was taking his wife to Italy for her health but going instead to Paris. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-23]** In France, MacGregor attempted a variation of the Poyais scheme (raising a £300,000 loan in August 1825); French authorities arrested him and put him and three associates on trial for fraud in 1826. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the £300,000 is the French issue, distinct from the £200,000 London loan [F-10].
- **[F-24]** At the 1826 French trial the court convicted only one of MacGregor's associates; MacGregor himself was acquitted, and he was never convicted of fraud for the Poyais scheme in Britain or France. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the "he got away with it" kicker; exact court/venue not specified in the featured account, see [Open Q3].
- **[F-25]** After further minor Poyais-related schemes in London through the 1830s (including an attempt to raise an £800,000 loan), MacGregor moved to Venezuela in 1838, was welcomed as a hero and restored to the rank of divisional general, and died in Caracas in 1845 aged 58, buried with full military honours in Caracas Cathedral. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the ultimate vindication-lever close. The con man died a decorated, pensioned free man.
- **[F-26]** As a young officer at Gibraltar, MacGregor developed an obsession with dress, rank insignia and medals that made him unpopular in his regiment; he reportedly forbade any enlisted man from leaving quarters without full dress uniform. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* documented psychology; the engine of the con, not colour.
- **[F-27]** Back in Britain before Poyais, MacGregor styled himself "Sir Gregor MacGregor, Bart.," falsely claiming the MacGregor clan chieftainship and ties to ducal and noble families, and paraded in an extravagant, brightly coloured coach wearing a foreign knightly order's badge. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* he had already faked a baronetcy and grand connections before Poyais; the pattern predates the con.
- **[F-28]** Historian Tamar Frankel argues MacGregor "probably believed his own story" and genuinely hoped to build a Poyaisian society, i.e. the honest reading leans toward a self-deluded fantasist rather than a purely cold cynic. — *Src:* [S1],[S4] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* an interpretation, genuinely debated; flag as a reading, not a fact. Play the ambiguity.
- **[F-29]** An Edinburgh cobbler accepted the flattering post of "Official Shoemaker to the Princess of Poyais," left his family, sailed, and, confronted with the empty reality, shot himself. — *Src:* [S1],[S5] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the man is **unnamed** in the record ("the Edinburgh cobbler / would-be royal shoemaker"); do NOT invent a name. See [Open Q4].
- **[F-30]** James Hastie, a Scottish sawyer, boarded with his wife and three young children; he survived and returned to Britain but two of his three children died in the ordeal, and his written account ("It seemed to be the will of Providence that every circumstance should combine for our destruction") is one of the few first-person settler testimonies. — *Src:* [S1],[S5] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the quoted line is Hastie's own words as recorded; the channel uses no quotes, so render as reported substance.

## Cast, motive & human-cost  ← the story fuel a fact-ledger prunes; all sourced

- **Villain / protagonist, the medal-obsessed fabulist:** MacGregor craved rank he had not earned and invented the paperwork to prove it. As a young officer he was already obsessed with dress, insignia and medals, to the point of forbidding his men out of quarters without full uniform [F-26]; back home he faked a baronetcy and a clan chieftainship and toured in a brightly coloured coach [F-27]. Poyais is that same tic scaled to an entire nation with himself as its prince. This is interiority the writer can open a character beat on, and it is the engine of the con, not "mood." **Crucial hedge:** Frankel reads him as a man who probably half-believed his own fantasy and hoped to actually build the place [F-28], so the honest register is *self-deluded fantasist*, not a cackling cold cynic. Play the ambiguity.
- **Human cost (light, aggregate):** ordinary tradespeople and families sold everything and sailed for cheap farmland in a country the guidebook promised was healthy, gold-bearing, and already built [F-06],[F-07],[F-05]. They stepped off the boat onto empty, swampy coast with no city, no government, and no one to meet them [F-15],[F-18]; the real king turned up and told them the whole grant was worthless [F-19]. Of the roughly 250 who sailed, more than half died of disease and exposure and fewer than 50 ever came home [F-16]. Register it concretely and move; this is a 200-year-old curiosity, not a loss to milk.
- **Optional light texture (named victims, NOT a required biography):** if the writer wants one fast human face, two are documented: an Edinburgh cobbler flattered with the title "Official Shoemaker to the Princess of Poyais" who shot himself on the empty shore ([F-29]; he is unnamed in the record, present him as the real, name-lost individual), and James Hastie, a sawyer who came home having buried two of his three children ([F-30]). Use at most one, in a line or two; do NOT build a set-piece.
- **Witnessed-absurd / telling details (all sourced):**
  - The settlers politely camped on the beach and waited for "the Poyaisian authorities" to contact them, then sent a search party inland that found only forgotten foundations and rubble [F-18].
  - The real Mosquito king arrived and revoked the grant to their faces, saying he had never made MacGregor a Cazique or given him any right to sell the land [F-19].
  - The fake nation had a full staff of officials with invented titles: Hector Hall commissioned as "Baron Tinto" to lead the settlers, a City banker named Mauger installed as head of the nonexistent Bank of Poyais and handed 5,000 banknotes, and a former Marine named "Vice-Cazique" [F-20].

## Verification chain (vindication lever — how the case is PROVEN)
- **What is proven / uncontested:** MacGregor sold bonds, land, and printed currency for a nation that had no capital, no government, and no development, on a site that was empty jungle where a search party found only rubble [F-05],[F-15],[F-18]; the guidebook and the printed notes are surviving physical documents [F-04],[F-08]; roughly 250 sailed and most died [F-13],[F-14],[F-16]; the real king revoked the grant to the settlers' faces [F-19]. **What stays soft / contested:** the *precise* death toll (an estimate, [Open Q1]); whether the ledger's loan total should count one or two issues ([Open Q2]); and MacGregor's inner state, cynical fraud vs. self-deluded fantasist, which is genuinely debated (Frankel leans self-delusion, [F-28]). The writer should separate the hard core from the psychology out loud; that honesty is a credibility beat.
- **Corroboration:** the backbone converges across independent authorities, the Wikipedia *featured* article carrying David Sinclair's scholarly monograph and Tamar Frankel's fraud study [S1],[S2],[S4], an independent biography [S2], and aggregated secondary accounts [S5], plus the surviving primary props (the guidebook [S3], the printed bonds and notes) and first-person settler testimony (Hastie [F-30]) that is independent of MacGregor's own promoters.
- **Exoneration (what the obvious villain did NOT do):** the land grant was **genuine**, MacGregor really was given about 8 million acres by a real Mosquito king [F-02], and his generalship under Miranda and Bolívar was **real** [F-01]. The lie was the *development*, not the deed. And the sharpest twist: some of his own ruined settlers refused to blame him, six of them, including a father who had buried two children, swearing an affidavit that pointed at Hall instead [F-21]. Clearing away the cartoon "lone evil genius" is what earns the real indictment: a self-believing fantasist plus a credulous, mania-drunk market killed those people together, and neither alone would have.

## Myths / misconceptions to bust  (OPTIONAL body texture, not a mandated block)
- **Myth:** Poyais was pure invention by a lone evil-genius con man. → **Actually:** MacGregor held a *real* (if worthless) land grant and had a *real* military reputation, and by Frankel's reading probably half-believed his own fantasy; the con was built on a kernel of truth plus a market mania, which is what made it work [F-02],[F-12],[F-28].
- **Myth:** a fake country's bonds could never fool a sophisticated market. → **Actually:** they traded right alongside genuine sovereign debt because 1820s London had almost no due-diligence discipline during the boom, and the fake paper collapsed on the same schedule as the real Latin-American debt [F-11],[F-12].
- **Myth:** the victims were greedy speculators who got what they deserved. → **Actually:** many were ordinary tradespeople and families who sold everything for farmland, not a flip; the "gold in the rivers" pitch targeted working emigrants, and some *still* defended MacGregor afterward [F-29],[F-30],[F-21].

## Open questions, gaps & hedges
- **[Open Q1 — death toll]** "At least 180 of ~250 died; fewer than 50 returned" is the featured-article figure, corroborated by independent secondary accounts, but it remains an **estimate** and varies slightly across retellings. Script it as **"more than half never came home"** unless a specific number is confirmed against Sinclair, *The Land That Never Was*. (Ties [F-16].)
- **[Open Q2 — the loan issues]** The 1822 London loan was £200,000 via Sir John Perring (bonds of £100/£200/£500 at 80%, 6%). MacGregor separately raised a £300,000 "loan" in the 1825 French scheme and later attempted an £800,000 issue, so retellings that cite a single larger total are conflating distinct issues. State the £200,000 as the London bond; treat the £300,000 as the French follow-up [F-23]. (Ties [F-10].)
- **[Open Q3 — the trial]** The 1826 French fraud trial and acquittal (only one associate convicted) is well-attested, but the exact court/venue/date is not specified in the sources pulled and should be confirmed at lock. (Ties [F-24].)
- **[Open Q4 — the cobbler's name]** The Edinburgh cobbler / "Official Shoemaker to the Princess of Poyais" who shot himself is documented but **unnamed** in the record. Do NOT invent a name; present him as the real, name-lost individual he is. (Ties [F-29].)
- **[minor — guidebook harvest wording]** The guidebook's "three maize harvests a year / a day's hunt feeds a family for a week" specifics are widely quoted but were not re-verified against the primary page this pass (the archive.org catalog page does not expose the full text). The general claim (healthy climate, easy abundance, no tropical disease) is safe [F-07]; hedge the exact numeric phrasing.
- These are the only spots to hedge; the rest of the ledger, including the guidebook's gold and climate claims, the banknote/land/bond mechanics, and the named victims, is safe to script from.
