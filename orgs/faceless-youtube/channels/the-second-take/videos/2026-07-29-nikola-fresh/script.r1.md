# Script: The $30 Billion Truck That Only Rolled Downhill

- **Source idea:** ST-029
- **Target length:** 8-10 min
- **Voice:** Miles (vSjOBQp24DUB2COr2xI9)
- **Estimated runtime:** 9:21

---

It's December 2016, in a hall in Salt Lake City, and Trevor Milton is standing next to a semi truck hidden under a giant white sheet. He pulls it off. This is the Nikola One, a hydrogen powered eighteen wheeler from a company almost nobody has heard of, and Milton tells the room to come up and touch it. Feel how sturdy it is. He works the room like a timeshare salesman. A pusher is a prop somebody shoves into position for the cameras, and Milton makes a point of the word. This is a real truck, he says. This is not a pusher. Four years later that company was worth nearly thirty billion dollars, which for one afternoon made it worth more than Ford. And the video that got it there showed that truck cruising down a highway at speed. By the company's own admission, it wasn't moving under its own power. Something else was moving it. Gravity.

The company was Nikola, founded around 2014 by Milton, and yes, it's named after Nikola Tesla, the same dead inventor that Tesla is named after. Two companies, one namesake, no relation. Which didn't stop Nikola from suing Tesla in 2018 for copying its windshield. The pitch was hydrogen: big rigs running on hydrogen instead of diesel, hauling freight across the country with nothing coming out of the tailpipe but water. Nobody had built any of it yet.

In January 2018, Nikola released a promotional video called Nikola One in Motion. It's exactly what it sounds like: the truck, on an open road, moving at highway speed, doing the one thing a truck has to do. For a company that had never handed a single vehicle to a single customer, that video was the product.

In March of 2020, Nikola announced it was merging with something called VectoIQ. VectoIQ was a SPAC, which is a company that's already listed on the stock exchange and owns nothing whatsoever. Just a ticker and a pile of money, sitting there waiting for a real company to climb inside. The merger closed on the third of June, and the next morning Nikola was trading on the Nasdaq under NKLA.

So how does a truck company with no trucks get onto the Nasdaq? It buys one. Not a truck, a company. And going public through the side door skipped something specific. When you IPO the regular way, the promises you make about the future are yours to defend: say you'll be building a million trucks by Tuesday, miss it, and investors can take you to court over it. Make that identical promise inside a merger and the law hands it a shield. It's the difference between saying it under oath and saying it in an ad. So Nikola could promise the market anything it liked, and put numbers on it.

And boy did they. On the ninth of June, six trading days in, NKLA more than doubled off its opening price, and Nikola was suddenly worth somewhere near thirty billion dollars. Milton got on Twitter and announced that Nikola was now worth more than Ford and Fiat Chrysler. The people who count these things for a living said the two were basically neck and neck, which is its own kind of insane. Ford builds trucks and sells them to people. Nikola had projections and that video.

Then on the eighth of September, 2020, General Motors put its name on it. Two billion dollars for an eleven percent stake, with GM engineering and building Nikola's pickup truck, the Badger. Nikola's shares jumped by half in a day. The largest carmaker in America had cosigned the whole thing. Two days later, a short seller published a report titled: How to Parlay an Ocean of Lies Into a Partnership With the Largest Auto OEM in America.

The short seller was Hindenburg Research, and a short seller is a firm that makes money when a stock goes down. Hindenburg said so in the report itself: they were short Nikola before they published a word of it. What they published was that the Nikola One in that video had been towed to the top of a shallow hill on a quiet road in Utah, released, and filmed from an angle that made the road look flat. Then they went and checked. They drove an SUV out to the same stretch of the Mormon Trail Road outside Grantsville, killed the engine at the top, and let go. Fifty six miles an hour, over about two miles, on nothing but the slope.

Four days later Nikola answered. The company didn't say the truck had been driving. The company said it had never stated the truck was driving under its own propulsion in the video, although the truck was designed to do just that. Designed to. So the truck couldn't, the video looked like it could, and that part was on you for watching it. Hindenburg called that a tacit admission of securities fraud.

After that, everybody started checking everything. Nikola had sold the Badger, the pickup GM agreed to build, as a truck built from the ground up on its own technology. The one prototype that actually existed got put together in 2020 out of parts pulled from a Ford F-150 Raptor. The ground it got built up from was a Ford F-150. The engineer who led the Badger's design later testified at Milton's fraud trial that when he pushed back on using the Ford parts, Milton texted him and called him an idiot. Milton had also gone on a podcast that July and said there were five Badgers coming off the assembly line right now in Ulm, Germany. Bosch, the company supposedly building them, later confirmed not one had been finished.

Then there was the hydrogen, which was the entire point of the company. Nikola had never built a hydrogen plant. A federal indictment would later say it had never so much as obtained a permit for one. And at the single hydrogen station it did finish, at its own headquarters in Arizona, the hydrogen was trucked in from an outside vendor. Milton had spent months telling people Nikola could make hydrogen in house for under three dollars a kilo. Asked in July of 2020 how much hydrogen Nikola was producing, he said none at all. Hindenburg's report said the prototype with H2 stenciled on the side had natural gas turbine parts inside it.

And the order book. Nikola pointed investors at something like ten billion dollars in truck orders. Its own SEC filings described those orders as non binding, meaning a customer could walk away any time before signing an actual lease. It's an Amazon cart. About a third of that ten billion belonged to one trucking company that had a little over a million dollars in the bank.

Milton was gone within two weeks of the report. He stepped aside on the twentieth of September, writing that Nikola was in his blood and always would be, and that he intended to defend himself against false accusations from outside detractors. And on the thirtieth of November, GM came back with a new deal: no equity stake, no Badger, just an agreement to sell Nikola some fuel cells. The shares fell by a quarter that day. The Badger was never built.

In July of 2021 a federal grand jury indicted Milton on four counts of fraud, for lying, prosecutors said, about virtually every aspect of the business. The jury was out about five hours and came back guilty on three of them, and not guilty on the fourth. In December of 2023 the judge sentenced him to four years in prison, took his ranch in Utah, four and a half thousand acres he'd bought partly with Nikola stock, and ordered him to pay the investors back. Prosecutors put what ordinary people lost buying that stock at more than 660 million dollars. Those are regular people who read about a hydrogen truck company and figured they were early.

Nikola the company had a bill of its own, from years earlier. In December of 2021 it settled with the SEC for 125 million dollars, without admitting or denying anything. Then it turned around and went after Milton in arbitration to get that money back, and won: a panel ordered him to reimburse Nikola about 165 million dollars, most of the penalty plus the company's legal fees, and a judge upheld it in 2024. The panel did put three percent of the blame on Nikola itself, for not stopping him.

The company never recovered from any of it. On the nineteenth of February, 2025, Nikola filed for Chapter 11, the kind of bankruptcy where you're supposed to reorganize and come back. It didn't come back. Its assets went to auction that spring, and the EV maker Lucid bought Nikola's factory in Coolidge, Arizona and everything in it for close to thirty million dollars. A company that was briefly worth nearly thirty billion dollars sold the building for thirty million.

Five weeks after that filing, in March of 2025, President Trump pardoned Trevor Milton. Milton said Trump phoned him personally. Trump said Milton had been unfairly prosecuted, that they destroyed five years of his life and he did nothing wrong. Milton never served a day of the four years. The October before, he and his wife had given more than 1.8 million dollars to Trump's re election effort. A pardon lifts the sentence. It doesn't undo the verdict, and nobody has said what happens to the money he was ordered to pay back.

The only company that comes out of this clean is Ford. They got passed on the scoreboard for a single afternoon by a truck company with no trucks, and then that truck company built its one pickup out of Ford parts.
