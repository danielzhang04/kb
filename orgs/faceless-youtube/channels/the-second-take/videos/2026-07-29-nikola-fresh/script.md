# Script: The $30 Billion Truck That Only Rolled Downhill

- **Source idea:** ST-029
- **Target length:** 7:30-9:30
- **Voice:** Miles (vSjOBQp24DUB2COr2xI9)
- **Estimated runtime:** 9:42

---

It's December 2016, in a hall in Salt Lake City, and Trevor Milton pulls a white sheet off a semi truck. This is the Nikola One, a hydrogen powered eighteen wheeler, and Milton tells the room to come up and touch it. Feel how sturdy it is. He works the room like a gym salesman in January. This is a real truck, he says. Less than four years later his company was worth nearly thirty billion dollars, which for one afternoon made it worth more than Ford. And the thing that sold it was a video: that truck, on an open road, at highway speed. It wasn't driving. Something else was moving it. Gravity.

The company was Nikola, founded in 2014 by Milton, and yes, it's named after Nikola Tesla, the same dead inventor that Tesla is named after. No relation. What Nikola sold was hydrogen. Big rigs that ran on hydrogen instead of diesel, with nothing coming out of the tailpipe but water. Every semi you have ever been stuck behind runs on diesel, and here was a guy saying he had fixed that. Nobody had built any of it yet.

You know the guy in a group project who says the slides are basically done? That was Milton's whole method. He talked in the present tense about things that didn't exist yet. Hydrogen, made in house, for under three dollars a kilo. A truck that fully functions. Five pickup trucks coming off the assembly line right now in Ulm, Germany. Right now. And he said all of it on stages, on podcasts, and on Twitter, and people went NUTS for it.

Now, back at that unveiling. Prosecutors later said Milton's own chief engineer had warned him weeks before that the truck would not be ready, and asked him to postpone. Milton went ahead. The room walked up and touched a truck that couldn't drive. Which left him with a problem, because sooner or later a truck company has to show you a truck moving. So you don't need a truck that drives. You need a truck that looks like it's driving. A little over a year later, Nikola put out a promotional video called Nikola One in Motion. The truck, doing the one thing a truck has to do.

Two years after that, Nikola went public. So how does a truck company with no trucks get onto the Nasdaq? It buys one. Not a truck. A company. Nikola merged with something called VectoIQ, and VectoIQ was a SPAC, which is a company that's already listed on the stock exchange and owns nothing at all. Just a ticker and a pile of money, waiting for a real company to climb inside. The merger closed in June of 2020, and the next morning Nikola was trading under the ticker NKLA.

Six trading days in, the stock more than doubled, and Milton got on Twitter and announced that Nikola was now worth more than Ford and Fiat Chrysler. Ford builds trucks and sells them to people who drive them. Nikola had never handed a vehicle to a single customer and had no money coming in. What it had was projections and that video. A Kickstarter with a stock ticker. And it was ordinary people buying it.

So how is any of that legal? Because they never did a normal IPO. Go public the normal way, by selling shares to the public, and the promises you make about the future are yours to defend. Say you'll build a million trucks by Tuesday, miss it, and investors can sue you. Make the same promise inside a merger and it gets a lot harder to sue you over it. It's the difference between saying it under oath and saying it in an ad.

That September, General Motors put its name on it. Two billion dollars for a stake in Nikola, with GM engineering and building Nikola's pickup truck, the Badger. Don't ask me why. Maybe GM was scared of missing the next Tesla. Either way, the biggest carmaker in America signed, and Nikola's shares jumped by half in a day.

Two days later, a short seller published a report on Nikola. A short seller is a firm that makes money when a stock goes down, and this one said so in the report itself: it was betting against Nikola before it published a word. The title called Nikola an ocean of lies. It got meaner from there.

The firm was Hindenburg Research, and what it had was the video. Hindenburg said the Nikola One had been towed to the top of a shallow hill on a quiet road in Utah, let go, and filmed from an angle that made the road look flat. Then they went and checked. They drove an SUV out to the same stretch of the Mormon Trail Road, killed the engine at the top, and let it roll. Fifty six miles an hour, on nothing but the slope.

Four days later Nikola answered. The company said it had never stated the truck was driving under its own propulsion in the video, although the truck was designed to do just that. Designed to. So the truck couldn't, the video looked like it could, and that part was on you for watching it. Hindenburg said that was as good as admitting to fraud.

The truck was the show. So what else was a show? Start with the Badger, the pickup GM had agreed to build. Milton had been selling it as a truck built from the ground up on Nikola's own technology. The one prototype that existed was put together partly out of Ford F-150 Raptor parts. Built from the ground up. The ground was a Ford F-150. Which is a bold look for a company that had once sued Tesla over the shape of a windshield.

The engineer who led the Badger's design would later say, under oath, that when he pushed back on using the Ford parts, Milton texted him and called him an idiot. So the one guy who checked was the idiot. And those five pickups coming off the line in Ulm? Bosch, Nikola's manufacturing partner, said afterward that not one of them had ever been finished.

The Badger was one truck. Hydrogen was the whole company, and Nikola had never built a hydrogen plant. A federal indictment would later say it had never even gotten a permit for one. The one hydrogen station Nikola ever finished, at its own headquarters in Arizona, got its hydrogen delivered. By truck. From somebody else. That's a Starbucks buying its coffee from the Dunkin next door. And that three dollars a kilo? Asked about it a month after the stock peaked, Milton said Nikola was producing none at all. Zero. At a hydrogen company.

Which left the orders. Nikola had pointed investors at ten billion dollars in truck orders, and its own filings with the SEC called those orders non binding. It's an Amazon cart. I've had a tent in mine for years. Hindenburg found that about a third of that ten billion belonged to one trucking company with a little over a million dollars in the bank.

Milton was gone inside two weeks of the report. He stepped aside as chairman. On the way out, he wrote that Nikola was in his blood, and always would be. Ten weeks later GM came back with a new deal. No stake in the company, no Badger, just an agreement to sell Nikola fuel cells, the part that turns hydrogen into electricity. GM was now selling hydrogen parts to a company that had never made any hydrogen. The shares fell by a quarter that day. The Badger was never built.

The following summer, a federal grand jury indicted Milton. Prosecutors said he had lied about virtually every part of the business. He went to trial in New York, and after two months of it the jury was out about five hours. Basically a lunch break. They convicted him of fraud and acquitted him on one count. The judge sentenced him to four years, took his ranch in Utah, which he'd bought partly with Nikola stock, and ordered him to pay the investors back. Prosecutors added up what regular people lost on that stock: more than 660 million dollars, from people who read about a hydrogen truck company and figured they were early. Four years, a ranch, and the bill.

Nikola had a bill of its own, from a couple of years earlier. The SEC had fined the company 125 million dollars over the same lies, settled without admitting anything. Then the company turned around and billed Milton for it. And won. Nikola spent the next few years burning cash and recalling the few trucks it did build, and in early 2025 it filed for Chapter 11, the kind of bankruptcy where you reorganize and come back. It didn't come back. Its assets went to auction that spring. A company that had been worth nearly thirty billion dollars sold its factory for thirty million.

Five weeks after that filing, President Trump pardoned Trevor Milton. Milton said Trump phoned him personally. Trump said Milton had been unfairly prosecuted, that five years of his life had been destroyed and he did nothing wrong. Milton never served a day of the four years. A pardon lifts the sentence. It doesn't undo the verdict. The October before, he and his wife had given more than 1.8 million dollars to Trump's re election campaign.

GM walked away with a fuel cell contract and a bad week of press. Hindenburg was short when it published, and the stock fell. The people who bought the stock paid for all of it. And the company that came out of it best was Lucid, an actual electric car maker, which bought that factory out of the wreck and offered jobs to the people who used to work in it. Back in 2016, Trevor Milton stood next to that truck and told a room full of people to come up and touch it. This is a real truck, he said. And it was a real truck. It just needed a hill.
