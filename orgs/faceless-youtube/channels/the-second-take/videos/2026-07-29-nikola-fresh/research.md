**The "Nikola One in Motion" video — staged, admitted, charged (keep the epistemic layers separate)**
- **[F-05]** In January 2018, Nikola released a promotional video titled "Nikola One in Motion" showing the semi-truck appearing to cruise at highway speed under its own power. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high (the video's existence and title are undisputed).
- **[F-06] (ALLEGED — Hindenburg)** Hindenburg Research's September 10, 2020 report alleged, citing text messages from a former Nikola employee, that the company towed the non-functional prototype to the top of a shallow grade on a remote stretch of road — identified as the Mormon Trail Road near Grantsville, Utah — released it, and filmed it coasting downhill under gravity alone, using camera framing designed to make the grade look flat. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* med-high (this is the short-seller's allegation, corroborated by F-07's physical test and later used at trial per F-10, but originates as Hindenburg's claim).
- **[F-07]** To test the physical plausibility of that allegation, Hindenburg's own investigators drove an SUV over the same stretch of road, cut power, and coasted to 56 mph over roughly 2.1 miles without any engine power — corroborating that the grade alone could plausibly produce a speed matching the video, without the truck's own propulsion. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* med (Hindenburg's own test, not independently re-run in this pass, but a specific, checkable methodology rather than a bare assertion).
- **[F-08] (ADMITTED — Nikola's own words)** Nikola's own September 14, 2020 rebuttal press release admitted the truck was not moving under its own propulsion in the video, but argued the company "never stated its truck was driving under its own propulsion in the video, although the truck was designed to do just that." — *Src:* S2 · *Conf:* high (this is Nikola's own public statement, quoted directly) · *Note:* this is the exact "never explicitly claimed self-propulsion" defense the brief flagged — ledger it as the company's own argument, not as an accepted fact that the video was not misleading.
- **[F-09]** Hindenburg's follow-up response rejected Nikola's rebuttal, calling it a "tacit admission of securities fraud" and arguing the video was designed to — and did — create the impression among investors that the truck was self-propelled at speed, regardless of the precise wording Nikola used to describe it. — *Src:* S3 · *Conf:* high (Hindenburg's stated position, presented as such — not adjudicated fact).
- **[F-10] (CHARGED / used at trial)** At Milton's 2022 criminal trial, prosecutors argued the truck had been towed to a hilltop and released to roll downhill to create an illusion of functionality, and presented the "Nikola One in Motion" video to the jury as evidence; US District Judge Edgardo Ramos permitted the video to be shown. — *Src:* S4,S7 · *Conf:* high (reported trial proceedings).

**The SPAC mechanism**
- **[F-14]** Nikola announced a merger agreement with VectoIQ Acquisition Corp — a special-purpose acquisition company (SPAC), a publicly traded shell company with no underlying operating business — on March 2-3, 2020, at an implied enterprise value of about $3.3 billion, alongside a $525 million private-placement (PIPE) investment led by Fidelity. — *Src:* S14 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-15]** The merger closed June 3, 2020; VectoIQ was renamed Nikola Corporation, and the combined company's shares began trading on Nasdaq under the ticker NKLA on June 4, 2020 — before Nikola had produced or delivered a single commercial vehicle. — *Src:* S14 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-16] (the structural loophole)** Under U.S. securities law's Private Securities Litigation Reform Act (PSLRA), forward-looking statements made "in connection with" a traditional IPO are excluded from a legal safe harbor that otherwise protects optimistic projections from investor lawsuits — but that same safe-harbor exclusion does NOT apply to a merger, including a SPAC merger. Practically, this meant a company reverse-merging into a SPAC (like Nikola into VectoIQ) could publicly share ambitious production/revenue projections with meaningfully less investor-lawsuit exposure than the same claims would carry inside an IPO prospectus, which also undergoes tighter underwriter due diligence than a de-SPAC deal historically did. — *Src:* S24 · *Conf:* med-high (well-established in legal/regulatory literature; the mechanism is settled law, not disputed).
- **[F-17]** Legal and regulatory commentary (including a Clifford Chance briefing titled "Putting SPACs on the Spot: the case of Nikola Motor Company") cites Nikola specifically as the central case study for how the SPAC route let projection-heavy, technologically unproven claims reach public markets with less scrutiny than a traditional IPO — a gap the SEC's own final rules, adopted January 2024, later moved to close by removing the safe harbor for SPAC/de-SPAC forward-looking statements. — *Src:* S24 · *Conf:* med-high.

**The GM deal, the Hindenburg report, and the fallout — in sequence**
- **[F-21]** On September 8, 2020, General Motors announced a $2 billion deal for an 11% equity stake in Nikola, under which GM would engineer and manufacture Nikola's planned Badger electric/hydrogen pickup and supply battery and fuel-cell technology; Nikola shares jumped as much as 53% on the news. — *Src:* S11 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-22]** Two days later, on September 10, 2020, Hindenburg Research published its report; Nikola's stock fell sharply over the following days. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-23]** On September 14, 2020, Nikola issued its rebuttal press release ("Nikola Sets the Record Straight on False and Misleading Short Seller Report"); Hindenburg published its rejection the same week, calling the rebuttal a "tacit admission of securities fraud." — *Src:* S2,S3 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-24]** On September 20-21, 2020, Milton announced he would step aside as executive chairman and board member. In his resignation message he wrote, "Nikola is in my blood and always will be... I made the decision to approach the board of directors and volunteer to step aside... The focus should be on the company and its world-changing mission, not me. We have to keep our eye on the target and deliver without distractions" — and separately stated he intended "to defend myself against false accusations leveled against me by outside detractors." - **[F-25]** Nikola's in-person "Nikola World" event, planned for December 3-5, 2020 to unveil a production-ready Badger, was postponed indefinitely on September 30, 2020 — officially attributed by the company to COVID-19 venue-capacity restrictions — amid the broader fallout; GM stated on the same date that the Nikola transaction had not closed by its original September 30 target. — *Src:* S22 · *Conf:* high on the postponement and date; *Conf:* low on whether COVID-19 was the true reason versus a convenient cover story (present the official stated reason, flag the timing coincidence).

- **[F-27]** The Badger pickup itself was never put into production, and no equity partnership between GM and Nikola materialized beyond the narrower fuel-cell supply agreement. — *Src:* S12 · *Conf:* high.

**The specific lies — charged, and what each one actually was**
- **[F-28] (ALLEGED/CHARGED)** The federal indictment and SEC order alleged Milton falsely claimed, in a July 2020 podcast interview, that Nikola had "five [Badgers] coming off the assembly line right now in Ulm, Germany"; Bosch — Nikola's manufacturing partner — later confirmed no Badger trucks had actually been completed. — *Src:* S16 · *Conf:* med-high.
- **[F-29]** Trial evidence showed the single Badger prototype that did exist was built in 2020 using parts taken from a Ford F-150 Raptor, combined with components from a separate, also-unfinished Nikola power-sports vehicle — not "built from the ground up" with proprietary Nikola technology, as Milton had publicly claimed. — *Src:* S16 · *Conf:* high (trial testimony).
- **[F-30]** A former Nikola employee, Brendan Babiarz, who led the Badger's design, testified that Milton called him "an idiot" over text for questioning the use of Ford parts, and that using the Raptor parts was a shortcut Milton insisted on to meet a self-imposed reveal deadline. — *Src:* S16 · *Conf:* med-high (single witness's trial testimony, but sworn and reported).
- **[F-31] (CHARGED)** The federal indictment stated that Nikola "had never obtained a permit for, let alone constructed, a hydrogen production station, nor had it produced any hydrogen," and that at the single hydrogen station it completed at its Arizona headquarters in April 2019, the hydrogen actually dispensed there was trucked in from an outside vendor rather than produced on-site. — *Src:* S4 · *Conf:* high (indictment language).
- **[F-32] (ADMITTED, after being pressed)** Milton publicly claimed — including in a March 2020 podcast — that Nikola could produce hydrogen in-house for under roughly $3-4/kg (Hindenburg's report cites Milton's claimed under-$3/kg figure as an "81% reduction" from an earlier ~$16/kg baseline), despite Nikola having produced zero hydrogen and having no signed electricity-supply agreement that would make such a cost achievable. When directly pressed on the question in July 2020, Milton admitted Nikola was producing "no hydrogen at all." — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high on the admission; *Conf:* med on the exact dollar figures cited (sources give slightly different numbers, $3 vs $4/kg).
- **[F-33]** Nikola's own SEC filings describe its publicly touted truck "reservations" — cited, including in investor-facing materials, as evidence of up to roughly $10 billion in potential future revenue — as non-binding: customers could cancel at any time before signing a lease agreement, and the filings characterize the list internally as an indicator of "potential demand," not a firm order backlog. Hindenburg's report separately noted that U.S. Xpress, which represented roughly one-third of the reservation total by value (about $3.5 billion), held only about $1.3 million in cash at the time — raising doubt that reservation would ever convert into a real, paid order. — *Src:* S10,S1 · *Conf:* high on the "non-binding, cancellable" filing language; *Conf:* med on the US Xpress cash figure (Hindenburg's own reporting, not independently re-verified this pass).

**Conviction and sentencing — Milton, the person**
- **[F-34] (CHARGED)** On July 29, 2021, a federal grand jury in the Southern District of New York indicted Milton on two counts of securities fraud and two counts of wire fraud, alleging he made false and misleading statements about "virtually every aspect" of Nikola's business — vehicle functionality, in-house technology, partnerships, and the order book. — *Src:* S4 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-35] (CONVICTED)** On October 14, 2022, after roughly five hours of jury deliberation following a two-month trial, Milton was found guilty on one count of securities fraud and two counts of wire fraud; he was acquitted on one additional securities fraud count (four counts were charged in total). — *Src:* S7 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-36] (SENTENCED)** On December 18, 2023, US District Judge Edgardo Ramos sentenced Milton to four years in federal prison (48 months on each count, served concurrently), a $1 million fine, three years of supervised release, and forfeiture of the 4,678-acre Wasatch Creeks Ranch in Morgan County, Utah — which Milton had purchased in 2020 partly financed using Nikola stock, paying about $8.5 million in cash for it. The court also ordered restitution to defrauded investors. Prosecutors had sought an 11-year sentence, a $5 million fine, and restitution based on their own figure for retail-investor losses — cited at sentencing as more than $660 million (with later court filings refining the figure toward approximately $660.8 million). — *Src:* S6,S17 · *Conf:* high on the sentence and forfeiture; *Conf:* med-high on the exact $660M/$660.8M figure, which is prosecutors' estimate, attributed as such, not an independently audited total.

**The company's separate settlement — SEC vs. Nikola Corporation (not Milton)**
- **[F-37] (SETTLED — no admission)** Separately from Milton's personal criminal case, Nikola the COMPANY settled SEC civil fraud charges on December 21, 2021, agreeing — without admitting or denying the SEC's findings — to pay a $125 million civil penalty (in five installments through 2023) for misleading investors about its products, technology, and commercial prospects. This is a corporate settlement with no admission of wrongdoing, a different legal category from Milton's later criminal conviction, and it should not be described as "Nikola was found guilty." — *Src:* S5 · *Conf:* high.

**A third, separate track — Nikola's civil arbitration award against Milton**
- **[F-38]** In a private arbitration proceeding, a panel ruled in October/November 2023 that Milton was liable to reimburse Nikola for the bulk of costs the company incurred because of his conduct: about $121.25 million (97%) of the company's own $125 million SEC settlement, plus roughly $46.5 million in Nikola's legal fees and expenses — reported combined totals of roughly $165 million (the arbitration award itself) to $168 million (after judicial confirmation, likely including additional interest/fees). In September 2024, US District Judge Diane Humetewa in Phoenix upheld the arbitration award over Milton's objection that it ignored other executives' "relative fault"; the panel had itself assigned Nikola 3% of the fault. — *Src:* S9 · *Conf:* med-high (the $165M vs $168M discrepancy across outlets is a reporting-precision issue, not a substantive conflict — both numbers appear across reputable sources depending on which components are counted).

**The pardon — Milton, the person (do not blend with the company's fate)**
- **[F-39]** On March 27-28, 2025, President Trump pardoned Trevor Milton. Milton announced the pardon on social media, saying Trump called him personally; Trump stated publicly he believed Milton had been "unfairly prosecuted," saying, "They destroyed five years of his life, and he did nothing wrong." — *Src:* S8,S20 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-40] (pardon ≠ innocence — the load-bearing distinction)** A presidential pardon relieves the pardoned person of the criminal sentence (Milton did not serve the prison term) and can restore certain civil rights, but it does not retroactively establish that the underlying conduct did not occur, and it is a distinct legal instrument from separately-owed civil obligations — a civil restitution order, the arbitration judgment against Milton [F-38], or the company's own SEC settlement [F-37] are not automatically erased by a pardon of the criminal sentence. Reporting on Milton's pardon states it "could wipe out hundreds of millions of dollars in restitution that prosecutors were seeking for defrauded investors" — note the conditional "could": the reporting available in this pass does not confirm a final, settled post-pardon status for that $660M+ restitution obligation, so the script must not assert it was definitively cancelled or definitively preserved. — *Src:* S19 · *Conf:* med (the legal principle — pardon ≠ vacated conviction, ≠ erased civil liability — is well-established law; the specific real-world resolution of Milton's restitution is the part that remains genuinely unconfirmed in sourcing found this pass).
- **[F-41]** Milton and his wife together contributed more than $1.8 million to Trump's 2024 re-election campaign effort in October 2024 — several months before the March 2025 pardon. Multiple outlets reported this timing and amount as noteworthy without asserting a proven quid pro quo; the script should present the donation as a reported fact and let the juxtaposition speak for itself, not assert a bribery claim that hasn't been charged or proven. — *Src:* S20 · *Conf:* high on the donation amount and timing; *Conf:* n/a on causation (not a fact in evidence).

**The company's separate ending — Nikola Corporation's bankruptcy (NOT Milton's story)**
- **[F-42] (company ≠ person — the second load-bearing distinction)** Nikola Corporation — the company, which Milton had already left in September 2020 and had no operating role in by 2025 — filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy on February 19, 2025, after years of cash burn, product recalls, and reputational damage stemming from the fraud revelations. This is a corporate bankruptcy filing, unconnected procedurally to Milton's personal pardon five weeks later; the two events should never be narrated as one continuous story beat ("he was pardoned and then his company failed" implies a causal/temporal link stronger than the record supports — the bankruptcy preceded the pardon by five weeks and was driven by years of the company's own operating losses). — *Src:* S18 · *Conf:* high.

## Cast, motive & human-cost

- **Protagonist/villain — Trevor Milton:** the pattern across every beat is the same showman's move — state the aspirational endpoint as if it were already the present-tense product (a truck that "fully functions," hydrogen "produced" at a breakthrough cost, a Badger "coming off the assembly line") and let the gap between claim and reality be someone else's problem to discover later. His own resignation letter's language — "the focus should be on the company and its world-changing mission, not me" [F-24] — reads, in hindsight, as the same promotional register that got him indicted, applied to his own exit. Interiority the writer can open on: not simple lying for its own sake, but a founder who appears to have genuinely believed the demos would eventually catch up to the promises, and kept promoting the promise past the point the technology existed to back it.
- **Human cost (light, aggregate):** prosecutors' own figure, used at sentencing, is that retail investors — ordinary people buying NKLA stock on the hype — lost more than $660 million combined [F-36]. This is the number to use for the human-stakes beat: not a named victim's story, but a concrete, sourced aggregate loss attributable to buying into claims later proven false.
- **Witnessed-absurd / telling details:** (a) a truck stenciled "H2" for a hydrogen future that, per the company's own admission, was producing zero hydrogen [F-32]; (b) a $2 billion truck-manufacturing partnership with the largest U.S. automaker collapsing to a bare parts-supply deal within twelve weeks of being announced [F-21,F-26]; (c) the Badger — pitched as an F-150 killer built "from the ground up" — turning out to be assembled in part from actual Ford F-150 Raptor parts [F-29]; (d) a founder tweeting his own company was "worth more than Ford" on the same day independent reporting says the two were essentially neck-and-neck [F-19]; (e) the SUV test: Hindenburg's investigators coasting an ordinary SUV to 56 mph, engine off, down the exact stretch of Utah road the truck was filmed on [F-07].

## Myths / misconceptions to bust

- **Myth:** "Nikola faked the whole truck — it never existed." → **Actually:** the Nikola One prototype existed and was shown publicly; what was faked was its functionality — it could not move under its own power, and the "in motion" video showed it towed and coasting, not driving. ([F-05],[F-06],[F-08])
- **Myth:** "The pardon means Milton was innocent / cleared of wrongdoing." → **Actually:** a pardon relieves the sentence; it is not a finding of innocence and does not undo the jury's guilty verdict as a historical fact, nor does it automatically erase separate civil obligations (the arbitration award, potential restitution). ([F-35],[F-38],[F-40])
- **Myth:** "Milton went to prison, then his company also collapsed." → **Actually:** Milton never served his prison sentence — he was pardoned before serving time — and Nikola the company's bankruptcy (Feb 2025) was a separate corporate event, driven by years of cash burn, that preceded the pardon by five weeks and had nothing procedurally to do with it. Keep person and company as two different endings. ([F-39],[F-40],[F-42])
- **Myth:** "Hindenburg proved fraud." → **Actually:** Hindenburg is a short-seller that disclosed its own short position in the same report that made the allegations [F-13] — a financial interest in the stock falling. Many of its claims were later corroborated at trial, but the report itself is an allegation by an interested party, not a verdict; the verdict came from the jury two years later. ([F-06],[F-13],[F-35])
- **Myth:** "Nikola's truck orders proved real demand — a $10 billion order book." → **Actually:** the reservations behind that figure were non-binding and cancellable at any time; Nikola's own SEC filings describe them as an indicator of "potential demand," not a firm backlog. ([F-33])

## Why this matters (universality): OPTIONAL body texture, never the close

- **The rhyme:** a SPAC merger let a pre-revenue company make IPO-grade claims with less of an IPO's built-in scrutiny — the same "skip the normal gate" shape recurs whenever a legal or procedural shortcut lets promotional claims reach public money before the product exists to back them (the dot-com IPO wave's "eyeballs" metrics rhyme loosely here, as do more recent SPAC blowups beyond Nikola). **The spottable tell:** when a company's most-cited evidence of a working product is a video, not a delivered unit, ask what happens if you turn the camera the other way. Weave only if it lands; do not tee up a "this always happens" moral — end on the story's own irony (the demo that was the whole company) instead. (General-knowledge rhyme; not itself drawn from the primary sources above.)
