[B-ROLL: two of the biggest boy bands in the world, and the man just off to the side of the frame]
The Backstreet Boys and NSYNC were two of the biggest boy bands in the world. And the man who built both of them was, at the same time, running one of the longest cons in American history. [BEAT] He sold ordinary people a safe little savings account, paying eight percent, invested in an airline. The airline didn't own a single plane. The accountant who signed off on all of it was an answering machine. And it worked for more than twenty years.

[B-ROLL: one company name sitting behind all of it]
And the whole thing runs through the name of one company: Trans Continental.

[B-ROLL: the record label that built the hits, the part that was completely real]
Lou Pearlman started a record label, and he called it Trans Continental Records. The strangest thing about this whole story is that this part is completely true. One of Trans Continental's acts was the Backstreet Boys, five kids Pearlman put together himself. Then, right behind them, he built NSYNC. Two of the best-selling groups of their entire era, both signed to his label, both his. Real bands, real hits, real sold-out shows, all under the name Trans Continental.

[B-ROLL: the trusted name quietly pointing at a second company]
Under the same Trans Continental umbrella, Pearlman ran a second company, an airline, called Trans Continental Airlines. And he sold ordinary people the chance to put their savings into it. It was pitched as a simple, safe savings account, paying a steady eight percent, year after year, sitting under the same Trans Continental behind the biggest bands in the country. [BEAT] There was just one problem with the airline.

[B-ROLL: an airline that existed only on paper]
It didn't exist. On paper, Trans Continental Airlines reported a fleet of forty-nine aircraft and seventy-eight million dollars in revenue. In reality, it had no planes, no employees, and no revenue of any kind. Prosecutors would later say there was not one true thing about the entire company. And when Pearlman needed to show an investor a photograph of this great fleet of his, the story goes that he took a toy plane off the top of his dresser, sat it on a runway, photographed it, and cropped out the hand that was still holding it. That was the airline. Any money that ever came back out of it, the eight percent people thought they were earning, was just cash from newer investors, quietly handed to the older ones.

[B-ROLL: the safety promises, none of them true]
And to anyone nervous about handing over their life savings, Pearlman had the answers ready. The account was insured by the FDIC, the same government agency that guarantees the money in a normal bank. It was backed by the insurance giant AIG. It was covered by Lloyd's of London, the famous British insurer. None of that was true. It carried no real insurance of any kind. Lloyd's reportedly caught wind of him using their name and, by one account, sent him a letter in 1999 telling him to stop. He ignored it and kept selling.

[B-ROLL: the auditor who blessed the books and did not exist]
And an offer like that, a steady eight percent that never dips, should fall apart the moment somebody sits down and checks the books. So Pearlman made sure there were books to check. Every financial statement was signed off, audited, and blessed by a firm of accountants called Cohen and Siegel. Cohen and Siegel had gone through everything. Cohen and Siegel were spotless. [BEAT] Cohen and Siegel did not exist. When investigators finally went looking for the firm and traced its phone number, the number rang to an answering service, which forwarded the call to a machine, which played a short message in Lou Pearlman's own voice. The independent auditor, the one supposedly keeping Lou honest, was Lou. He'd reportedly even named the firm after a pair of famous old gangsters.

[B-ROLL: even the banks handed over millions on the strength of the fake stamp]
And it did not just work on retirees. Real banks, with real compliance departments, lent Pearlman tens of millions of dollars against those fake audits. By one account, one of them handed over around nineteen million dollars on the strength of a clean report from the accounting firm that was a machine in Lou's voice. He even ran the same play overseas, reportedly flying a group of German lenders out, putting up signage, and hiring actors to pose as his business partners, at an office that doubled as his imaginary accounting firm.

[B-ROLL: why nobody looked behind the curtain; the whole thing was a show]
So how does a fake airline sell fake savings accounts for twenty years, to retirees and bankers alike, with nobody ever looking behind the curtain? [PAUSE] Because Lou Pearlman made looking behind the curtain feel almost insulting. When he wanted someone's money, he sent a private jet to fly them down to Orlando. A limousine met them at the airport. They were walked into a big, busy office, where Lou sat behind his desk, on the phone, closing deals with names they recognized. And then came the closer. He would walk them down the hall to the recording studio, open the door, and there, a few feet away, rehearsing, were the actual Backstreet Boys, or the actual members of NSYNC. The bands were living proof that Trans Continental was exactly what it looked like. The bands' own manager later said it was the greatest picture in the world.

[B-ROLL: the bands, unaware they were the pitch, and being robbed the whole time]
And the bands had no idea they were part of a sales pitch. Pearlman would introduce the total strangers as dear old friends and quietly tell his groups to go be charming. What none of them knew was that the same man using their fame to sell a fake airline was also quietly robbing them. Despite selling millions of records, the money the bands actually saw was almost nothing. NSYNC's first album sold around ten million copies, and each member got paid about ten thousand dollars for it. The five Backstreet Boys, across their first four years, split around three hundred thousand dollars between them, while Pearlman and the label pulled in millions. He collected as their manager. He collected again as their producer. Then he paid himself a third time, on top of that, as if he were a sixth member of the band who just happened to never sing. When Brian Littrell finally got suspicious and hired a lawyer to find out where all the money had gone, he got the same answer a lot of people eventually got. [BEAT] Lou had it. And even Lance Bass, at the absolute peak of NSYNC, was still flying to Europe in coach.

[B-ROLL: who actually paid, comedy off]
The people who paid for all of it were not the banks or the bands. They were about eighteen hundred ordinary people, most of them elderly retirees in South Florida, looking for one safe place to leave the money they had spent their whole lives saving. It spread the way these things spread, through trust, one neighbor telling another that they had finally found something solid. One of them was a woman named Jean Madigan. Near the end, when things were starting to feel wrong, she called to pull her money out. Pearlman took the call himself, warm as ever, told her not to worry, everything was going great, and then explained that he couldn't actually get her the money right now. [BEAT] When the whole thing finally came apart, the people who had trusted him got back, by one account, about four cents for every dollar they had handed over.

[B-ROLL: the collapse and the disappearing act]
The end began quietly, in 2006, when a federal prosecutor down in Florida opened a file and started pulling on the thread. He barely got going. About eight days after the investigation began, Lou Pearlman left the country. Soon after, Florida regulators came out and said plainly what the savings program had always been: a fraud, with at least ninety-five million dollars of investors' money simply gone. And Pearlman himself had disappeared. For months he was a ghost, until a pair of tourists recognized his face in Bali. The run was over, and he was brought back to the United States to answer for it.

[B-ROLL: the sentence, and the fine print nobody saw coming]
In 2008, Lou Pearlman pleaded guilty and was sentenced to twenty-five years in federal prison. And the judge attached one strange, almost hopeful line to the sentence. For every one million dollars Pearlman managed to help recover and pay back to his victims, he would get a single month knocked off his time. [PAUSE] It was a real offer, and a man staring down twenty-five years has every reason on earth to take it, to go find every last dollar he had ever squirreled away and buy his freedom back a month at a time. But he barely moved the number. After more than twenty years and hundreds of millions of dollars, the money was not hidden. It was gone.

[B-ROLL: the close; the storefront outlived the store]
Lou Pearlman died in prison in 2016. The airline never owned a plane. The savings accounts were never real. The auditor was an answering machine. [PAUSE] But the two boy bands he built to make all of it look real are still out there, still playing, on the radio, right now.
