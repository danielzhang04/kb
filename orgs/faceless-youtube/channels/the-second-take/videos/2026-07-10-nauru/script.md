# The Richest Country on Earth Ran on Bird Poop

[B-ROLL: a tiny green speck of an island, impossibly rich]
For a few years in the 1970s, the richest country in the world, per capita, was a speck in the Pacific Ocean you've probably never heard of. Eight square miles. A few thousand people. No industry, no farming, almost no jobs. And the entire economy, the thing that made them richer than almost anyone alive, was bird poop. [BEAT] Today that same island is mostly a wasteland, and it survives by renting itself out to other countries. This is where all the money went.

[B-ROLL: a million years of birds quietly building a fortune]
The country is Nauru. For thousands of years, seabirds stopped on this one little island and did what birds do. Layer after layer built up and hardened and fossilized into phosphate rock. And not just any phosphate. Some of the purest, most valuable phosphate on the planet, the raw material the whole world needs to grow its food. An entire country sitting on top of a mountain of it.

[B-ROLL: money arriving without anyone having to work for it]
So when Nauru became independent in 1968, it hit the jackpot. They dug up the phosphate, sold it to the world, and the money came pouring in. And because there were only a few thousand people to split it between, the income per capita skyrocketed. By the 1970s, Nauru had one of the highest incomes anywhere on Earth, right up there with the big oil states. Regular people got royalty checks just for being citizens of the island. Almost nobody had to work. The money simply arrived.

[B-ROLL: the island finally owning its own mine]
Before independence, for most of the century, the phosphate had been dug up by outsiders, a foreign operation run mostly out of Australia and Britain, and most of the profit left the island. Independence in 1968 was the moment Nauru finally owned its own mine, and kept its own money for the first time.

[B-ROLL: the two catches hiding inside the miracle]
But two things were wrong with the miracle, and both were baked in from the very start. To get the phosphate out, you have to tear the island apart. Strip-mining doesn't put the land back when it's done. It chews the ground down to bare rock. So every dollar Nauru earned came from digging a bigger hole in the middle of their own country. And there was only so much bird poop to dig up. Everyone knew, from day one, that one day it would run out.

[B-ROLL: the plan to stay rich forever]
And to their credit, Nauru saw it coming. So they did the smart thing. They took a big share of the phosphate money and put it into a national trust fund, a giant pot of savings meant to outlive the mining. The idea was simple, and pretty wise. The rock would run out someday, but if you invested the fortune well, the island could live off the returns forever. That trust fund was Nauru's entire future. [BEAT] So what happened to that plan?

[B-ROLL: the fortune going out into the world]
And at first, it looked responsible. The trust bought real, serious things. Office towers and hotels overseas, a whole portfolio of property around the world. The crown jewel was a skyscraper right in the middle of Melbourne, Australia, called Nauru House, owned outright by an island of a few thousand people. They started a national airline, Air Nauru, with a fleet so big for such a tiny country that, by some accounts, it could carry about a tenth of the entire population in the air at the same time. It lost money hand over fist, but they had their own flag carrier.

[B-ROLL: the national fortune meets show business]
And then there was the musical. In the early 1990s, a man named Duke Minks, a former road manager for a 1960s British pop band who had somehow become an adviser to the government of Nauru, talked them into helping fund a stage show in London. Not just any show. A West End musical about the love life of Leonardo da Vinci, called Leonardo, A Portrait of Love. It opened in the summer of 1993. It closed about a month later, after thirty-seven performances. Nauru had poured something like two million pounds into it, and got almost none of it back. The president of the country reportedly flew in for opening night.

[B-ROLL: the famous story, and the bigger truth under it]
Now, the musical is the part everyone remembers. The bird-poop country that blew its fortune on a flop show about da Vinci. Except the musical cost around two million pounds. The trust fund, at its peak, was worth more than a billion. The show was a rounding error. [PAUSE] The whole fortune, the skyscraper, the hotels, the airline, all of it, was quietly mismanaged and bled away year after year, while the phosphate that paid for everything ran out underneath them.

[B-ROLL: the number falling off a cliff]
And the fall was brutal. In 1991, the trust fund was worth around one and a third billion Australian dollars. By 2004, it was worth maybe three hundred million. Most of the fortune, simply gone. And the phosphate that had started all of it was nearly finished. In 2004, the island's phosphate exports brought in about six hundred and forty thousand dollars. Not million. Thousand. From a country that used to be one of the richest on the planet. That same year, Nauru House, the skyscraper in Melbourne, the jewel of the whole empire, was sold off to help cover the island's debts.

[B-ROLL: what the island itself had become, quiet now]
And while the money vanished, look at what the digging had done to the island underneath it. Decades of mining had stripped about eighty percent of Nauru down to bare limestone. The whole center of the country became a jagged grey moonscape where almost nothing grows and almost nobody can live, so the people crowded onto a thin ring of coast. The farmland was gone. The food came in on ships instead, canned and processed, and the island's health fell apart with it. Nauru now has some of the highest rates of obesity and diabetes found anywhere in the world. By 2013, about a quarter of its people were living below the poverty line. On an island that, forty years earlier, had been too rich to bother working.

[B-ROLL: a country with nothing left to sell but itself]
So as the phosphate wound down and the fortune drained away, Nauru went looking for something else to sell. And what it found, in the late 1990s, was itself. [PAUSE] It became an offshore banking haven, a place where you could open a bank that was really just a name on a piece of paper. According to a former deputy head of Russia's central bank, around seventy billion dollars of Russian criminal money washed through Nauru in a single year. The island sold passports to foreigners, some of which were reported to have ended up with members of al-Qaeda. The rest of the world noticed, blacklisted Nauru, and squeezed it until it shut the whole thing down.

[B-ROLL: the last product, renting out the country]
And then Nauru found its final product. In 2001, Australia needed somewhere far away to send the asylum seekers trying to reach it by boat, and Nauru said yes. For a price, the tiny island would hold a detention camp full of people a much richer country did not want. It has opened and closed and opened again over the years, but it quietly became the thing propping the whole economy up. In recent years, that single detention deal has brought in well over half of the entire Nauruan government's revenue. Australia had been one of the outsiders mining the phosphate out of Nauru in the first place, back before independence. Now Australia was the one paying to keep the hollowed-out island alive, on the condition that it hold the people Australia had turned away.

[B-ROLL: the birds, the rock, and a skyline that used to be theirs]
It took the birds thousands of years to build Nauru's fortune. It took about thirty to spend it. The phosphate is nearly gone, the trust fund with it. What's left is eight square miles of mined-out rock, a health crisis, and a skyscraper in Melbourne that used to be theirs, with somebody else's name on it now.
