# The Company That Shipped Bricks and Called Them Hard Drives

We're in the 1980s. Big hair, Pac-Man, Back to the Future, and one of the dumbest corporate frauds anybody has ever pulled off.

It's 1983, and the personal computer had just landed in normal people's houses. Anybody with money was dragging a beige box home and setting it up on the kitchen table. Now, computers run on these things called hard drives. A hard drive is the part of your computer that remembers everything after you switch it off: your files, your work, your private collections. Every computer needs one. So while the big computer brands were out front fighting each other like Samsung and Apple fight over phones, the hard drive companies were quietly getting rich in the back. They were selling shovels in a gold rush.

And one of those companies packed 26,000 clay bricks into boxes that said hard drive on the side, and sold them to customers around the world. This is the story of that company.

The company was MiniScribe, out of Colorado, started in 1980 by a guy named Terry Johnson. IBM picked MiniScribe to supply the drives for its personal computers, and the place took off. Within four years they were doing 125 million dollars a year. And at their peak in 1988 they were selling to giants like Compaq and pulling in over 600 million dollars a year. Adjusted for inflation, that's almost as much money as Reddit makes today. On paper, anyway.

Now rewind four years. At the end of 1984, IBM slashed its orders, and MiniScribe fell off a cliff. The company let a quarter of its people go, and Terry Johnson was out the door.

So their bankers at Hambrecht & Quist put about 20 million dollars in and sent their own turnaround guy to run it. His name was Q.T. Wiles, and people called him Doctor Fix It. He was Bob the Builder for failing companies. Can we fix it? Yes we can, as long as nobody counts the inventory. And he ran MiniScribe from Los Angeles while running several other companies at the same time. Full remote, forty years early.

Wiles ran the place on fear. He set sales targets. Hit them, and you got a fat bonus. Miss them, and your head was on the chopping block. And every time you hit a target he raised it, so the next quarter was harder than the one you had just survived. In one meeting he made two of his own managers stand up in front of everybody and fired them on the spot, just so the room understood who was in charge. Lovely man.

By January 1987 the arithmetic caught up with them. MiniScribe's outside accounting firm was Coopers & Lybrand, one of the giants, and the job of an outside firm is to check that what a company says it owns is actually sitting there. Their people came through the warehouse and counted the product themselves. Whatever they counted went onto their own count sheets, and those sheets went into their own locked boxes. The accountants bring their own padlocks, which tells you how much anybody trusts anybody in this business. Then somebody at MiniScribe ran an internal count of the inventory, which is just the stuff sitting in the warehouse that your books say you own, and found that a big pile of it wasn't there.

Coming up short like that meant somebody was getting fired, so instead of reporting it, the managers wrote down a better number. Then they went to the auditors' boxes, popped them open with Allen wrenches and paper clips, took out the real count sheets, and put the fake ones in. This was not Ocean's Eleven. This was grown men picking a lock with office supplies and then blaming the gap on a typo. Coopers & Lybrand went home with numbers MiniScribe had written for them, and the audit came back clean.

And from the outside, MiniScribe looked like the best story in the industry. Revenue climbed to 185 million dollars in 1986, then almost doubled again the year after that. Which is a hell of a growth rate, if you don't ask how much of it was clay.

So the targets kept climbing, and the fake numbers had to climb with them. By now the managers needed something better than a swapped count sheet. They needed product. Something an auditor could walk up to, put his hands on, and tick off a list. And how many of those boxes does an auditor actually open?

Almost none of them. There are thousands of boxes in a warehouse, so Coopers & Lybrand did what's called a test count. They pull a sample, count that, and if the sample matches the paperwork, everything else in the building gets a pass. It's the TSA. Nobody is opening every bag, they pull a few and wave the plane through.

So they put their heads together and came up with a plan. They rented a warehouse near headquarters and went shopping at a local outfit called the Colorado Brick Company. They bought 26,000 bricks, handpicked so that a brick in a box weighed and felt exactly like a boxed hard drive. They packed them, gave every single box its own serial number, because hard drives have serial numbers, and shrink wrapped the lot onto pallets. About four million dollars of premium Colorado clay, sitting on the books as inventory. An auditor pulling a sample doesn't take the drive out and plug it in. He finds the box on the pallet, picks it up, and it feels like a hard drive.

Then they shipped it, because Wiles set sales targets, and a brick sitting in a warehouse isn't a sale. Some of the pallets went overseas to Singapore. Others went out to customers who had agreed in advance to leave the pallets wrapped and mail them back a few weeks later, which means somebody at MiniScribe had to call their own customers and ask them, politely, not to open the boxes. So the same bricks counted as a sale in one quarter, came home, and got counted as inventory in the next. It's buying a giant TV for the Super Bowl and returning it on Tuesday, except the TV is a brick, and you're doing it to your own shareholders.

And this wasn't a one quarter thing. It ran quarter after quarter, and it grew. They started padding the count with broken drives and factory scrap, whatever else was lying around, and the junk padding grew to more than twice what the bricks were worth. One of the executives brought his own family in at night to help pack the boxes. Take your family to work day.

What finally killed it wasn't the auditors. It wasn't a regulator, and it wasn't a reporter digging. It was a layoff. Right before Christmas, MiniScribe cut a batch of employees loose and handed pink slips to its own witnesses. Those people went home, picked up the phone, and called the Denver newspapers. Best exit interview in American history.

The papers ran the story. In February 1989 a new management team came in under a guy named Richard Rifenburgh, and they went looking. What they found, in their own words, was a massive fraud inside a company run amok. They had to go back and redo three straight years of results. MiniScribe had owned up to one bad quarter at the end of 1988, a loss of 14 million dollars, and the real number was closer to 40. That September, the company filed for bankruptcy.

Then came the lawsuits, and for a minute the investors got everything they wanted. The people who had bought MiniScribe bonds on the strength of those fake numbers sued everybody. In February 1992 a Texas jury came back for them with more than 550 million dollars, and 200 million of that was punishment aimed straight at Coopers & Lybrand, the accountants who had signed off on a warehouse full of masonry. Hambrecht & Quist got hit too, which is a nice touch, seeing as they're the ones who found Wiles and installed him in the first place.

Days later, the judge threw the whole thing out. Not enough evidence to support it, he said, and just like that the 550 million evaporated. It was the Steve Harvey moment of corporate law. Congratulations, you've won. Sorry, wrong name. The parties settled a few months after that for about 128 million, paid out by the executives, the bank, and the accountants.

As for Wiles, a federal jury convicted him in 1994 of securities fraud and insider trading, and he went to prison. His defense was that he had no idea any of this was happening and the people below him did it on their own. The people below him testified that he knew, and it came out that he had sold a big block of his own MiniScribe shares in the spring of 1988, right before the holes started showing. The man who fired two managers in a meeting just to show everyone he was in control, somehow wasn't in control. An appeals court later tossed part of the conviction, and several of the men who actually ran the scheme testified their way into immunity, so the guys who bought the bricks mostly walked.

In the end MiniScribe was gone, a rival drive maker called Maxtor bought what was left for about 46 million dollars, Wiles lost Doctor Fix It forever, and the investors collected roughly a quarter of what a jury had promised them. And in 1988, right in the middle of all of it, MiniScribe was named the best managed company in the disk drive industry. Depending on what you mean by managed.
