# The Company That Shipped Bricks and Called Them Hard Drives

[B-ROLL: pristine hard-drive boxes stacked to the ceiling, a fortune in inventory]
In the late 1980s, a hot American tech company shipped about 26,000 brand-new hard drives out to warehouses around the world. On the books, they were worth around four million dollars. Every one had a serial number. Every one was packed, sealed, and counted. There was just one problem. [BEAT] They were bricks. Actual clay bricks. And that pile of bricks sailed straight through a professional audit.

[B-ROLL: the company riding high before any of it]
The company was MiniScribe, and for a while it was a real star. It made hard drives out of Colorado, back when the personal computer was blowing up and suddenly everybody needed somewhere to put their data. MiniScribe made the part inside the machine that held all of it, it sold them by the truckload to big names like IBM, and its stock was flying.

[B-ROLL: the turnaround celebrity brought in to save it]
Then the company hit a rough patch, and in walked the man who would define the whole story. His name was Q.T. Wiles, and in the business world of the 1980s, Wiles was a legend. He was a turnaround guy, the man you called when a company was sinking, and he'd rescued enough of them that people actually called him Doctor Fix-It. He came out of a respected investment bank called Hambrecht and Quist, and they were the ones who sent him in to run MiniScribe and turn it around. So when Wiles took over, Wall Street relaxed. The grown-up was in the room.

[B-ROLL: the fear that actually ran the building]
Except the way Wiles really ran MiniScribe was by fear. He ran it mostly from California, a thousand miles away, while juggling a handful of other companies at the same time. He set the sales targets high and refused to bend them an inch. Hit the number, and there was a fat bonus waiting. Miss it, and you were gone. By one account, he once made two of his own managers stand up in the middle of a meeting and fired them right there in front of everybody, and said he'd done it just to show the rest of the room who was in charge.

[B-ROLL: honest people cornered into a lie]
So everyone underneath Wiles was trapped. The targets were impossible. Missing them got you humiliated and fired. And the one move nobody was allowed to make was to walk in and say the numbers weren't real. So they stopped saying it. And they started faking it instead.

[B-ROLL: the first quiet trick, done with a paperclip]
It started small, and on paper. Early on, the company ran a quiet internal count and found it was a few million dollars short of what the books claimed. The honest move is to report that. Instead, a few managers wrote down a bigger, fake number. The outside auditors, a big firm called Coopers and Lybrand, had locked their own inventory records away in boxes so nobody could tamper with them. So one of the managers taught himself to pick the locks, with Allen wrenches and paper clips, cracked open the auditors' boxes, and swapped the real count for the fake one. He even fixed it so the change looked like an honest typo, a couple of digits written down in the wrong order.

[B-ROLL: paper fraud graduating into something you can hold]
That bought them some time. But a fake number on a page can only stretch so far, because sooner or later a real auditor wants to see the actual product sitting in the actual warehouse. Which is how a disk-drive company ended up buying bricks. [PAUSE]

[B-ROLL: pallets of bricks getting dressed up as product]
They went to a local supplier, the Colorado Brick Company, and bought around 26,000 bricks. They rented a second warehouse. They packed the bricks into genuine MiniScribe hard-drive boxes, gave each one a real-looking serial number, and chose bricks that weighed about what a boxed drive weighed, so a sealed box of bricks felt just like a sealed box of drives. Then they shrink-wrapped them onto pallets, exactly like real stock. From the outside, it was four million dollars of inventory. Inside, it was a rock.

[B-ROLL: the same fake units counted twice]
And they didn't just park the bricks in a corner and count them once. They shipped them. Some went overseas to Singapore. Others went out to friendly customers who had agreed, in advance, to take a pallet of sealed boxes, sit on it for a few weeks, and quietly ship it back. That let MiniScribe count the same fake drives as a sale in one quarter, then check the very same boxes back into inventory the next.

[B-ROLL: the fraud was bigger than the bricks]
And the bricks were just the part that's easy to picture. Right alongside them, the company was also taking broken drives and factory scrap, the stuff that should have been thrown out as garbage, and quietly logging it as good, sellable inventory, another nine million dollars of product that was really trash.

[B-ROLL: the check that was supposed to catch all this]
So how does a warehouse full of bricks pass an audit? The version you usually hear is that the auditors weighed the boxes, and the crooks made the bricks weigh exactly like a hard drive to beat the scale. That's not really what happened. Auditors don't weigh and open every box, there are far too many, the same reason airport security doesn't tear open every single suitcase. They do what's called a test count. They spot-check a sample and trust that the rest of the pile matches. So the bricks only ever had to survive a spot check. And the paperwork that told the auditors what was in that pile was the same paperwork the managers had already broken in and rewritten. The audit was checking a faked number against boxes it mostly never opened. It passed. MiniScribe got a clean bill of health.

[B-ROLL: the layoffs that lit the fuse]
What finally brought it down wasn't a regulator, or a sharp-eyed auditor, or Wiles suddenly growing a conscience. It was a round of layoffs. Right before the holidays, MiniScribe cut a stack of staff, and some of the people they cut were the exact people who had packed the bricks. So those people picked up the phone and called the newspapers in Denver. [BEAT] And once reporters started pulling on the thread, the whole thing unraveled fast.

[B-ROLL: the real numbers, finally]
A new management team came in, dug into the books, and found what they later described as a massive fraud at a company run amok. The real numbers were a horror show. One quarter, MiniScribe had reported a loss of around fourteen million dollars. The actual figure was closer to forty. By the time the accountants finished, the company wasn't sitting on a fortune, it was tens of millions of dollars underwater. Within about a year, the company that made the part inside your computer filed for bankruptcy, and what was left of it was sold off to a competitor, Maxtor, for around forty-six million dollars.

[B-ROLL: the investors coming for the grown-ups in the room]
Then came the reckoning, and it was a big one. Back when times were good, MiniScribe had raised money by selling bonds to investors, and those bondholders had now lost a fortune. So they sued. In 1992, a Texas jury came back with a verdict of more than five hundred and fifty million dollars. And they didn't aim it only at the executives. They put two hundred million of it on the auditors, Coopers and Lybrand, the firm that had signed off on the bricks. And another forty-five million on Hambrecht and Quist, the same bank that had sent Wiles in to save the place to begin with. The grown-ups in the room were on the hook right next to him.

[B-ROLL: the headline number quietly evaporating]
Except that's not the number anyone actually paid. Within days, the judge threw the whole verdict out, and ruled it wasn't supported by the evidence. The five hundred and fifty million everybody remembers, the record-breaker, just evaporated. The two sides settled instead, for around a hundred and twenty-eight million. The verdict everybody remembers had lasted about a week.

[B-ROLL: the man at the top, still saying he never knew]
As for Wiles, a jury convicted him in 1994 of fraud and lying to investors, and he went to prison, though an appeals court later threw out part of the conviction. And through all of it, his story never budged. He said he didn't know. The bricks, the broken-into boxes, the double-counted drives, all of it, he claimed, had happened underneath him without his knowledge. His own managers testified the opposite. And a court noted that Wiles had quietly sold off a big chunk of his own stock right before the bad numbers came out.

[B-ROLL: the bricks, the one honest thing in the building]
MiniScribe is gone now, bankrupt and sold off for parts to a competitor. But for one strange stretch in the 1980s, there were 26,000 bricks sitting in hard-drive boxes, each with its own serial number, that a major accounting firm had officially signed off as four million dollars of cutting-edge technology. [PAUSE] The hard drives were fake. The bricks were real.
