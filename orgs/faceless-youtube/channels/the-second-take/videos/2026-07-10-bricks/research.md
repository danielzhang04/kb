## Fact ledger  ← the scriptwriter may state ONLY what appears here

**The company & the man**
- **[F-01]** MiniScribe was a Colorado-based hard-disk-drive maker (HQ Longmont, CO), a hot 1980s supplier in the PC-storage boom. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* IBM-supplier framing is period-accurate but treat "IBM supplier" as general-knowledge context, lead with "hot disk-drive maker."
- **[F-02]** Q.T. (Quentin Thomas) Wiles was a venture capitalist from investment bank Hambrecht & Quist, nicknamed "Dr. Fix-It" for corporate turnarounds; he ran MiniScribe as CEO/chairman. — *Src:* S1,S2,S5 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-03]** Wiles ran MiniScribe largely remotely (from Sherman Oaks/LA) while simultaneously leading several other companies. — *Src:* S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* "four other companies" per S2; treat count as approximate.
- **[F-04]** Wiles set iron-clad sales targets he continually raised, with large bonuses for hitting them and termination for missing them. — *Src:* S2,S5 · *Conf:* high.

**Founding, growth & customers (r2 top-up)**
- **[F-25]** MiniScribe was founded in 1980 in Longmont, Colorado, by Terry Johnson, a 20-year hard-drive-industry veteran (prior roles at IBM, Memorex, and Storage Technology Corp). No source describes a garage founding. — *Src:* S1,S2,S7 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the sourced origin is "founded by a 20-year industry veteran," not a garage story; if the intro wants an origin beat, drop "garage" or reframe it as a trope this founding does not match.
- **[F-26]** No source gives a unit/volume count ("X hard drives a year") for 1985 or any other year. What IS sourced: MiniScribe's revenue actually fell to about $114 million in 1985 (down from a $125 million high in 1984) after IBM sharply cut back PC/XT-related orders in late 1984, which triggered a 26 percent staff layoff. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* this runs opposite to a "shipping big numbers to IBM by 1985" framing and predates Wiles; the well-sourced growth-to-IBM beat is 1983 to 1984 ($77M to $125M, off the original PC/XT contract), not 1985, which was the dip that brought Wiles in.
- **[F-27]** Q.T. Wiles was installed as MiniScribe's CEO and chairman in 1985, after Hambrecht & Quist invested about $20 million in the struggling company and sent their turnaround specialist to run it; he took over from founder Terry Johnson (departed late 1984) via a brief interim stretch under president Roger Gower. — *Src:* S1,S2,S5 · *Conf:* high on the year; med on the exact month (one account specifies April 1985).
- **[F-28]** MiniScribe's publicly reported revenue climbed from about $114M (1985) to $185M (1986) to $362M (1987) to $603M (1988), the year it was named the best managed company in the disk drive industry. That $603M figure, the "over 600 million dollars" peak, was itself inflated by the same fraud (channel stuffing, fabricated orders, and the brick scheme) at the time it was first reported, not just discovered fraudulent in hindsight after an honest report. — *Src:* S1,S2,S7 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-29]** MiniScribe's sourced major customers were IBM (the PC/XT supply contract, cut back late 1984) and, afterward, Compaq, confirmed as MiniScribe's major customer by 1988 across two independent accounts. One source also lists Apple as a past customer, but another account states MiniScribe bid for an Apple contract in 1988 and did not win it. — *Src:* S1,S2,S5 · *Conf:* high on Compaq; low on Apple (source conflict) · *Note:* use Compaq as the sourced second name; Apple is contested, not confirmed.

**The brick operation**
- **[F-05]** Under that pressure, managers hid an inventory shortfall by packing roughly 26,000 bricks into MiniScribe-branded hard-drive boxes with fake serial numbers. — *Src:* S1,S2,S3,S5 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* "26,000" is the consistently repeated figure across all sources.
- **[F-06]** The bricks were bought from a local supplier, the Colorado Brick Co., and packed at a rented off-site warehouse near the Longmont headquarters. — *Src:* S5 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* single strong source names "Colorado Brick Co."; the rented second Colorado warehouse is corroborated by S1,S2.
- **[F-07]** The bricks were chosen/packed to match the volume and weight of a real drive box, then shrink-wrapped onto pallets. — *Src:* S2,S5 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* this is the detail that let them pass as real product on a sampling check.
- **[F-08]** MiniScribe's books recorded the bricks as in-transit inventory worth approximately $4 million (≈$4.3M booked; ~$3.66M carried as inventory value per one account). — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* figures vary slightly by source (~$4M in-transit / $4.3M / $3.66M); state as "about four million dollars."
- **[F-09]** The bricks were shipped overseas (to Singapore) and to domestic customers who had agreed in advance to leave the pallets shrink-wrapped and ship them back weeks later — so the same fake units counted as sales in one quarter and returned the next. — *Src:* S1,S2,S5 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* customers named in one source as CompuAdd and CalAbco (S2, Conf: low on the names).
- **[F-10]** The fraud extended well beyond bricks: defective drives and scrap were also re-entered as good inventory and double-counted; one account values the scrap padding at ~$8.9M. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the bricks are the icon; the wider inventory inflation is the larger crime.

**How the auditors were beaten (the payload beat)**
- **[F-11]** Coopers & Lybrand was the independent auditor; the standard inventory check is a *test count* — sampling a representative subset, not weighing or opening every box. — *Src:* S1,S4 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* THIS is the documented mechanism; see [F-12].
- **[F-12]** When an internal January 1987 count showed a $2–4M shortfall, managers produced an inflated count and then physically broke into the auditors' locked boxes (using Allen wrenches / paper clips, per trial testimony) and swapped in the inflated numbers, disguised as transposition "human error." — *Src:* S1,S2,S5 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* investor-relations manager Warren Perry testified (1994) he learned to pick the locks.
- **[F-13]** The scheme succeeded — MiniScribe cleared the independent audit. — *Src:* S4 · *Conf:* high.

**Exposure (the revenge angle)**
- **[F-14]** The scheme unravelled when MiniScribe laid off staff before the 1988/1989 holiday shutdown — including employees who had worked on the brick scheme — and those laid-off employees called Denver-area newspapers, who broke the story. — *Src:* S1,S4 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the exposure was revenge by fired insiders, not a regulator or auditor catch.
- **[F-15]** A new management team under Richard P. Rifenburgh (took over Feb 1989) investigated and confirmed a "massive fraud" in a "company run amok," restating results for 1986–1989. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* a summer-1989 board report used the "massive fraud"/"run amok" language.
- **[F-16]** The restated reality was catastrophic: e.g. a reported Q4 1988 loss of ~$14M was actually ~$40M; a later 9-month 1989 period showed a $116.1M loss on $349.8M sales; the company reached roughly $88M negative net worth. — *Src:* S2,S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* figures are from restatement-era reporting; use one clean contrast (reported vs. real) rather than all.

**Consequences**
- **[F-17]** MiniScribe filed for bankruptcy (Chapter 11, 1989) and its assets were sold to Maxtor in 1990 for about $46M. — *Src:* S1,S2,S3 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* dates given as Sept 1989 filing / bankruptcy effective ~Jan 1990 / Maxtor deal spring 1990.
- **[F-18]** In February 1992 a Texas jury awarded MiniScribe bondholders more than $550M — including $200M punitive against Coopers & Lybrand and $45M against Hambrecht & Quist — one of the largest such verdicts of its time. — *Src:* S6 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* suit brought on behalf of ~152 investors in a $97.5M debenture offering.
- **[F-19]** Days later the trial judge overturned the $550M verdict as "not supported by sufficient evidence," and the parties then settled; a June 1992 settlement of about $128.1M was paid by MiniScribe executives, Hambrecht & Quist, and Coopers & Lybrand to creditors/shareholders. — *Src:* S3,S6 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the headline number people remember ($550M) was never actually paid; the real recovery was ~$128M. Important accuracy nuance.
- **[F-20]** Q.T. Wiles was convicted by a federal jury (1994) of securities fraud and insider trading and imprisoned; CFO Patrick Schleibaum was also convicted (June 1994); several executives (incl. Gerald Goodman, Warren Perry, Owen Taranta) received immunity for testimony. — *Src:* S1,S2,S5 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* sentence length conflicts (36 months per S1 vs. "4 yrs, served ~2.5" per S2,S5); part of Wiles' conviction was overturned on appeal in 1996 (US v. Wiles). State as "convicted and sent to prison," hedge the exact term.
- **[F-21]** The court record noted Wiles sold a significant block of his shares in April–May 1988, shortly before the shortfalls surfaced — the basis of the insider-trading count. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* phrase as what the case alleged/found, not as bare assertion.

**Connective / relational facts (ownership · cause · identity · sequence)**
- **[F-22]** Hambrecht & Quist was not just the banker — Wiles came *from* H&Q, so the same firm that installed the celebrated "turnaround" CEO later sat as a defendant paying into the settlement. — *Src:* S1,S6 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the ownership/identity link that makes the H&Q liability land.
- **[F-23]** Causal spine: Wiles' unmeetable quotas → managers falsify to survive → Wiles raises targets again → the falsification escalates from paper (lock-box swaps) to physical props (bricks) → the fired participants expose it. — *Src:* S2,S5 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* this is the escalation ladder, sourced as a described pattern, not a narrative frame.
- **[F-24]** Identity/sequence: the *same* fake units were counted twice — shipped out as "sales" one quarter, returned and re-shelved the next — i.e. the sale and the inventory were the same bricks wearing two hats. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* high.

**Scale comparison for the Reddit line (r2 top-up)**
- **[F-30]** Reddit, Inc. reported full-year 2025 revenue of about $2.2 billion (up 69 percent year over year), its first full year of GAAP profitability at about $530 million net income. — *Src:* S8 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-31]** MiniScribe's reported 1988 peak revenue of about $603 million equals roughly $1.6 to $1.7 billion in 2025 dollars after CPI inflation adjustment (about 166 to 180 percent cumulative inflation 1988 to 2025, depending on the exact months compared). — *Src:* S8,S9 · *Conf:* high on the method (multiplier varies slightly by calculator/month basis but stays in the $1.6 to $1.7 billion range) · *Note:* this means "as much money as Reddit" is approximately but not exactly true: the inflation-adjusted 1988 figure comes to roughly three-quarters of Reddit's actual 2025 revenue, same order of magnitude, not an equal one. Close enough for a comedic aside; not a precise claim.

## Cast, motive & human-cost
- **Villain / protagonist — Q.T. Wiles:** the celebrated "Dr. Fix-It" whose whole method was manufactured fear; ran the place remotely as a turnaround theater [F-02,F-03,F-04]. Motive engine: not a con man who set out to ship bricks — a numbers tyrant whose impossible quotas *produced* fraud from below and then trapped him in it [F-23]. The interiority the writer can open on: a man who believed control *was* the product.
- **The middle managers (the real brick-packers):** ordinary employees who chose forgery over firing — the human middle of the machine. One executive reportedly brought family in to help pack (attributed to Owen Taranta in one account, to the CFO in another — [conflict, Conf: low]). This is the queasy-funny heart: a family packing bricks at night to make a quarter.
- **Human cost (light, aggregate):** bondholders and shareholders lost real money — the recovery suit was on behalf of ~152 investors in a ~$97.5M debenture offering [F-18]; creditors were ultimately paid ~$128M in settlement [F-19], a fraction of paper losses; the company died and its people scattered [F-16,F-17]. State it fast and concrete; don't milk it.
- **Witnessed-absurd / telling details:** (a) the bricks came from an actual outfit named **Colorado Brick Co.** [F-06]; (b) executives broke into the auditors' locked boxes with **Allen wrenches and paper clips** [F-12]; (c) customers **agreed to leave the pallets shrink-wrapped and mail them back** so the same bricks could be counted twice [F-09].

## Reportable scenes & characterization  (NO quotes — narrator reported speech only)
- **[Q-01]** Wiles ran meetings as intimidation theater: by one account he ordered two controllers to *stand up* in front of their colleagues and then fired them on the spot, saying it was just to show everyone he was in control of the company. — *Src:* S5. *(Reported single-source scene, tellable flat under the color license, grammar §4; keep hard numbers out of it.)*
- **[Q-02]** Under the quota terror, managers didn't invent the fraud in a boardroom — it grew task by task: first swapping numbers in the auditors' lockboxes, then, when that wasn't enough, renting a warehouse and packing literal bricks. — *Src:* S2,S5. *(The escalation, as described.)*
- **[Q-03]** The fired insiders who'd packed the bricks picked up the phone to Denver newspapers — the people who built the lie ended it, out of spite. — *Src:* S1,S4.

## Verification chain (vindication lever — how the case is PROVEN)
- **What is proven / uncontested:** the brick scheme, the lock-box tampering, the audit being cleared, the bankruptcy, the Maxtor sale, the criminal convictions of Wiles and Schleibaum, and the ~$128M settlement are all documented and adjudicated [F-05,F-12,F-13,F-17,F-19,F-20]. **What stays soft:** the exact family-packer identity, exact prison term, and the "auditors literally weighed the boxes" claim (see Open questions).
- **Corroboration (independence-tagged):** the brick count + warehouse + double-count appear across four independent write-ups (Wikipedia S1, Silicon Underground S2, StorageNewsletter S3, businessblunders S5); the verdict/settlement is anchored in contemporaneous UPI wire reporting (S6) independent of the tech-history blogs.
- **Exoneration / what the obvious villain did NOT do:** Wiles' defense was that he didn't know — he claimed ignorance and blamed subordinates; middle managers testified he was aware, and the insider-trading timing counted against him [F-20,F-21]. Part of his conviction was later overturned on appeal (1996). Surface this so the writer can stage the contested "did the CEO know" beat honestly rather than flatly declaring guilt.

## Myths / misconceptions to bust
- **Myth:** "The auditors weighed the boxes, and the crooks made the bricks weigh exactly like a hard drive to fool the scale." → **Actually:** the documented defeat of the audit was a *test count* (sampling) plus physically breaking into the auditors' locked boxes to swap the numbers; the weight-matching mattered for the props passing as product, but the "auditors weighed them and signed off" story is a popular simplification not supported by the sourced record. Handle as a *bust*, not a stated fact. ([F-11,F-12] vs. the popular claim.)
- **Myth:** "Investors won $550 million." → **Actually:** the $550M jury verdict was overturned within days; the real recovery was a ~$128M settlement. ([F-18,F-19]) — strong vindication-with-a-twist payload.

## Why this matters (universality): OPTIONAL body texture, never the close
- **The rhyme:** the same shape recurs whenever a "turnaround genius" sets targets reality can't meet and the org fabricates the numbers rather than miss — later echoed at WorldCom, Enron-era earnings games, Wells Fargo's fake accounts, Wirecard's phantom cash. **The spottable tell:** inventory/assets that grow *while* the business bleeds cash, and a leader who punishes the messenger [F-16]. General-knowledge rhymes — weave only where it lands; do NOT tee up a "this repeats every generation" moral. The video should end on the story's own irony (the people who built the lie are the ones who ended it), not a lesson.

## Open questions, gaps & hedges
- **Auditors "weighing" the boxes:** the brief's seed assumed auditors physically weighed the drives and signed off. The sourced record does NOT confirm a weighing procedure — it documents test-count sampling + lock-box tampering. Treat the weighing angle as a myth to bust, not a fact to assert. (Flagged as the single most important accuracy correction for the writer.)
- **Who brought family to pack bricks:** attributed to Owen Taranta (S2) vs. the CFO (S5) — use the general beat ("an executive brought family in to pack"), hedge the name, or omit the name.
- **Wiles' exact sentence:** 36 months (S1) vs. ~4 years / served ~2.5 (S2,S5) — say "convicted and sent to prison," don't state a precise term; note the 1996 partial reversal.
- **Exact booked $ of the bricks:** ~$4M / $4.3M / $3.66M across sources — say "about four million dollars."
- **Un-fetched primary:** the SEC administrative doc on Owen D. Taranta (sec.gov/litigation/admin/34-41729) returned 403; worth a spot-check at lock for a primary-source anchor on the brick figures.
- **1985 shipment/unit volume (r2 top-up, closed as unsourceable):** no source anywhere gives a unit count ("X hard drives a year") for 1985 or any year. The sourced 1985 marker is revenue, and it is a DIP ($114M, down from $125M in 1984) after IBM cut back orders, not a growth peak; the sourced IBM-driven growth run is 1983 to 1984. If the script needs a "shipping X to IBM" beat, use a revenue figure with an explicit year (1984, not 1985), or reframe the line to avoid implying 1985 was a high point. [F-26]
- **Second major customer beyond IBM (r2 top-up, closed):** Compaq is well sourced (two independent accounts) as MiniScribe's post-IBM major customer by 1988. Apple appears in one source's customer list but conflicts with another account stating an Apple contract was pursued and not won; use Compaq, not Apple, unless a stronger source surfaces. [F-29]
- **Garage founding (r2 top-up, closed as refuted):** not supported by any source; MiniScribe was founded in 1980 by industry veteran Terry Johnson, not out of a garage. [F-25]
- **"As much as Reddit" claim (r2 top-up, closed as approximate):** MiniScribe's reported 1988 peak ($603M) is about $1.6 to $1.7 billion in today's dollars, roughly three-quarters of Reddit's actual 2025 revenue ($2.2B), not an exact match.
