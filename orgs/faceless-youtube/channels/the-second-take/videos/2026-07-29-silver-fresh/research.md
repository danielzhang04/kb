## Fact ledger  ← the scriptwriter may state ONLY what appears here

**Who & why**
- **[F-01]** Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt were sons of Texas oil billionaire H.L. Hunt; they used the family's oil fortune to buy silver. — *Src:* S1,S3 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-02]** Bunker Hunt was a zealous anti-Communist and a prominent member of the John Birch Society who bankrolled conservative causes; despite his wealth he was known for flying coach, wearing off-the-rack clothes, and driving nondescript cars. — *Src:* S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the deflate-the-powerful character detail; present as reported.
- **[F-03]** The Hunts' driving belief was that paper money was being debased by government spending and 1970s inflation, and that silver was "real money" a government could not print; that ideology, not just profit, drove the buying. — *Src:* S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* motive; documented worldview, present as their reasoning.

**How they bought it up**
- **[F-04]** They accumulated silver through a mix of physical bullion and silver futures contracts (roughly half the position was futures by end-1979), buying the futures on margin (a deposit, not full payment) so borrowed leverage magnified the position. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the leverage is the seed of the collapse.
- **[F-05]** By the end of 1979, Bunker Hunt, Herbert Hunt, and the Hunt-controlled International Metals Investment Company controlled about 195 million troy ounces of silver. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-06]** That roughly 195-million-ounce hoard is commonly described as about **half of the world's deliverable silver supply**. — *Src:* S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* THIS is the framing the title's "half" rides on; state it as *deliverable* supply.
- **[F-07]** By other measures the same hoard was about a third to two-thirds of the world's privately-held (non-government) silver: the CFTC characterized it as roughly two-thirds of the identifiable privately-held supply, while other counts put it near a third. — *Src:* S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the figure is genuinely denominator-dependent; hedge, and don't claim "half of ALL the silver on Earth."
- **[F-41] (top-up — THE DEFINITIVE HALF-VS-THIRD RESOLUTION)** The same ~195–215-million-ounce Hunt-consortium hoard produces wildly different headline fractions depending on the denominator, and all of the following are sourced: (a) against **total world above-ground silver stock** — all forms, including jewelry, silverware, coinage, and industrial holdings, estimated at roughly **12 billion ounces** — the Hunts controlled only about **2%**; (b) against **"investable" above-ground silver bullion/coin** specifically (about **2.8 billion ounces**, per CPM Group's 2018 Silver Yearbook), they controlled roughly **9%**; (c) against the world's **deliverable / exchange-tradable silver supply** — the narrower pool actually relevant to a market "corner," and the framing multiple secondary sources converge on — the Hunts controlled **roughly half**; (d) against **privately-held (non-government-vaulted) silver** specifically, estimates range from about **a third to two-thirds**, with the CFTC's own characterization cited at roughly two-thirds of identifiable privately-held supply. **Verdict: the title's "half" is defensible ONLY under framing (c), the deliverable/tradable-supply denominator — it is NOT true of all silver on Earth (that's closer to 2%).** The script must state the deliverable-supply qualifier explicitly and early (not just as fine print), e.g. "about half of all the silver that could actually change hands on the world's exchanges" — never an unqualified "half the world's silver." If the production wants a single hedge-free number instead, "roughly a third of the world's privately-held silver" is the more conservative, more universally-defensible headline, at the cost of losing the cleaner "half" hook. — *Src:* S1,S2,S6,S7 · *Conf:* med (the framing-dependence itself is well corroborated; each individual percentage carries its own source-quality caveats noted in F-06/F-07).
- **[F-08]** Early on, in 1973, the brothers bought tens of millions of ounces and flew much of it to Switzerland on chartered planes guarded by armed Texas ranch hands. — *Src:* S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the signature absurd image.
- **[F-09]** To crew that silver airlift, Bunker's brother-in-law Randy Kreiling reportedly recruited about a dozen cowboys from a ranch by holding a shooting match, and the best shots won the job of riding shotgun on the silver to Switzerland. — *Src:* S3 · *Conf:* low · *Note:* colorful, single-sourced secondary; present as "the story goes," or use lightly.
- **[F-10]** The Hunts also took on partners, working with a Saudi sheikh thought to represent the royal family, and channeled buying through the International Metals Investment Company. — *Src:* S1,S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* IMIC is the Hunt-controlled entity; the Saudi-partner detail is secondary, hedge.
- **[F-29] (top-up)** The Saudi/IMIC partnership was not just extra capital — it was a structural workaround: U.S. exchanges capped how much silver any one domestic trader could hold, so the Hunts brought in Saudi Arabian investors (described as connected to the royal family) and routed a large share of the buying through IMIC, an entity incorporated in Bermuda, i.e. offshore and outside a single domestic trader's position limit. The combined Hunt-Saudi-IMIC consortium is what reached the ~195–215M-oz range by 1979, not the Hunt brothers' personal accounts alone. — *Src:* S7 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the "why IMIC existed" causal beat; the Saudi partners' identities remain undocumented beyond "royal-connected," hedge.

**The buildup years, 1973–79 (top-up)**
- **[F-26] (top-up)** The Hunts' silver buying did not start in 1979 — it began in 1973, when silver traded at roughly $2 an ounce, well before the 1979–80 squeeze; accounts differ on the exact first-wave volume (one puts 1973 purchases at "over 35 million ounces," another puts the position at "55 million ounces" by 1974), so state it as "tens of millions of ounces" rather than a single precise figure. — *Src:* S6,S7 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* contradiction flagged — 35M vs 55M vary by source; both agree the buildup predates 1979 by six years.
- **[F-27] (top-up)** The Hunts' father, oil billionaire H.L. Hunt, died in April 1974; the inheritance the brothers came into is described as freeing up substantially more capital that accelerated their silver buying through the mid-to-late 1970s. — *Src:* S6 · *Conf:* med.
- **[F-28] (top-up)** The 1973 Swiss silver shipment moved roughly 40 million ounces on three chartered Boeing 707s from Chicago and New York to Zurich; the armed escort was recruited from the Hunt family's Circle K Ranch in Texas (Bunker's brother-in-law Randy Kreiling reportedly picked the crew via a shooting match, per F-09), and on arrival the silver was split among six separate storage locations across Switzerland. — *Src:* S8 · *Conf:* low-med · *Note:* this refines/names the ranch behind F-08/F-09's "Texas ranch hands" — still secondary color, present as "reportedly."

**The run-up**
- **[F-11]** Silver traded below about $10 an ounce before August 1979, then the Hunts' buying helped drive it up through the autumn. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-12]** By 31 December 1979, silver was about $34.45 an ounce; in January 1980 it peaked above $50 an ounce. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the ~$6-to-nearly-$50 run. **Precise figures (top-up):** silver opened 1979 at about $6.08/oz (2 Jan 1979) and reached a London-fix high of $49.45/oz on 18 January 1980, with COMEX futures touching an intraday all-time high near $50.35/oz — a roughly 713% run in just over a year. Use "$6 to nearly $50" as the safe rounded line; "$49.45" is the citable exact figure if the script wants one. — *Src:* S1.
- **[F-13]** As the price climbed, the paper value of the Hunts' silver position rose from about $1.1 billion in mid-1979 to roughly $6.8 billion by January 1980. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.

**The house changes the rules**
- **[F-14]** In January 1980 the exchanges moved against the position: COMEX imposed position limits on silver futures (around 7 January), and on 21 January COMEX restricted silver trading to liquidation orders only (no new long positions), a rule change widely referred to as Silver Rule 7. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the "Silver Rule 7" name is the common label; the substance (liquidation-only) is documented.
- **[F-15]** The Hunts and their defenders argued the rule change was the exchange saving its own insiders: the boards included traders who were short silver and stood to lose if the price kept rising, a conflict-of-interest claim that is the story's core "the house rigged it" beat. — *Src:* S2,S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* present as the Hunts' argument / the alleged conflict, not as settled fact.
- **[F-16]** With new buying blocked and the price sliding, the position that leverage had inflated now worked in reverse: falling prices triggered margin calls the Hunts had to meet in cash. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the causal hinge.
- **[F-14a] (top-up)** "Silver Rule 7" is the common label for a two-step COMEX action, not one single rule with one date: on 7 January 1980 the exchange, using emergency authority, imposed new speculative position limits (some accounts specify 500 contracts spot/next-month and 2,000 contracts all-months-combined, down from a prior 5,000-contract ceiling); on 21 January 1980 the COMEX board went further and restricted silver trading to liquidation orders only (banning new long positions outright); margin requirements were also raised sharply in the same window (secondary accounts cite roughly 10% to over 50%). If the script needs one date for "the rule that broke them," 7 January 1980 is when position limits first bit; 21 January 1980 is when it became a full new-buying ban. — *Src:* S1,S7 · *Conf:* med-high · *Note:* exact percentage/contract figures vary slightly by source; the two-step sequence and both dates are corroborated across sources.

**Silver Thursday & the bailout**
- **[F-17]** On 27 March 1980, Silver Thursday, silver collapsed to about $10.80 an ounce; by 25–26 March Herbert Hunt had told creditors the Hunts no longer had the cash or collateral to meet their margin calls. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-18]** Their broker Bache was left with an unsecured debit of about $122 million in the Hunt accounts, and the threat that a Hunt default could cascade through Wall Street brokers. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-19]** To contain the damage, a consortium of thirteen banks arranged a roughly $1.1 billion loan to the Hunt family company Placid Oil, secured by the family's oil, gas, and pipeline assets and guaranteed by the brothers, to cover their obligations. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the irony beat — "free-market" silver bulls rescued by a bank bailout.
- **[F-33] (top-up)** The thirteen-bank rescue consortium (F-19) is described in secondary accounts as led by Citibank and Bank of America; one such account adds that collateral extended beyond the well-corroborated oil/gas/pipeline assets to include art and racehorses. — *Src:* S7 · *Conf:* low-med · *Note:* the Citibank/BofA-led framing appears in one secondary source only; the art/racehorses collateral detail is weaker still (single source) — hedge both, and lean on S1's oil/gas/pipeline collateral (F-19) as the well-corroborated version if the script needs one clean line.
- **[F-30] (top-up)** The Hunts' silver futures were not carried at one broker — accounts were spread across at least five futures commission merchants: Bache Halsey Stuart Shields (the primary broker, and the one left most exposed), ContiCommodity Services, ACLI International Commodity Services, E.F. Hutton, Merrill Lynch, and Blyth Eastman Dillon. A Hunt default therefore threatened to cascade losses through multiple major Wall Street firms simultaneously, which is why regulators and the Fed treated it as a systemic-risk event, not just one broker's problem. — *Src:* S9 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the SEC's own 1982 staff report on the crisis is the primary source for this broker list but returned a 403/404 on direct fetch this pass; the list is corroborated by secondary write-ups and worth a primary spot-check at lock.
- **[F-31] (top-up)** By 27 March 1980 the Hunts' and IMIC's combined silver-related debt is reported at over $1.2 billion; separately, some accounts describe the built position's peak notional value at roughly $4.5 billion against under $400 million of the Hunts' own capital originally committed — leverage compounding leverage. — *Src:* S9 · *Conf:* low-med · *Note:* these figures come from secondary synthesis, not the same primary source as F-13's $1.1B→$6.8B mark-to-market figures; treat as directionally consistent (the position was leveraged many times over) rather than reconciling exact dollar amounts across sources.
- **[F-32] (top-up)** Federal Reserve chairman Paul Volcker did not personally negotiate or arrange the Hunt rescue loan — under sworn testimony he said he neither initiated nor guided the loan talks — but in the weeks before Silver Thursday he had quietly urged commercial banks to curb lending for precious-metals speculation, consistent with the Fed's broader anti-inflation Special Credit Restraint Program announced 14 March 1980 (higher marginal reserve requirements, a discount-window surcharge on large banks). Bankers reportedly read the Fed's close, continual monitoring of the Hunt situation as tacit approval for the eventual bank rescue. This is the accurate shape of the "Fed-blessed bailout" claim: proximity and monitoring, not a documented Fed order to lend. — *Src:* S6,S7 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* present as "the Fed watched and implicitly signaled support," not "the Fed ordered the bailout" — the stronger claim isn't supported.

**Aftermath**
- **[F-20]** In 1988 a federal jury (in a suit brought by the Peruvian minerals company Minpeco) found Bunker, Herbert, and others liable for conspiring to corner the silver market, resulting in a judgment of about $134 million after adjustments. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-21]** In September 1988 Bunker and Herbert Hunt filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, among the largest personal bankruptcies in Texas at the time. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-22]** In 1989 the brothers settled charges with the CFTC and were fined and barred from trading commodities. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high. **(top-up)** Secondary accounts put the fine at $10 million against Bunker Hunt (a comparable figure for Herbert) plus a lifetime bar from commodities trading. — *Src:* S6 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the $10M figure is secondary, not from S1's primary-adjacent record; use "multi-million-dollar fine and a lifetime trading ban" if a hedge is preferred over the precise number.

**Aftermath, continued — Bunker's later life, the payoff beats (top-up)**
- **[F-35]** After the September 1988 bankruptcy filing, Bunker Hunt's assets were liquidated to pay creditors and the IRS; his thoroughbred racing operation was dispersed in a November 1988 Keeneland sale of 580 horses that grossed about $46.9 million — at the time the largest single thoroughbred dispersal sale in auction history. — *Src:* S4 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-36]** Bunker Hunt's other major post-bankruptcy liquidation was his extensive collection of ancient Greek, Roman, and Islamic coins and antiquities, sold at a series of Sotheby's auctions (including a June 1990 New York sale) to help settle his debts. **No source found in this research supports a "stamp collection"** — the well-documented collection is ancient coins/antiquities, not stamps; if a stamp-collection beat was assumed anywhere downstream, correct it to coins. — *Src:* S4, corroborated by auction-house/museum listings (Sotheby's catalogue references, J. Paul Getty Museum provenance records) · *Conf:* high on "coins not stamps," medium on precise auction totals.
- **[F-37]** Nelson Bunker Hunt died on 21 October 2014 in Dallas, Texas, at age 88; reported causes were cancer and dementia, and he died at an assisted-living facility. William Herbert Hunt outlived him. — *Src:* S4 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-38]** In a widely repeated exchange reported contemporaneously by UPI in March 1985, when confronted about how the family's fortune could have collapsed so far, Bunker Hunt was asked how anyone could lose a billion dollars and reportedly replied: "Well, a billion dollars just ain't what it used to be." The same UPI piece frames the Hunt family fortune as falling from about $8 billion in 1980 to roughly $1 billion by 1988 — driven not only by the silver collapse but also by Libya's 1973 nationalization of Hunt Oil's Libyan operations, falling oil prices, and troubled real-estate and sugar ventures. — *Src:* S5 · *Conf:* med-high on the quote and its contemporaneous attribution (UPI wire, 1985); med on the precise setting (retellings vary between "a Senate committee" and "a reporter" asking the question) — present the line as reported, hedge the exact venue.
- **[F-39]** Tiffany & Co.'s chairman, Walter Hoving, placed a full-page advertisement in The New York Times on 26 March 1980 — the day before Silver Thursday, while the Hunts still appeared to be winning — stating it was "unconscionable for anyone to hoard several billion, yes billion, dollars' worth of silver" and drive up prices on everyday silver goods "from baby spoons to tea sets," as well as photographic film and other products. — *Src:* S10 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* corroborated across multiple independent secondary retellings and a photograph of the original ad circulated online, but not verified against a primary NYT archive fetch in this pass — worth a direct NYT-archive spot-check at lock if the exact wording will be quoted on screen.

**Connective / relational facts (ownership · cause · identity · sequence)**
- **[F-23]** The money that bought the silver was oil money: the Hunts converted an oil fortune into the world's largest silver hoard, so when silver collapsed it was the oil empire (Placid Oil's assets) that had to be pledged to save them. — *Src:* S1,S3 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-24]** Leverage is the whole hinge: the same borrowed margin that let two men buy up half the deliverable silver is exactly what destroyed them, because a leveraged position that soars on the way up faces ruinous margin calls on the way down. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-25]** Sequence: buy quietly with leverage → the price soars → the exchange changes the rules to liquidation-only → new buying stops and the price reverses → margin calls the Hunts can't meet → the crash and the bank bailout. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-42] (top-up)** Identity/irony beat: Tiffany & Co. — a company whose own product line (silverware, jewelry) was directly hurt by the price spike — took out a public ad *attacking* the Hunts one day before the Hunts' own position collapsed on its own [F-39]; the "vindication" the ad called for arrived within 24 hours, for reasons unconnected to the ad itself. — *Src:* S1,S10 · *Conf:* med.
- **[F-43] (top-up)** Relational chain, brokers: the Hunts' leverage wasn't contained to two men — it was distributed across at least five brokerage firms [F-30], which is why a Hunt default was treated as a *systemic* Wall Street risk (the reason a 13-bank, Fed-monitored rescue happened at all) rather than one broker's bad debt. — *Src:* S9 · *Conf:* med.

## Myths / misconceptions to bust
- **Myth:** "They owned half the silver in the world." → **Actually:** about half the world's *deliverable* silver (and a third to two-thirds of privately-held silver depending on the count), but only about **2%** of all above-ground silver stock and ~9% of "investable" bullion/coin stock — the "half" number only survives with the deliverable-supply qualifier attached. Keep the "half" honest by naming that framing every time it's used. ([F-05,F-06,F-07,F-41])
- **Myth:** "The market naturally corrected." → **Actually:** the collapse was triggered by a deliberate rule change (liquidation-only trading) that stopped new buying, a human decision the Hunts say protected the exchange's own insiders. ([F-14,F-15])
- **Myth (top-up):** "The Federal Reserve ordered/arranged the Hunt bailout." → **Actually:** a 13-bank consortium arranged the $1.1B loan; Volcker testified he neither initiated nor guided those talks. What's true is narrower and still ironic: the Fed was tightening credit everywhere else at the same moment and had quietly discouraged banks from lending into precious-metals speculation, and bankers read the Fed's close monitoring as tacit cover for the rescue. Don't overstate it as a formal Fed directive. ([F-19,F-32])
- **Myth (top-up):** "Bunker Hunt's post-bankruptcy collection was stamps." → **Actually:** no source in this research supports a stamp collection; the documented liquidation was an extensive collection of ancient Greek, Roman, and Islamic coins and antiquities, sold via Sotheby's. ([F-36])

## Why this matters (universality): OPTIONAL body texture, never the close
- **The rhyme:** the leverage trap, a bet so big it can only work if the price keeps going up, that turns a fortune into a margin call the instant it reverses; the same shape recurs in every blow-up from LTCM to the 2021 meme-stock short squeezes. **The spottable tell:** anyone who has to keep buying to keep the price up, on borrowed money, has already lost, they just don't know when.
