# Script: How Two Brothers Bought Up Half the World's Silver

- **Source idea:** ST-012
- **Target length:** 8-10 min
- **Voice:** Miles (vSjOBQp24DUB2COr2xI9)
- **Estimated runtime:** 9:04

---

So there were these two brothers, Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt. Texas oil money, the kind you inherit. And by January of 1980, the two of them and their partners had quietly bought up about half of all the silver in the world that could change hands. Ten weeks later a broker rang them up asking for cash, and they didn't have it.

They started buying in 1973, when silver went for about two dollars an ounce and absolutely nobody cared. Tens of millions of ounces to begin with, and then they just kept going, year after year, for the rest of the decade. By the end of 1979 the Hunts and their partners were sitting on around 195 million ounces of the stuff, and all that buying helped drive the price from about six dollars an ounce to nearly fifty in a little over a year. Their pile had been worth about a billion dollars that summer. By that January the silver alone was worth almost seven billion. On paper.

Add up every silver thing ever dug out of the ground, the jewelry, the tea sets, the coins in your grandmother's drawer, the industrial stuff sitting in factories, and the brothers had roughly two percent of it. But almost NONE of that silver is for sale. The pile that can actually show up at an exchange and get handed to a buyer is a lot smaller. Of that pile they had about half.

So why silver? Because Bunker Hunt didn't believe in money. Not the folding kind, anyway. He was a John Birch Society man, an anti-Communist true believer who bankrolled conservative causes all over the country, and he was convinced that Washington was printing dollars into worthlessness and that the only honest money left was a metal no government could run off a press. For a man with an absurd amount of money, he barely spent any of it: he flew coach, wore off the rack, drove cars nobody looked at twice. And in 1974 his father, the oil billionaire H.L. Hunt, died, which freed up a great deal more money for a man who thought money was fake.

Now rewind to 1973, because the buying had already started, and it started weird. The Hunts bought tens of millions of ounces at around two dollars, then chartered three Boeing 707s, loaded roughly forty million ounces of it onto them in Chicago and New York, and flew the lot to Zurich. The guards were cowboys. Ranch hands off the family's own Circle K Ranch in Texas, picked by Bunker's brother-in-law, who held a shooting match and gave the job to whoever could shoot straightest. That's not a hiring process, that's a Western. And when it landed, the silver got split across six different storage spots around Switzerland.

So how do two guys buy up half a market? Not with cash, mostly. By the end of 1979 about half of what they held wasn't silver at all, it was silver futures, which are just contracts where somebody promises to hand you silver at a set price on a set date. And you don't pay for those up front. You put ten percent down, like a house, and your broker fronts the rest. None of this is investment advice, by the way. So a dollar of oil money could hold a whole lot more silver than it could ever buy. On the way up, that makes you look like a genius.

Except that shouldn't have worked. The American exchanges cap how much of one metal any single trader is allowed to hold, specifically so no one guy can sit on the whole market. So the Hunts got themselves some partners. They brought in Saudi investors connected to the royal family, and they routed a big chunk of the buying through a company called the International Metals Investment Company, incorporated in Bermuda. Different name, different country, same pile of silver. The paperwork lived in Bermuda. The silver never did. On the exchange's books nobody was holding too much.

A corner only pays if the other side has to come to you, and by January 1980 they did. Everybody who had sold silver they didn't own now had to go out and buy it to settle up, from the men holding it, at whatever number those men liked. It's Ticketmaster, except they owned every seat in the building. The traders on the other side of those contracts were staring at ruin. So the exchange changed the rules.

On the seventh of January 1980, COMEX, the New York exchange where silver futures get traded, declared an emergency and cut the number of contracts anybody was allowed to hold. Two weeks later the board went all the way. As of the twenty-first of January, silver could be traded on liquidation orders only. You could sell. You could not buy. People call it Silver Rule 7, and if that sounds vaguely familiar, it's because in 2021 Robinhood took the buy button off GameStop and half the internet lost its mind. Same move, forty years earlier, on a metal instead of a meme stock.

The Hunts said the game was rigged. The men who voted that rule through sat on the exchange's board, and some of them were the very traders who had sold silver they didn't have, the ones who were finished if the price kept climbing. The brothers' version was simple: the referees had money riding on the game. New buying stopped dead, and the price of silver, which had spent a year going nowhere but up, turned around and went the other way.

All that borrowed money that had multiplied their silver on the way up multiplied it exactly as hard on the way down, and every time the price dropped the brokers wanted more cash in the account by the end of the day. That's a margin call, and you can't pay one in metal. They wanted dollars. The fake ones. So there sat the Hunts, on top of the only honest money in the world, and the one thing nobody would take from them was silver.

On the twenty-sixth of March, the chairman of Tiffany took out a full-page ad in the New York Times calling it unconscionable that anyone would hoard several billion dollars of silver and shove up the price of everything from baby spoons to tea sets to the film in your camera. Tiffany, of course, sells silver, and every one of those tea sets had just gotten more expensive to make. And it ran a day too late to matter, because by the time it hit the newsstand Herbert Hunt had already told the family's creditors there was no more cash and no more collateral.

The next day was Thursday the twenty-seventh of March, and silver fell to about eleven bucks an ounce. A year of climbing, gone in a day. People have called it Silver Thursday ever since. Their main broker, a firm called Bache, was left holding around 120 million dollars of Hunt debt with nothing behind it, and Bache wasn't even the only one exposed: the brothers' accounts were spread across five different Wall Street firms. Which is how two Texans with a metal fixation turned into everybody's problem.

Because if the Hunts went down, the brokers went with them, and nobody in Washington wanted to find out what happened after that. So thirteen banks put together a loan of about 1.1 billion dollars to Placid Oil, the family's oil company, secured against the family's oil, its gas and its pipelines, and guaranteed by the brothers personally. Which means the two men who thought banks and paper dollars were a con on the American people got out of this by borrowing a billion of them. Hard money, my ass. The Fed had quietly told banks to stop lending money for metal speculation, and then watched the Hunt situation very, very closely, and the bankers read the watching as permission.

The bill took eight years to arrive. In 1988 a federal jury, in a case brought by a Peruvian minerals company, found that Bunker, Herbert and others had conspired to corner the silver market, and hung about 134 million dollars in damages on them. That September the brothers filed for Chapter 11, which is the kind of bankruptcy where nobody shuts you down, a court just takes charge of who gets paid what. Everything's bigger in Texas, and this was one of the biggest personal bankruptcies the state had ever seen.

Then they sold his things. In November 1988 they walked 580 of Bunker's thoroughbred racehorses through a sale ring at Keeneland for about 47 million dollars, the biggest horse sale anybody had ever held up to that point. His collection of ancient Greek, Roman and Islamic coins went to Sotheby's. Fifteen years earlier the man had been flying silver to Zurich under armed guard because he wanted money a government couldn't touch, and he ended up paying off his creditors with Roman coins.

The year after that they settled with the commodities regulator, paid a fine, and were barred from trading commodities for life. Bunker Hunt died in 2014, at eighty-eight, and Herbert outlived him. And years before the bankruptcy, somebody asked Bunker how a family fortune goes from around eight billion dollars down to about one billion, and he didn't get defensive about it even a little. He said a billion dollars just ain't what it used to be.
