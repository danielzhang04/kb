At a major American bank, employees opened as many as three and a half million accounts and credit cards that customers never asked for. Real people got signed up for things they'd never heard of, and some of them got charged fees for it. And when you go looking for the villain who cooked this up, the mastermind sitting in a back room deciding to do it, there isn't one. What you find instead is a number, picked years earlier because it rhymed with the word great.

The bank was Wells Fargo. And for years, it sold Wall Street on being the best in the business at something called cross-selling. Cross-selling just means getting a customer who already has, say, a checking account to also take a credit card, and a savings account, and an online login, and maybe some insurance. More products per person. Wells Fargo boiled that down to a single scorecard number: how many products the average household had. They reported it to investors as proof of how well the bank was run. And they had a target for it.

The target was eight. Eight products in every household. Back in 1999 the bank had a whole internal campaign built around it, called Going for Gr-eight. And the reason it was eight, and not seven or ten, was partly that eight rhymed with great.   That was the real reason. A rhyme. And the goal was older than most of the people who'd get blamed for the scandal. It traces back to a banker named Richard Kovacevich, who'd built the cross-selling machine at a bank called Norwest before Norwest merged into Wells Fargo.

But once a bonus rides on a number like that, people stop trying to serve the customer and start trying to move the number. And the pressure rolled downhill, off the scorecard, past the executives, and onto the people standing at the counter who had a quota to hit by the end of the day.

A teller who needed one more product to hit the day's number could open a checking account the customer never asked for. To make it look real and active, they'd move a little of that customer's own money into it. They'd set the PIN on the new debit card to 0000, so they controlled it. And they'd invent an email address, sometimes their own, to sign the customer up for online banking, so the alerts that might have tipped anyone off went somewhere nobody would ever see.  [short pause]  The move even had a nickname inside the bank. Pinning.

It wasn't just checking accounts. Employees ordered debit cards and credit cards in customers' names, around 565,000 credit cards nobody had authorized, some of which quietly ran up annual fees and interest and dinged people's credit scores. They signed customers up for insurance they didn't want, renters' insurance, life insurance. And years later, when it settled with the Justice Department, the bank admitted that employees had created false records and used customers' personal information without permission. The forged consent the scandal became famous for wasn't a rumor. The bank admitted it.

The first tally came out of a review of about 93 million accounts opened between 2011 and 2015. It found around 2.1 million that might not have been authorized. Then in 2017 the bank ran a wider review, reaching back to 2009 and forward to 2016, about 165 million accounts this time, and the number climbed to as many as three and a half million. Now, that jump got reported as the fraud doubling. It didn't. The bigger review went back over those original years too and raised their count from 2.1 million to about 2.55 million, and the newly added years brought in about 981,000 more. Same fraud. They'd just counted more of it. Of all those accounts, about 190,000 actually got hit with real fees. And as many as matters in every one of those numbers, because they're the bank's own estimate of accounts that might have been unauthorized, not a hard count of confirmed fakes.

Somebody paid for all of this, and at first it was the employees. Between 2011 and 2016, Wells Fargo fired about 5,300 people over sales-practice violations, and referred more than 23,000 for investigation. And these were, overwhelmingly, the people at the counter. Tellers and personal bankers. Not anyone upstairs.

When the board finally ran its own independent investigation, it found that senior management had looked at firing roughly one percent of the sales force every single year for this kind of cheating, and had treated that as an acceptable cost of doing business.  [short pause]  So the firings were never a shock at headquarters. Year after year, thousands of people were caught gaming the number, and the people who owned the number had decided that was a price worth paying to keep it climbing. Nobody had to sit in a room and order a fraud. It took a target that couldn't be hit honestly, a bonus for hitting it anyway, and a quiet tolerance for firing whoever got caught.

Some employees refused to play along. They called the company's ethics line, the number you're supposed to call, to report exactly what was happening. In cases that later came to light, the people who called got fired. And on their way out, the bank sometimes filed the industry's standard termination form in a way that followed them to the next job and made them hard to hire anywhere else. In 2017 the Labor Department ordered Wells Fargo to reinstate a wealth-management banker it had fired back in 2011 for objecting to accounts being opened without consent, and to pay that banker about 5.4 million dollars. At the time it was the largest award the agency had ever ordered for a single whistleblower. It ordered the bank to take back a branch manager in Pomona, California, fired after complaining that salespeople were opening unauthorized accounts to hit targets, and to pay that manager about 577,000 dollars.

The outside world found out in 2016. On September 8th, the regulators came down together. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau fined the bank 100 million dollars, the biggest penalty it had ever handed out. The OCC, the federal regulator that oversees national banks, added 35 million. The city and county of Los Angeles took another 50. About 185 million dollars in a single day. Later that same month, the CEO, John Stumpf, sat in front of the Senate Banking Committee and had to answer for all of it, including the part where the target that started everything had been picked partly because eight rhymed with great. He resigned that October.

Then in 2018 came something nobody had done to a bank before. On February 2nd, on Janet Yellen's last day as its chair, the Federal Reserve capped how big Wells Fargo was allowed to get. It froze the bank's total assets right where they stood at the end of 2017, around 1.95 trillion dollars, and said the cap stayed on until the bank fixed how it was run. The Fed had never put a ceiling on an entire company like that. It stayed on for more than seven years, and only came off in 2025.

In 2020 the bank settled the criminal and civil investigations for three billion dollars. It admitted, on the record, that the misconduct had run from 2002 all the way to 2016, that it had collected fees and interest it never should have, and that employees had faked records and misused people's personal information. Five hundred million of that three billion was set aside to pay back investors.

So what happened to the people who owned the number? For a while, almost nothing. Carrie Tolstedt had run Wells Fargo's Community Bank, the retail arm that set and enforced the sales targets, for roughly a decade ending in 2016. She had announced her retirement months before the scandal blew up, and the bank had praised her on the way out. Both she and Stumpf were, at first, set to leave with their pay intact. It took months of public and political pressure before the board went and clawed it back. In the end it forced Stumpf to give up about 69 million dollars, and Tolstedt about 67 million, and it fired her for cause, after the fact.

And the regulators weren't finished. In 2020 the OCC banned Stumpf from the banking industry for life and fined him 17.5 million dollars, a record for a case like that. It brought actions against eight former executives in all. The SEC charged Stumpf too, and he settled for 2.5 million. Tolstedt settled with the SEC in 2023 for a 3 million dollar penalty, plus giving back money she'd been paid while the numbers were inflated. The SEC's case against her was that from 2014 to 2016 she had publicly championed that cross-sell number to investors as proof the bank was the best in the business, while it was being pumped up by accounts that were unused, unneeded, or flat-out unauthorized.

And out of everyone, in the entire scandal, exactly one person was criminally charged. Carrie Tolstedt. But not for the fraud. In 2023 she pleaded guilty to a single count of obstructing a bank examination, getting in the way of the regulators as they looked into it. That September, a judge sentenced her to three years of probation, six months of home confinement, a 100,000 dollar fine, and 120 hours of community service. No prison. The judge turned down the prosecutors' request for a year behind bars, and said she'd been singled out.

So the whole affair produced exactly one criminal conviction. And it wasn't for opening a single fake account. It was for getting in the way of the people investigating them. Nobody was ever convicted of the fraud itself, because in the end nobody had exactly ordered it. There was just a target the whole bank had been chasing, a number somebody picked back in 1999 because it rhymed with the word great. The people who called the ethics line to report it lost their jobs. And the number that started all of it never had to answer for anything.
