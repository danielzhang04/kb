- **[F-01]** The "eight products per household" goal was formalized at Wells Fargo as the internal campaign **"Going for Gr-eight" in 1999** (per its 1999 annual report); the number eight was chosen partly because it rhymed with "great," and the cross-selling strategy traces to Richard Kovacevich, who built it at Norwest before Norwest merged with Wells Fargo. — *Src:* [S12] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the rhyme and the goal **predate** John Stumpf's CEO tenure — attribute the *origin* to the 1999 campaign / Kovacevich, not to Stumpf.
- **[F-02]** CEO John Stumpf publicly articulated and defended the eight-products goal, including in a 2010 shareholder letter, and was widely criticized for the "eight rhymes with great" rationale when he was questioned at the September 2016 Senate Banking Committee hearing. — *Src:* [S12] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* he owned and championed the target; he did not coin it. Phrase as "the rationale he gave," which is on the public record.
- **[F-03]** Wells Fargo reported a "cross-sell ratio" (average number of products per customer household) as a headline performance metric to investors, presented as a measure of the bank's success. — *Src:* [S5] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-04]** On **September 8, 2016**, the CFPB fined Wells Fargo **$100 million** for "the widespread illegal practice of secretly opening unauthorized accounts," the largest penalty the CFPB had then imposed. — *Src:* [S1] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-05]** The same day, the OCC assessed a **$35 million** civil money penalty and the City and County of Los Angeles received **$50 million**, for a combined **$185 million** in 2016 penalties. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-06]** The bank's original third-party analysis reviewed ~93.5 million accounts opened **May 2011 – mid-2015** and identified **~2.1 million potentially unauthorized accounts** — about **1,534,280 unauthorized deposit accounts and 565,433 credit-card accounts**. — *Src:* [S1],[S4] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* this is the original "2.1 million" figure; keep the window (2011–2015) attached so it isn't confused with the later expanded number.
- **[F-07]** To open unauthorized deposit accounts, employees **transferred funds out of customers' authorized accounts** into new ones without consent, a practice that sometimes triggered overdraft, insufficient-funds and monthly-maintenance fees. — *Src:* [S1],[S2] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-08]** Employees used a technique nicknamed **"pinning"** — setting a customer's debit-card PIN to **0000** — to control accounts and enroll customers in online banking without their knowledge. — *Src:* [S3] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the single most concrete, showable mechanic; the PIN literally being "0000" is the sharp detail.
- **[F-09]** Employees **created phony email addresses** (often using their own contact information) to enroll customers in online banking and to keep the customer from discovering the account. — *Src:* [S1],[S3] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-10]** Employees **requested and issued debit cards, and created PINs, without customers' knowledge or consent**, and applied for roughly **565,433 credit-card accounts** that customers had not authorized — some of which incurred annual fees, interest charges, and hits to customers' credit. — *Src:* [S1],[S7] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-11]** Beyond bank accounts and cards, employees also enrolled customers in **unwanted insurance products**, including renters' insurance (Assurant) and life insurance (Prudential). — *Src:* [S3] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* per the sourced record; the Prudential thread later produced its own fired-whistleblower story [F-22].
- **[F-12]** In its 2020 deferred-prosecution agreement Wells Fargo **admitted** that employees had, among other things, **created false bank records and misused customers' personal information (identity theft)** — i.e., the forging of consent/signatures the scandal is known for is an admitted fact, not merely an allegation. — *Src:* [S7] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* phrase as the company's own admission in the DPA; this is the defamation-safe way to state "they forged signatures."
- **[F-13]** On **August 31, 2017**, an **expanded review by PwC** covering ~165 million retail accounts opened **January 2009 – September 2016** raised the total to **~3.5 million (about 3.53 million) potentially unauthorized accounts** — composed of **2.55 million** from the original window plus **981,000** from the newly-added periods. — *Src:* [S4] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* THE headline number. The jump from 2.1M to 3.5M is a *wider review window* (2009–2016 vs 2011–2015), not a second wave of fraud — the writer must not imply the fraud doubled; the *counting* widened.
- **[F-14]** Under the expanded 3.5-million figure, about **190,000 accounts** had incurred fees and charges (up from ~130,000 previously), and Wells Fargo provided **$2.8 million** in additional refunds on top of **$3.3 million** already refunded. — *Src:* [S4] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-15]** Between **2011 and 2016**, Wells Fargo **fired about 5,300 employees** for sales-practices violations (including falsifying records), and referred **more than 23,000** employees for sales-practices investigation. — *Src:* [S12] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the 5,300 are overwhelmingly rank-and-file branch staff — central to the "the target, not a mastermind, was the perpetrator" payload.
- **[F-16]** The board's April 2017 independent investigation recorded that senior management had treated firing roughly **1% of the Community Bank sales workforce per year** for sales-integrity violations as an acceptable/tolerable cost of the sales model. — *Src:* [S11] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the "1% is fine" attitude is the smoking gun that the losses were a *designed feature*, not a surprise.
- **[F-17]** On **February 21, 2020**, Wells Fargo agreed to pay a combined **$3 billion** to the DOJ and SEC and entered a **three-year deferred-prosecution agreement**, admitting the sales-practice misconduct ran **2002–2016**, that it collected millions in fees and interest it should not have, harmed customers' credit, and unlawfully misused personal information. — *Src:* [S7] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-18]** Of that $3 billion, **$500 million** was paid to the SEC for **distribution to harmed investors**. — *Src:* [S5],[S7] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-19]** **Carrie Tolstedt** headed Wells Fargo's **Community Bank division from approximately 2007 to 2016**, the retail unit that set and enforced the cross-sell sales targets. — *Src:* [S3],[S6] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-20]** The **SEC charged Tolstedt in November 2020 and she settled in 2023**; the SEC's case was that from **mid-2014 through mid-2016** she publicly described and endorsed the cross-sell metric as a measure of the bank's success **while it was inflated by accounts and services that were unused, unneeded, or unauthorized**. — *Src:* [S5] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* phrase as "the SEC found/alleged," attributed to the SEC action — do NOT assert as the channel's own conclusion.
- **[F-21]** Sales pressure produced improvised tactics on the ground — e.g., a market manager's **"sales road map,"** a cheat-sheet on what order to pitch products to maximize the chance a customer signed up for more. — *Src:* [S12] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* reported detail illustrating bottom-up pressure; keep as reported color, not a load-bearing claim.
- **[F-22]** In reported cases, employees who raised concerns through Wells Fargo's **ethics hotline were met with no action or were terminated**, and the bank in some cases filed negative **U5 termination forms** that tainted fired reporters' industry records. — *Src:* [S3] · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the asymmetry core; corroborated in specific form by the OSHA orders [F-23],[F-24], which are the hard-sourced instances.
- **[F-23]** In **2017 OSHA (U.S. Dept. of Labor) ordered Wells Fargo to reinstate and pay about $5.4 million** to a wealth-management whistleblower who had been fired (originally in 2011) after objecting to bankers opening accounts and enrolling customers in products without consent — at the time OSHA's **largest individual whistleblower award**. — *Src:* [S10] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* a *documented, adjudicated* retaliation case — the safest evidence for the "they punished the people who reported it" beat.
- **[F-24]** The Department of Labor also ordered Wells Fargo to reinstate and pay about **$577,500** to a former **Pomona, California branch manager** terminated after complaining that salespeople were creating unauthorized accounts to meet targets. — *Src:* [S10] · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-25]** **John Stumpf resigned** as chairman and CEO on **October 12, 2016**, weeks after the Senate hearing. — *Src:* [S3] · *Conf:* high.

- **[F-27]** The board forfeited/clawed back about **$67 million total** from Tolstedt — roughly **$19 million** unvested equity plus about **$47.3 million** in additional clawed-back vested options — and terminated her **for cause, retroactively**. — *Src:* [S11] · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the clawbacks came only under sustained public/political pressure — before that both had been set to leave with their pay (Tolstedt's planned retirement was announced with praise months earlier).

- **The "villain" is an incentive, not a person — that is the whole payload.** There is no mastermind who dreamed up opening 3.5 million fake accounts. There is a **target** ("eight products per household," picked partly because it *rhymed with great* [F-01]), a **cross-sell ratio** the bank sold to investors as proof it was the best in the business [F-03], and a **pressure structure** that fired ~5,300 people for cheating [F-15] while treating a ~1%-a-year firing rate as an acceptable cost of the model [F-16]. The engine of the fraud is that last fact: management could see the fires and had decided they were affordable. The character work the writer opens on is the *institution's* psychology — a bank so proud of cross-selling it made the metric its identity — not a single face.
- **The two names to handle carefully (document-altitude only):** **John Stumpf**, the CEO who championed and defended the eight-products goal [F-02] and left in October 2016 [F-25]; and **Carrie Tolstedt**, who ran the Community Bank that set the targets [F-19] and whom the SEC found endorsed an inflated metric to investors [F-20]. Everything the script says about either must be pinned to a finding: the OCC ban and fines [F-28],[F-32], the SEC settlements [F-30],[F-31], the clawbacks [F-26],[F-27], the guilty plea to *obstruction* [F-32]. The wry register rides the **absurdity of the incentive and the asymmetry of the outcomes**, never a personal roast of either individual (they are living; §dna YMYL/defamation).
- **Human cost (light, aggregate):** ordinary customers had accounts and cards opened in their names, their **own money quietly shuffled** into accounts they never asked for [F-07], some hit with overdraft, maintenance and annual fees and with **damaged credit** [F-10],[F-14],[F-17]; ~190,000 accounts actually incurred charges [F-14]. And ordinary *employees* were ground between an impossible quota and an ethics line that could get them fired [F-22],[F-23],[F-24]. Register it concretely and move — no single-victim set-piece.
- **Witnessed-absurd / telling details (all sourced):**
  - The quota number was chosen because **"eight" rhymes with "great,"** and Stumpf was made to say so under oath in the Senate [F-01],[F-02].
  - **"Pinning":** an employee sets your debit PIN to **0000** so they can open online banking in your name [F-08].
  - Employees **invented email addresses** — sometimes their own — to enroll customers who had no idea [F-09].
  - Management had, per the board's own report, decided that firing **1% of the sales force a year** for cheating was a tolerable cost of doing business [F-16].
  - The **only person criminally charged** in the whole affair pleaded guilty not to the fraud but to **obstructing the examination**, and got **no prison time** [F-32],[F-33].

## Verification chain (vindication lever — how the case is PROVEN)
- **What is proven / uncontested (admitted or adjudicated):** the fake accounts existed at scale — the bank *admitted* to the DOJ that employees created false records and misused customer information [F-12],[F-17]; the CFPB/OCC found the specific mechanics and fined $185M [F-04],[F-05],[F-07]; the bank's own disclosures put the number at ~2.1M then ~3.5M [F-06],[F-13]; ~5,300 were fired [F-15]; the Fed capped the bank's assets [F-34]; the CEO accepted a lifetime ban and $17.5M and the Community Bank head pleaded guilty to obstruction [F-28],[F-32]. The writer should separate the admitted core from the intent question out loud — that honesty is the credibility beat and is *itself* the payload ("the system did it").
- **Corroboration (independence-tagged):** the backbone converges across **mutually independent authorities** — the CFPB and OCC (banking regulators), the SEC (securities regulator), the DOJ (criminal), the Federal Reserve (asset cap), the Department of Labor/OSHA (whistleblowers), and a federal sentencing court — plus the bank's **own** disclosures and its board's **own** independent (Shearman & Sterling) report [S11], plus contemporaneous primary reporting (Washington Post, American Banker, CNBC). - **Exoneration (what the obvious villain did NOT do / where the cartoon is wrong):** there was **no single con-man who invented the fraud** — the "eight" target predates Stumpf and traces to a 1990s Norwest campaign [F-01], and no top executive was found criminally liable *for the account-opening fraud itself* [F-32]. Clearing away the "one evil CEO" cartoon is what earns the real, sharper indictment: an **incentive built on a rhyme**, a **metric sold to investors**, and a management that had **priced in** firing its own cheats — a machine that manufactured a nationwide fraud from the bottom up, which no individual villain and no honest employee alone could have produced.

## Myths / misconceptions to bust  (OPTIONAL body texture, not a mandated block)
- **Myth:** the number of fake accounts "doubled" from 2.1M to 3.5M. → **Actually:** the *review window widened* (from May 2011–mid 2015 to Jan 2009–Sep 2016, ~93.5M accounts to ~165M reviewed); the 3.5M is 2.55M from the original window **plus** 981,000 from the newly-added years — a bigger count, not a second crime wave [F-06],[F-13].
- **Myth:** it was just a few thousand rogue low-level employees ("bad apples"). → **Actually:** ~5,300 were fired, but regulators concluded the **incentive structure and its senior owners** were culpable — a lifetime ban and $17.5M for the CEO, a criminal obstruction plea and bar for the Community Bank head, and a board finding that a ~1%/year firing rate was treated as acceptable [F-15],[F-16],[F-28],[F-32].
- **Myth:** the wrongdoers went to prison. → **Actually:** the **only** person criminally charged pleaded guilty to *obstructing the exam* (not the fraud) and received **probation and home confinement, no prison** [F-32],[F-33].
- **Myth:** the ethics line protected people who spoke up. → **Actually:** documented whistleblowers were fired and later ordered reinstated with damages by the Labor Department [F-23],[F-24].
