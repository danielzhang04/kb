[B-ROLL: a paper fortune of billions, and no cash to pay a bill]
At the start of 1980, two brothers owned about half of all the silver in the world that could actually be bought and sold. On paper, they were sitting on nearly seven billion dollars of it. [BEAT] A few weeks later, they couldn't come up with the cash to cover a single bill, and a group of the biggest banks in America had to step in and bail them out.

[B-ROLL: the Texas oil family behind it]
They were Nelson Bunker Hunt and his brother Herbert, sons of billionaire Texas oil tycoon H.L. Hunt, and they'd grown up inside one of the great American fortunes. But Bunker Hunt, the older one, the one who drove all this, did not act like a rich man. He flew coach. He wore off-the-rack suits and drove a plain, forgettable car. He was also a hardcore anti-Communist and a member of the John Birch Society, and he had one very specific, very deep belief about money.

[B-ROLL: the belief that paper money was a lie]
Bunker Hunt thought paper money was a con. He watched the government print dollars and let inflation run through the 1970s, and he decided the cash in everybody's wallet was quietly being turned into garbage. So he went looking for money the government couldn't print. Real money. And he landed on silver. Not as an investment, exactly. More like a lifeboat. For Bunker Hunt this was never really a clever trade to get richer. He was already rich. If the dollar was doomed, the only safe move was to get out of paper entirely, into something you could actually hold in your hand.

[B-ROLL: an oil fortune turning itself into metal, flown to Switzerland]
So the Hunts started buying silver. A lot of it. As early as 1973 they were buying it by the tens of millions of ounces, and they didn't just leave it in a warehouse. They loaded millions of ounces onto chartered planes and flew it to Switzerland, guarded by armed cowboys from a Texas ranch. The story goes that the man running the operation picked the guards by holding a shooting contest, and the best shots won the job of riding along with the silver. [BEAT] These were very serious people, about a very strange idea.

[B-ROLL: how you buy half the silver with borrowed money]
So how did a rich man's hobby turn into a threat to the whole market? Well, the Hunts didn't only buy silver bars. They bought silver futures, which are just contracts to buy silver later, and you can buy those on borrowed money. You put down a fraction of the price, and the rest is borrowed. So the Hunts' fortune didn't buy the silver it could afford. It bought several times that much. That's the whole magic of borrowed money: it lets a big fortune act like a far bigger one. And they didn't stop there. They took on partners to keep the buying going, reportedly including a Saudi sheikh with deep pockets of his own. By the end of 1979, Bunker, Herbert, and a company they controlled were holding around a hundred and ninety-five million ounces of silver. About half of all the silver on Earth that could actually be traded.

[B-ROLL: the price going vertical]
And when two men quietly buy up half the available supply of anything, the price does exactly what you'd think. Silver had been sitting under ten dollars an ounce. By the end of 1979 it was almost thirty-five. By January 1980 it touched nearly fifty. The Hunts' pile of silver, worth about a billion dollars the summer before, was suddenly worth close to seven billion.

[B-ROLL: the people on the other side of the bet, getting nervous]
But every one of those futures contracts had someone on the other side of it. For every Hunt buying silver, somebody had promised to deliver silver later at a set price. And a rising market was a nightmare for them: the higher silver climbed, the more they'd lose when the day came to hand it over. As silver screamed toward fifty dollars, a lot of powerful people on Wall Street were staring at losses big enough to wipe them out. So the exchange did something about it.

[B-ROLL: changing the rules mid-game]
In January 1980, the main silver exchange changed the rules. From now on, it said, you could only trade silver to close out a position. In plain terms: you were allowed to sell, but you were not allowed to place new bets to buy. And that one rule quietly pulled the plug on the entire thing, because the Hunts' whole game depended on more buyers pushing the price higher. Now the biggest buyers of all had been ordered to stop. The Hunts always insisted this was the house protecting itself, that those boards were full of men who'd bet against silver and would have been ruined if it kept climbing. Either way, the effect was the same. And a market where everyone is suddenly only allowed to sell has just one direction to go. With new buyers locked out, the people already holding silver started rushing for the exits, and the price didn't just stall. It went into free fall.

[B-ROLL: leverage running in reverse]
And as it fell, all that borrowed money turned around and pointed the other way. This is the trap inside borrowing. On the way up, borrowing had made the Hunts richer faster than almost anyone in history. On the way down, those same loans demanded cash, immediately, to cover the losses. Every dollar silver fell, the Hunts owed more money. And their fortune wasn't sitting in cash. It was sitting in silver, the thing that was now dropping like a stone.

[B-ROLL: the day it all came due]
It broke on March 27th, 1980, a day the market still calls Silver Thursday. Silver crashed to under eleven dollars an ounce, less than a quarter of its peak. And when a leveraged bet turns against you, the demand is brutally simple: wire in cash by tomorrow to cover the loss, or the broker sells off everything you've got to cover it for you. Herbert Hunt had already gone to the family's creditors and admitted it: they didn't have the cash, and they didn't have the collateral, to cover what they owed. Two men worth billions on paper could not find the money. One of their brokers was left holding an unpaid hole of about a hundred and twenty million dollars, and for a few hours it looked like the Hunts might drag a good chunk of Wall Street down with them.

[B-ROLL: the bailout nobody expected]
So who rode to the rescue of the two men who thought the financial system was a fraud? [PAUSE] The financial system. A group of thirteen banks got together and arranged a loan of about one and a tenth billion dollars to the Hunts' oil company, backed by the family's oil fields and pipelines, purely to stop the dominoes from falling. The hard-money crusaders, the men who had bet everything that paper money was worthless, were rescued from ruin by an enormous pile of borrowed paper money.

[B-ROLL: the reckoning]
And it only bought them time. A few years later, a jury found the Hunts had illegally conspired to corner the silver market, and handed down a judgment worth over a hundred million dollars. In 1988, Bunker and Herbert Hunt, sons of one of the richest men in America, filed for bankruptcy, among the largest personal bankruptcies Texas had ever seen, and regulators barred them from trading any commodities ever again.

[B-ROLL: the silver was real, the fortune was paper]
Bunker Hunt had spent a decade and a fortune trying to prove one thing: that paper money was a lie, and only silver was real. And in a strange way, he was right. The silver was real. It sat in a vault, solid and shining, exactly as promised. It was the fortune that turned out to be paper. [PAUSE] And when the bill finally came due, it came due in dollars, and the two men who owned half the silver anyone could actually buy couldn't pay it.
