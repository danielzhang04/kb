## Fact ledger  ← the scriptwriter may state ONLY what appears here

**Who & why**
- **[F-01]** Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt were sons of Texas oil billionaire H.L. Hunt; they used the family's oil fortune to buy silver. — *Src:* S1,S3 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-02]** Bunker Hunt was a zealous anti-Communist and a prominent member of the John Birch Society who bankrolled conservative causes; despite his wealth he was known for flying coach, wearing off-the-rack clothes, and driving nondescript cars. — *Src:* S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the deflate-the-powerful character detail; present as reported.
- **[F-03]** The Hunts' driving belief was that paper money was being debased by government spending and 1970s inflation, and that silver was "real money" a government could not print; that ideology, not just profit, drove the buying. — *Src:* S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* motive; documented worldview, present as their reasoning.

**How they bought it up**
- **[F-04]** They accumulated silver through a mix of physical bullion and silver futures contracts (roughly half the position was futures by end-1979), buying the futures on margin (a deposit, not full payment) so borrowed leverage magnified the position. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the leverage is the seed of the collapse.
- **[F-05]** By the end of 1979, Bunker Hunt, Herbert Hunt, and the Hunt-controlled International Metals Investment Company controlled about 195 million troy ounces of silver. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-06]** That roughly 195-million-ounce hoard is commonly described as about **half of the world's deliverable silver supply**. — *Src:* S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* THIS is the framing the title's "half" rides on; state it as *deliverable* supply.
- **[F-07]** By other measures the same hoard was about a third to two-thirds of the world's privately-held (non-government) silver: the CFTC characterized it as roughly two-thirds of the identifiable privately-held supply, while other counts put it near a third. — *Src:* S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the figure is genuinely denominator-dependent; hedge, and don't claim "half of ALL the silver on Earth."
- **[F-08]** Early on, in 1973, the brothers bought tens of millions of ounces and flew much of it to Switzerland on chartered planes guarded by armed Texas ranch hands. — *Src:* S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the signature absurd image.
- **[F-09]** To crew that silver airlift, Bunker's brother-in-law Randy Kreiling reportedly recruited about a dozen cowboys from a ranch by holding a shooting match, and the best shots won the job of riding shotgun on the silver to Switzerland. — *Src:* S3 · *Conf:* low · *Note:* colorful, single-sourced secondary; present as "the story goes," or use lightly.
- **[F-10]** The Hunts also took on partners, working with a Saudi sheikh thought to represent the royal family, and channeled buying through the International Metals Investment Company. — *Src:* S1,S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* IMIC is the Hunt-controlled entity; the Saudi-partner detail is secondary, hedge.

**The run-up**
- **[F-11]** Silver traded below about $10 an ounce before August 1979, then the Hunts' buying helped drive it up through the autumn. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-12]** By 31 December 1979, silver was about $34.45 an ounce; in January 1980 it peaked above $50 an ounce. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the ~$6-to-nearly-$50 run.
- **[F-13]** As the price climbed, the paper value of the Hunts' silver position rose from about $1.1 billion in mid-1979 to roughly $6.8 billion by January 1980. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.

**The house changes the rules**
- **[F-14]** In January 1980 the exchanges moved against the position: COMEX imposed position limits on silver futures (around 7 January), and on 21 January COMEX restricted silver trading to liquidation orders only (no new long positions), a rule change widely referred to as Silver Rule 7. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the "Silver Rule 7" name is the common label; the substance (liquidation-only) is documented.
- **[F-15]** The Hunts and their defenders argued the rule change was the exchange saving its own insiders: the boards included traders who were short silver and stood to lose if the price kept rising, a conflict-of-interest claim that is the story's core "the house rigged it" beat. — *Src:* S2,S3 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* present as the Hunts' argument / the alleged conflict, not as settled fact.
- **[F-16]** With new buying blocked and the price sliding, the position that leverage had inflated now worked in reverse: falling prices triggered margin calls the Hunts had to meet in cash. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the causal hinge.

**Silver Thursday & the bailout**
- **[F-17]** On 27 March 1980, Silver Thursday, silver collapsed to about $10.80 an ounce; by 25–26 March Herbert Hunt had told creditors the Hunts no longer had the cash or collateral to meet their margin calls. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-18]** Their broker Bache was left with an unsecured debit of about $122 million in the Hunt accounts, and the threat that a Hunt default could cascade through Wall Street brokers. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-19]** To contain the damage, a consortium of thirteen banks arranged a roughly $1.1 billion loan to the Hunt family company Placid Oil, secured by the family's oil, gas, and pipeline assets and guaranteed by the brothers, to cover their obligations. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the irony beat — "free-market" silver bulls rescued by a bank bailout.

**Aftermath**
- **[F-20]** In 1988 a federal jury (in a suit brought by the Peruvian minerals company Minpeco) found Bunker, Herbert, and others liable for conspiring to corner the silver market, resulting in a judgment of about $134 million after adjustments. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-21]** In September 1988 Bunker and Herbert Hunt filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, among the largest personal bankruptcies in Texas at the time. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-22]** In 1989 the brothers settled charges with the CFTC and were fined and barred from trading commodities. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.

**Connective / relational facts (ownership · cause · identity · sequence)**
- **[F-23]** The money that bought the silver was oil money: the Hunts converted an oil fortune into the world's largest silver hoard, so when silver collapsed it was the oil empire (Placid Oil's assets) that had to be pledged to save them. — *Src:* S1,S3 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-24]** Leverage is the whole hinge: the same borrowed margin that let two men buy up half the deliverable silver is exactly what destroyed them, because a leveraged position that soars on the way up faces ruinous margin calls on the way down. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-25]** Sequence: buy quietly with leverage → the price soars → the exchange changes the rules to liquidation-only → new buying stops and the price reverses → margin calls the Hunts can't meet → the crash and the bank bailout. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.

## Cast, motive & human-cost
- **Protagonists — the Hunt brothers:** Bunker the true believer (John Birch anti-government ideologue who thought paper money was a fraud and silver was honest money, yet flew coach and dressed off-the-rack, F-02/F-03) and Herbert the operational one. The interiority the writer can open on: not greed exactly, a conviction that the whole financial system was fake and silver was the one real thing, taken to a world-cornering extreme.
- **"Human cost" (light):** there's no mass-casualty tragedy here; the losers are the brokers left holding the bag, the market thrown into chaos, and ultimately the Hunts themselves, who went from among the richest men in America to bankruptcy (F-18/F-21). Keep it wry, not mournful.
- **Witnessed-absurd / telling details:** (a) silver flown to Switzerland on chartered planes guarded by armed Texas cowboys chosen by a shooting match [F-08/F-09]; (b) a billionaire silver king who flew coach and drove a beater [F-02]; (c) two men holding a paper fortune that swelled to ~$6.8 billion, then couldn't find the cash for a margin call [F-13/F-17].

## Myths / misconceptions to bust
- **Myth:** "They owned half the silver in the world." → **Actually:** about half the world's *deliverable* silver (and a third to two-thirds of privately-held silver depending on the count), not half of all silver in existence, most of which sits in government vaults and existing objects. Keep the "half" honest by naming the *deliverable* framing. ([F-05,F-06,F-07])
- **Myth:** "The market naturally corrected." → **Actually:** the collapse was triggered by a deliberate rule change (liquidation-only trading) that stopped new buying, a human decision the Hunts say protected the exchange's own insiders. ([F-14,F-15])

## Why this matters (universality): OPTIONAL body texture, never the close
- **The rhyme:** the leverage trap, a bet so big it can only work if the price keeps going up, that turns a fortune into a margin call the instant it reverses; the same shape recurs in every blow-up from LTCM to the 2021 meme-stock short squeezes. **The spottable tell:** anyone who has to keep buying to keep the price up, on borrowed money, has already lost, they just don't know when. Weave only where it lands; do NOT tee up a "this repeats every generation" moral. End on the story's own irony (real-money crusaders undone by a paper margin call), not a lesson.
