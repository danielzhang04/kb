[B-ROLL: a foreign gambler running an entire nation's money]
For a little while, three hundred years ago, the most powerful man in France was a foreign gambler who had once been sentenced to death for killing a man. Within the span of five years, he took a bankrupt country, invented modern paper money to save it, made an entire city rich on that paper, and then watched the whole thing turn to dust. [BEAT] And he wasn't really a con man. He was mostly right. His name was John Law, and the money in your wallet is basically his idea.

[B-ROLL: the gambler with a head for numbers]
Law was a Scot, born in Edinburgh in 1671 into a family of bankers. But young John wasn't interested in the family business. He was interested in gambling, and he was very good at it, because he had a real gift for the math of odds. He spent his twenties at the card tables, winning and losing fortunes. And in 1694, in London, over a woman, he killed a man named Beau Wilson in a sword duel.

[B-ROLL: a death sentence, and a run for it]
He was convicted of murder and sentenced to hang. Somehow the sentence got knocked down, and then Law did the practical thing and escaped, slipping across to the Continent. For the next ten years or so he was a fugitive, drifting between cities, gambling for a living. But in all that time at the tables, he'd been chewing on a much bigger idea.

[B-ROLL: the big idea about money]
Law had worked out a theory about money that was way ahead of its time. Everyone back then believed a country's wealth was the gold sitting in its vaults. Law thought that was backwards. Money, he figured, isn't wealth. It's just a tool for moving wealth around. And gold was a clumsy tool, because there was only ever so much of it. So why not print paper money instead, backed by a national bank, and use it to get the whole economy moving? At the time it sounded insane, and for years nobody would let him near a treasury. [BEAT] Then he found a country desperate enough to say yes.

[B-ROLL: France, broke and desperate]
In 1715, the old king of France, Louis the Fourteenth, died, and left behind a country buried under war debt. France was effectively broke, and it was being run by a stand-in, a regent, for the new boy king. And a broke government will listen to almost anyone with a plan. So this fugitive Scottish gambler walked in with his theory of paper money, and France decided to roll the dice.

[B-ROLL: the bank that printed paper]
In 1716, Law opened a bank. You could bring it your gold coins, and it would hand you paper notes in return. The notes were easier to carry, easier to spend, and, most importantly, people trusted them. It worked so well that in 1718 the French government took the bank over and made it the royal bank, the machine that printed the nation's money. He had gotten an entire country comfortable using paper.

[B-ROLL: the company selling a dream]
Then came the other half of the plan. Law had also started a company, the Mississippi Company, and handed it a monopoly on trade with France's colony of Louisiana. He sold shares in it, little slices of the company anyone could buy and sell, and he sold Louisiana as a paradise stuffed with gold just waiting to be scooped up. In reality, Louisiana was mostly empty wilderness and produced almost nothing. But that turned out not to matter, because of what Law did next.

[B-ROLL: bolting the machine together]
He bolted the two halves into one enormous machine. His company swallowed his bank. Then it took over collecting France's taxes, minting France's coins, and managing France's entire national debt. So one man now ran the bank that printed the money, and the company whose stock everyone was buying, and the treasury of the country. [PAUSE] And the royal bank would happily lend you fresh paper money to go buy shares in the company. So the paper went out, people bought shares, the share price climbed, everyone cheered, and the good news justified printing even more paper. A machine that fed itself.

[B-ROLL: the mania on the rue Quincampoix]
And for about a year, it was the most exciting thing on Earth. The shares took off. In January 1719, a single share cost around five hundred livres. By December, it was ten thousand. A narrow Paris street called the rue Quincampoix became the center of the universe, mobbed day and night with dukes and shopkeepers shoving shoulder to shoulder to buy in. So many people got suddenly, absurdly rich on paper that this is where the word millionaire is said to have been born. And in January 1720, the gambler reached the summit: John Law was made Controller General of Finances, the most powerful money man in all of France.

[B-ROLL: the crack in the paper]
But the whole tower rested on one belief: that the paper was actually worth something. And Law had printed an ocean of it to keep that share price climbing. That was the problem. When you flood a country with paper money, there's suddenly far more of it chasing the same bread and land and goods, so each note quietly starts to buy less. Prices began to soar. And a few clever people looked at the rising prices, looked at their fat stacks of paper, and decided they'd rather be holding gold. So they started trading their notes back in for coins.

[B-ROLL: the doom loop runs backwards]
And that was the trap in Law's machine. France was, in effect, printing money to prop up its own stock, and the printing was the very thing making the money worth less. As more and more people swapped their paper for gold, the royal bank started running low on actual coins. So Law made the move that finished him. He used the power of the state to force people to keep the paper. He made the notes the only legal money in France, and made it a crime to hold much gold at all. But nobody has to be forced to take money they actually trust. So instead of saving the paper, Law had just told the whole country it was worthless.

[B-ROLL: the stampede]
The trickle became a stampede. Crowds mobbed the bank trying to get their gold out before it was gone, and the bank had to slam its doors. The share price fell off a cliff. From ten thousand livres it dropped to two thousand, then one thousand, and within the year it was back to five hundred, right where it had started, as if the whole miracle had never happened. Except it had. Fortunes made on paper turned back into paper, and then into nothing, and all over France the people who had felt rich for a year were wiped out.

[B-ROLL: the most powerful man in France, running]
And John Law, who months earlier had been the most powerful man in the country, was now the most hated man in it. By the end of 1720 he fled France, broke, leaving his grand houses behind him. He spent the rest of his life the way he'd started it, drifting between cities, gambling for money. He died in Venice in 1729, poor, and was buried in a small church there.

[B-ROLL: the idea that outlived the man]
But Law wasn't wrong. Paper money, a national bank that prints it, using borrowed money to grow an economy, none of that sounds crazy anymore. The cash in your pocket is, more or less, the thing a fugitive gambler dreamed up at a card table. He just also proved, the very first time it was ever tried, exactly how it breaks. [PAUSE] He gave the world, so the story goes, the word millionaire. He died without a coin to his name.
