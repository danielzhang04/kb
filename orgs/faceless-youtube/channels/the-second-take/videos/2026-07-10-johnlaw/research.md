## Fact ledger  ← the scriptwriter may state ONLY what appears here

**The gambler**
- **[F-01]** John Law was born in 1671 in Edinburgh into a family of bankers and goldsmiths ("Law of Lauriston"). — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-02]** He was a compulsive gambler who applied a sharp head for probability to cards, and lost and won large sums around London and Europe. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-03]** In 1694 he killed a man, Edward "Beau" Wilson, in a sword duel in London (over a woman), was convicted of murder, and was sentenced to death. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-04]** The sentence was reduced (treated as manslaughter/commuted to a fine), and Law escaped custody and fled to the Continent, spending about a decade as a gambler and financial speculator across Europe. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* he was a fugitive from a death sentence for years.

**The idea**
- **[F-05]** Law developed a genuine economic theory: that money is just a means of exchange, not wealth itself, and that a country could grow rich by replacing scarce gold coin with paper money issued by a national bank. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the visionary-not-just-charlatan core.
- **[F-06]** He laid this out in a 1705 book, "Money and Trade Considered," and argued paper money and dividend-paying shares were a better, more flexible form of money than metal. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.

**Getting France to say yes**
- **[F-07]** When Louis XIV died in 1715, France was buried in war debt, and the country was run by a Regent, Philippe II, Duke of Orléans, for the child king Louis XV. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-08]** Desperate for a fix, the Regent let Law try his experiment: in 1716 Law opened a private bank (the Banque Générale) that took gold deposits and issued paper banknotes. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-09]** The notes worked and gained trust, and in 1718 the French government took the bank over, making it the Banque Royale, a de facto national bank issuing state-backed paper money. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.

**The machine (the bank fused to the company)**
- **[F-10]** In 1717 Law founded the Mississippi Company (the Compagnie d'Occident), given a monopoly on trade with France's colony of Louisiana, and hyped it as a fortune waiting to be tapped. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* Louisiana produced almost nothing; the value was the hype, not real trade.
- **[F-11]** Law then bolted the pieces together: the company swallowed France's other colonial trading firms (becoming the Compagnie des Indes, 1719) and, in 1720, absorbed the Banque Royale itself, so one entity controlled the paper money AND the hot stock. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-12]** The company also took over collecting France's taxes, minting its coins, and managing its national debt, promising to pay the debt off out of its monopoly profits, so the company's share price effectively became France's national solvency. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the causal spine of the whole story.
- **[F-13]** The Banque Royale lent people paper notes to buy Mississippi shares, which pushed the share price up, which made the scheme look more successful, which justified printing more notes: a self-feeding loop. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the doom-loop mechanism; state plainly.

**The bubble**
- **[F-14]** Mississippi Company shares went from around 500 livres in January 1719 to about 10,000 livres by December 1719, roughly a 1,900% rise in under a year. — *Src:* S2 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-15]** Crowds of commoners and nobles jammed the rue Quincampoix in Paris to trade the shares in a speculative frenzy, and the word "millionaire" was coined for the sudden paper fortunes. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the "millionaire" coinage is widely repeated but its exact origin is debated; hedge ("the word millionaire is said to date from here").
- **[F-16]** At the peak, in January 1720, Law was made Controller General of Finances, effectively the most powerful economic official in France: he now personally ran the money, the stock, and the treasury. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* one man, no check on him.
- **[F-17]** For a stretch it looked like real success: Law pushed reforms and by some accounts French industry rose sharply and the number of French export ships jumped from 16 to 300. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the "it briefly worked" beat; keep it, it makes the fall land.

**The collapse (1720)**
- **[F-18]** All the printing to prop up the share price fed inflation; as prices surged, some investors started cashing shares and notes back into gold coin. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-19]** To stop the run, the authorities tried to force the paper: they restricted large gold payments, made the Banque Royale's notes legal tender for taxes and debts, and in May 1720 tried a "controlled" devaluation of both notes and shares, then reversed it days later, shattering confidence. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the panic-management that made it worse.
- **[F-20]** The public rushed to convert paper to coin, the bank had to close its doors, and the share price collapsed: from the ~9,000–10,000 peak down to about 2,000 livres by September 1720, ~1,000 by December, and back to ~500 by 1721, where it had started. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* high · *Note:* the full round trip to zero gain.
- **[F-21]** Law was dismissed as Controller General in 1720 (having held the post only months). — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.

**The fall of the man**
- **[F-22]** In December 1720 Law fled France, impoverished, his French properties given up; he spent his remaining years gambling around Europe and never recovered his wealth. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-23]** He died poor in Venice in 1729, reportedly of pneumonia, and is buried there (San Moisè church). — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-24]** The episode is remembered as one of the first great paper-money and stock bubbles, a sister event to the South Sea Bubble in England the same year, and it left France so wary of paper money and central banks that the country avoided both for generations. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the "set France back" line is commonly stated (often ~80 years, to the Revolution's assignats); hedge as "for generations," don't assert a precise span.

**Connective / relational facts (ownership · cause · identity · sequence)**
- **[F-25]** One man held every lever: Law ran the national bank that printed the money, the company whose stock the money was buying, and the treasury that owed the debt the whole thing was meant to erase, so there was nobody positioned to say stop. — *Src:* S1 · *Conf:* high.
- **[F-26]** The fatal link: because the company had taken over the national debt, holding up the share price WAS national policy, so the government kept printing money to buy its own stock, which is exactly the act that destroyed the money. — *Src:* S1,S2 · *Conf:* med · *Note:* the identity that makes the collapse inevitable; the writer's core mechanism beat.

## Cast, motive & human-cost
- **Protagonist — John Law:** a Scottish gambler and convicted killer, fugitive from a death sentence, who was also a real financial visionary; the interiority to open on is a man who genuinely believed he'd cracked the code of money and got a whole kingdom to bet on it [F-02,F-03,F-05]. Not a simple con man, that's the nuance that makes him interesting.
- **The antagonist is the idea itself / the doom loop:** there's no villain opposite Law; the "antagonist" is the self-feeding machine he built (print notes → buy shares → print more) that no one could stop [F-13,F-25].
- **Human cost (light):** ordinary Parisians who piled into the shares on borrowed paper were wiped out when it round-tripped to 500 livres [F-20]; France's finances were left roughly where they started, minus the trust [F-24]. Keep it wry; this is hubris, not a body count.
- **Witnessed-absurd / telling details:** (a) a man under a commuted death sentence became the most powerful financial official in France [F-03,F-16]; (b) the word "millionaire" was born in the crush on the rue Quincampoix [F-15]; (c) the government literally printed money to buy its own stock to make itself look solvent [F-26].

## Reportable scenes & characterization  (NO quotes — narrator reported speech only)
- **[Q-01]** A gambler who had killed a man in a duel and skipped out on a death sentence talked a desperate French government into letting him rebuild the entire national economy on paper he printed himself. — *Src:* S1.
- **[Q-02]** For a year, commoners and aristocrats jammed a narrow Paris street shoulder to shoulder, buying and flipping shares on borrowed banknotes, minting brand-new "millionaires" by the week. — *Src:* S1,S2.
- **[Q-03]** When the price started to slip, Law used the powers of the state to force people to keep the paper, making the notes legal money and choking off gold, which only convinced everyone the paper was worthless. — *Src:* S1,S2.

## Myths / misconceptions to bust
- **Myth:** "John Law was just a con man." → **Actually:** he was a genuine economic theorist whose ideas (paper money, a central bank, using credit to stimulate an economy) became foundations of modern finance; the system failed on execution and France's debt, not because the whole idea was a scam. ([F-05,F-06,F-24])
- **Myth:** "The Mississippi bubble was about Louisiana gold." → **Actually:** the colony produced almost nothing of value; the share price was pure speculation on a hyped monopoly, inflated by the bank lending people money to buy it. ([F-10,F-13])

## Why this matters (universality): OPTIONAL body texture, never the close
- **The rhyme:** the pattern of propping an asset price by printing money to buy it, which recurs from the South Sea Bubble the very same year to modern debates about central banks and asset prices. **The spottable tell:** when the same institution both prints the money and profits from the thing the money is buying, there's no brake. Weave only where it lands; do NOT tee up a "this happens every generation" moral. End on the story's own irony (the gambler who invented modern money and lost everything, including the country's trust), not a lesson.
