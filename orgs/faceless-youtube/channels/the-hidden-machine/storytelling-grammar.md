# The Second Take — Storytelling Grammar

**What this is:** the channel's single source of truth for *how a video is told* — the voice, the story
shape, the staging. It is a **generative grammar**: kinds of move to invent from, never a template to
clone. Read it every run; where it conflicts with generic doctrine, this doc wins for The Second Take.

**The North Star:** a smart, funny friend telling you an unbelievable true story — excited to tell it,
reacting to it as they go, explaining it with comparisons you instantly picture. Not a writer crafting
prose, not a finance teacher. **Raw and a little messy beats polished and bland.**

**The voice reference:** the approved excerpts in `channels/the-second-take/example-scripts.md`. That is
the target energy, and every inline example below is drawn from it. Match it; don't quote it.

---

## 1. The voice

**1.1 — Casual friend, plain words.** No literary phrasing, and no term a normal person wouldn't say
out loud ("sovereign debt," "underwritten," "yield") — say the plain version or cut it. **When a term
or mechanism the viewer may not know has to stay, contextualize it in the same breath:** a plain gloss
or an analogy pitched at someone who has never thought about it, never an assumption of knowledge
("computers run on these things called hard drives, which are basically where your computer keeps
everything it remembers"). This is a money-*story*, not a finance tutorial: deliver the amazing fact,
skip the mechanics lesson. **Spoken grammar beats written grammar every time:** "none of them are" is
what a person says out loud, so it is what gets written, and a copy editor's correction that nobody
would say is a defect. *Test: would you actually say this line to a friend?* (Formatting: no em/en
dashes, ever — an AI tell the viewer can't hear. Breath is periods, commas, and colons. `script.md` is
pure voiceover prose: no cues, no brackets, no stage directions.)
> ✗ "underwritten by a respectable banking house, in notes of a hundred"
> ✓ "MacGregor, seeing that people believed him, then convinces the Bank of Scotland's official printer
> to make him 5,000 custom Poyais dollar notes."

**Every line parses on the first listen, and names the thing instead of the idea of it.** There is no
rewind on a voiceover, so a sentence whose meaning depends on the viewer working out what a pronoun
points at ("That's it doing that") is already gone by the time they get there. Worse is **concept-prose**:
the writer naming the *shape* of what happened instead of what happened ("everything after this is
people trying to make the world's version match his target"). Say who did what, to what, and what came
of it, in the order it happened.
> "They rented a warehouse, and went shopping. They bought 26,000 bricks from the Colorado Brick
> Company, handpicked so that a box with a brick felt and weighed exactly like a box of hard drives."

**Paragraphs are idea blocks, four to five sentences on average.** A block carries one idea to its end
and stops; the next block starts the next idea. Short sentences are how a speaker lands a hit, and they
land *inside* a block, powered by the longer sentences around them.
> "The company was MiniScribe, a hard drive manufacturer out of Colorado, founded in [verify: year] by
> [verify: founder(s)/garage]. And they were HOT. By 1985 they were shipping [verify: volume] hard
> drives a year to electronics giants like IBM and [verify: second customer]."

A sentence standing alone as its own paragraph is the defect, and a page of them is the staccato
monotone this pipeline falls into by default: every line gets identical weight, so nothing lands and
the whole script reads as one long flat drop. Block length varies because thoughts vary. A run of short
sentences *inside* a block, carrying real content, is just how people talk when they pick up speed
("Peru. Chile. Argentina.").

**Tense: past is the storytelling default.** The story already happened, so tell it in the past ("They
bought 26,000 bricks from the Colorado Brick Company"). Present tense is for framing the viewer into a
period ("We're in the 1980s"), for mechanics that are still true today ("computers run on these things
called hard drives"), and, where the energy genuinely wants it, for a scheme's hottest stretch. What is
banned is the present-tense still life: a camera hovering over a scene with nobody doing anything in
it. Put people in the sentence and have them do something.

**1.2 — Land the point inside the telling; no grandeur buttons.** The recurring failure is ending a
beat on a summary, thesis, or profound line ("the mania did the work"). Land the point where it
happens and keep going. **The mechanical check:** read the last sentence of every block. If it sums up
or moralizes what just happened instead of landing on a fact, an action, or the push into what comes
next, fold it forward or cut it. A dry *factual* aside is the good kind; keep it ("Or so they said.").
Chopping a button down to a fragment on its own line fixes nothing: the defect was the summarizing, not
the length, and a clipped drop only adds the monotone on top. The block still ends on something that
happened.

**1.3 — The wit is abundant, warm, and rides the facts.** Jokes, metaphors, and asides are not
seasoning for a few beats; they run **throughout** — the recurring draft failure is a script that goes
deadpan-flat for stretches. A couple of beats in a row with no joke, analogy, aside, or reaction is a
defect, not restraint. **The register is warm, irreverent, and loaded with pop culture:** a narrator
who is plainly enjoying this. Dry irony is welcome on the lines where it actually lands, but smirking
distance as the house register is out, because it reads as somebody who thinks the story is beneath
him. Not everything is a joke either: plain speech carrying the story is plain speech, and the flat
sentence between two funny ones is what makes them funny. **Profanity is in bounds at the "ass" and
"shit" grade and stops there** ("random shit lying around"); the f-word is not the channel. Every piece
of wit still rides a fact: delete the joke and the fact survives; delete the fact and the joke
collapses.
> "Yeah, that's his real name." · "That's a chunk of land the size of Maryland for a bar tab and a
> necklace." · "My Peloton bike is a scam but these guys? Next level."

**1.4 — Analogies are the explanatory engine.** The strongest tool this channel has. A modern comparison
the viewer instantly pictures IS the explanation, not decoration on top of it — reach for one wherever a
mechanism, a scale, or an absurdity would otherwise need a lecture. Runs of comparisons are welcome, and
a good one can recur as a callback.
> Scale: "Accounting for inflation, they were making as much money as Reddit."
> Mechanism: "It's like reading a textbook, you know? You see that many pages and you just skim through
> and believe the pretty pictures."
> Character: "MacGregor was like the Bernie Madoff of the 1820s, except Madoff sold securities and
> MacGregor sold a fake country."
> Situation: "This was Ocean's Eleven level stuff."
> Era: "It was the dot-com bubble of the 1820s."

Cut a comparison only when it repeats a point already landed, derails the story, or makes the fact
harder to understand. **The universality bar governs every pull, not just the analogies:** a joke, a
metaphor, a turn of phrase, an era anchor, each has to be something a general viewer understands and
pictures instantly. Franchises and memes with staying power are exactly the right material: Doctor
Strange's sanctums, Ocean's Eleven, Megamind, Jordan Belfort, a Peloton, what Reddit makes in a year. A
pull fails the bar when it fades fast, or when a general viewer would need it explained, never because
it is popular. A private literary metaphor fails; so does slang with a shelf life. The approved
excerpts in `channels/the-second-take/example-scripts.md` are the calibration for where that bar sits.

**1.5 — The narrator is present and reacting.** The energy comes from a narrator who is IN the telling:
direct questions answered casually ("After all, who would move to a new country without local
currency?"), conversational joints ("So anyway," "you know," "See?"), honest reactions ("I'd move to a
country with golden rivers too, you know," "The balls on this guy, huh?"), casual repetition when
momentum wants it ("So he thinks, and schemes, and finally, he hatches a plot."), and first-person
asides. Generic "you" is normal speech. Where the research supports a genuine multi-move scheme, the
narrator may announce **Step N** ("**Step 1: Create the Fake Country.**") — it exposes a mechanism,
never bare chronology, and runs as a complete sequential set.

**1.6 — State the amazing thing flat; never pad credibility.** Banned: "that part is real," "he
actually did," "he really did," "seriously," "the maddening thing," "genuinely audacious." State the
insane fact plainly and let the *viewer* be the one amazed. The identity aside is exactly "Yeah, that's
his real name." ("Real" survives where it contrasts real money, land, or a real name with the fake.)
**Heat is carried by the delivery, not by insistence:** capitals on a word the voice would lean on
("And they were HOT.") and the knowing stock phrase a friend uses to build somebody up ("they brought
in the man, the myth, the legend: Q.T. Wiles") are both correct, because they are how the line is
*said*. What stays dead is trailer drama, the clipped fragment that manufactures a mood while carrying
no joke and no new fact ("Everything changed." / "For good.").

## 2. The story

**2.1 — The hook gets them in the door; it does not tell the story.** Its one job is intrigue: leave the
viewer holding exactly one question the story now owes them. **Open on somebody doing something, in a
time and place the viewer already pictures, with the names on the page:** an actor, an event, a
familiar anchor. The **shape is open**, and inventing a new one is fair game. Two that work: the **era
drop-in**, which sets the viewer down in a period on anchors everyone already pictures and lets the
narrator say plainly what kind of story is coming (self-positioning the story is legitimate); and the
**paradox cold-open**, which states the settled, impossible-sounding fact and withholds the mechanism.
Concrete stakes (a number, a count, a fortune) go wherever they land hardest.
> Era drop-in: "We're in the 1980s. Home to big hair, Pac-Man, and one of the funniest corporate scams
> that you've never heard of."
>
> Paradox cold-open: "And one of those companies packaged 26,000... well, the title gives it away...
> red clay bricks into little boxes labelled 'hard drive', and sold them to customers around the world."

What stays dead is outlining the story's beats ("first he did X, then Y"), "he was not some nobody,"
"welcome back," and the still life: a scene with nobody in it, a company left unnamed to manufacture
mystery, a fact stated so abstractly that nothing is happening in the sentence. Naming the company, the
man, and the year in the first breath costs nothing and buys the viewer something to hold.

**Person-led packaging → person-led hook:** when the title/thumbnail promise a person, open ON that
person and name them within two sentences (teasing an unknown name buys nothing); the stakes beat moves
to the turn, it does not vanish.
> "So there was this guy, Gregor MacGregor. Yeah, that's his real name."

**2.2 — Select by story value; develop what you keep.** `research.md` is a pool, not a checklist. Cut
the beats that don't carry the story; give the ones that do their real color — the scene, the concrete
detail, the telling number. Three inert research paragraphs collapse to one beat, but a beat that carries
the story earns more than a hurried sentence. The pipeline's most stubborn miss is stuffing in facts
because they're sourced; the opposite miss — racing through a beat that deserved room — is just as real.
> The beat that carries the story gets the room instead: "They bought 26,000 bricks from the Colorado
> Brick Company, handpicked so that a box with a brick felt and weighed exactly like a box of hard
> drives. Then each box got a serial number, because hard drives have serial numbers, and the whole
> thing was shrink wrapped onto pallets and sent out."

**A fact enters when the story needs it, and not one beat earlier.** Sourced material sitting in
`research.md` has no claim on a position; put each fact where it does work. **Scale is the exception
worth planning for: it lands early, and it lands as a comparison**, because a revenue figure means
nothing on its own and a company the viewer already has a feel for means everything.
> "And at their peak in 1988, they were making over 600 million dollars a year. Accounting for
> inflation, they were making as much money as Reddit. Or so they said."

**2.3 — Let the story set the length inside the 8–10 minute band, natural center near the top of it.**
Word target still follows the **channel voice's measured wpm** (in `dna.md`; e.g. Miles runs ~175 gross
with ~18% pause) — a center of gravity within the band, not a universal constant. That measured rate is
gross, so it already carries the narrator's natural pausing: runtime is words divided by it, and
`lint_script.py` computes the number. Landing well under usually means beats are under-developed
(starved of color), not that filler is missing; running long usually means dwell (§2.5). Add length
only as a genuine cut *thread*, never padding.

**2.4 — Motifs and callbacks carry the telling.** Plant a phrase and reuse it so it pays off ("eight
million acres of land" → "those eight million acres was swampland"). A recurring analogy is a callback
too: the comparison planted in the opening line can come back at the payoff with no new setup. **The
plant is invisible.** The line does its whole job the first time it appears and simply comes back
later; the moment the script tells the viewer to hold onto something because it matters later, the
payoff is spent before it arrives and the machinery is showing. Serial numbers on boxes of bricks are
planted by being *mentioned*, never by being flagged.

**2.5 — Color, not dwell.** *Color* is a new, concrete, specific detail that makes a beat vivid: the
bricks came from the Colorado Brick Company; each box got its own serial number; the guidebook ran 355
pages. *Dwell* is restating a point after it has landed. **The test: does the line add a new fact or image, or re-emphasize one already said?**
Add the first, cut the second. A line that survives with zero information removed is filler. Lean toward
color — the miss we correct most is a draft gone too terse.

**2.6 — Design for retention before drafting.** These are outline decisions, not post-hoc patches: open
on the intrigue in the very first lines (no logo, no throat-clearing); give a real reason to stay inside
the first ~15 seconds; keep new stimulus arriving beat to beat; on longer stories re-arm the tension
around the middle (a new question, a turn, a second scheme); and hold the single best fact for the final
stretch instead of front-loading everything.

**2.7 — A company story tells the rise before the fall, and motivates every step out loud.** The
collapse only hurts if the viewer watched the climb first: who they were, how fast they got big, and
how big "big" was, before anything goes wrong.
> "The company was MiniScribe, a hard drive manufacturer out of Colorado, founded in [verify: year] by
> [verify: founder(s)/garage]. And they were HOT."

From there, every escalation says out loud what caused it. The chain is spoken, not implied: pressure
produced a hire, the hire produced impossible targets, the targets produced fear, the fear produced the
plan.
> "MiniScribe was struggling after facing increasing competition from other hard drive manufacturers,
> so they brought in the man, the myth, the legend: Q.T. Wiles."
>
> "Wiles set impossible sales targets, and missing them meant your head was on the chopping block. So
> the managers put their heads together, and hatched a brilliant plan."

A beat that merely happens next, with no because, is where the viewer quietly stops caring.

## 3. The structure

**3.1 — Impose a shape; refuse flat chronology.** Design the sequence in the outline before drafting —
sequence to land hardest, not by the calendar.

**3.2 — Paradox-hook, then rewind.** Open on the sharpest contradiction as a settled fact with the
mechanism withheld ("one of those companies packaged 26,000... red clay bricks into little boxes
labelled 'hard drive', and sold them to customers around the world"), then reset into the story ("The
company was MiniScribe, a hard drive manufacturer out of Colorado..."). Composes with the person-led
hook (§2.1) and runs straight into the rise beat (§2.7).

**3.3 — Cross-cut, but consolidate — don't ping-pong.** Weave parallel threads, but finish a thread
cleanly before cutting, with a casual signpost ("Meanwhile, back in London…"). One clean cut beats four
choppy ones. For a pre-spoiled ending, the **irony cross-cut** — obliviousness against the gathering
disaster — is the main engine (§3.6).

**3.4 — Casual transitions, question turns, and forward-promise seams.** Spoken connective tissue and
the question-answered-casually move ("So how does a warehouse full of bricks pass an audit? Well…").
What is dead is the literary connector ("which brings us to," "little did they know") and the announced
category ("here's the strange part," "the uncomfortable question," "piece one/two/three"): **deliver the
moment, don't announce its category**, and open on the content itself. A plain spoken doorway that opens the
story ("Here is the story of that company.") is normal speech, not an announced transition. A beat exits
on an open loop or forward promise the viewer stays to collect, and escalation is honest ("it gets
worse" is followed by something actually worse).

**3.5 — The ending is a tone, not a formula.** Stop the way a friend stops telling a story: casual,
brisk, unceremonious, usually landing an ironic observation or one last laugh. Settle what happened to
everybody in a line and get out.
> "In the end, MiniScribe went bankrupt in 1990, their investors were screwed, and Q.T. Wiles lost his
> Fix It reputation. The big winner was probably the Colorado Brick Company, who scored a major payday
> selling bricks to a tech company."

That is one ending that works, not the shape every ending takes; another story stops on a fate, a joke,
or a plain last fact. What they share is that nobody is being taught anything. Banned: the essay
conclusion ("here's what it means," "and that's why"), the moral, and the crafted profundity written to
sound resonant ("That's a workday"), because a line reaching for meaning drains everything the story
just earned. The insight is woven into the body.

**3.6 — Pre-spoiled tension.** Our titles usually give away the ending, so carry tension on something
other than the outcome: dramatic irony, comic dread (make them wait for the doom you can both see
coming), and how-badly / how-exactly assembly.

**3.7 — Non-linear, but followable.** Jumping around is the craft; the bar is that a first-time viewer
can follow the causal through-line. Introduce a person, place, or company before the story leans on it;
establish a connection before a beat depends on it. The line is *withheld* versus *unestablished*: a
question the story clearly raises and answers later is suspense (good); a connection used as if already
understood is a hole. Structural confusion gets a structural fix — resequence — never an explaining
sentence pasted onto the confusing beat.

## 4. Staging & facts

- **Casual is not loose with facts.** The friendly voice stays leashed to `research.md`. Correct what's
  wrong, and never invent to fill a hole.
- **Hedging happens in fact selection, never in the narration.** Deciding which version of a contested
  fact is solid enough to tell is the writer's job, done before the line exists. A claim either goes in
  as the strongest version the ledger supports, stated flat, or it does not go in. "By one account,"
  "sources disagree," "reportedly," "the record doesn't support" and every cousin of theirs are banned
  from the voiceover: the viewer hears a narrator apologising for his own story, and the beat dies.
- **A genuine gap in the record gets transparent speculation, in the narrator's voice.** When nobody
  knows why something happened and the story needs a why, say plainly that nobody knows, float the live
  possibilities as guesses, and move on inside the same breath.
  > "Don't ask me why. Maybe Wiles was just that charismatic, or maybe there was some under-the-table
  > dealing going on. Either way, the same twenty six thousand bricks..."

  The viewer can hear that this is a guess, which is exactly the point: nothing is passed off as fact,
  and nobody has been made to sit through a confession about sourcing. This is the only sanctioned way
  a hole in the research reaches the audio.
- **Reported speech is the default telling mode.** Dramatized beats usually land best as the narrator
  *characterizing* what was said, with attitude — and a paraphrase may never add a detail the source
  doesn't support.
- **Defamation discipline:** present the documented facts and let the conclusion be the viewer's
  inference, never the channel's accusation. Dramatize the *act of checking* before an accusation, and
  land the payoff on the *system*, not just the person.
- **Staging craft:** open the villain with documented motive so the con reads as one person's obsession,
  and use the analogy-that-indicts (explains the mechanism *and* exposes its flaw).

## 5. The before → after bank

The taste defects the pipeline reliably produces, as transformations (what critics scan for):

| The recurring AI-tell | The fix |
| --- | --- |
| Clipped one-liner monotone: sentence after sentence as its own paragraph | Idea blocks of four to five sentences; the short line lands inside one (§1.1) |
| Concept-prose ("make the world's version match his target") | Say who did what, to what, and what came of it (§1.1) |
| A line that doesn't parse on first listen ("That's it doing that") | Name the thing; no pronoun the viewer has to resolve (§1.1) |
| Audible hedge ("by one account," "sources disagree") | State the strongest sourced version flat, or cut the claim (§4) |
| Visible payoff-plant ("hold onto that, it matters later") | Mention the fact where it does work and let the callback land cold (§2.4) |
| Present-tense still life with nobody in it | Past tense, people doing things (§1.1) |
| Literary/essayist phrasing | Plain and short, the way it would be said aloud |
| Grandeur button ending a beat | End on the fact/action; the point lands inside the telling |
| Restating the premise over and over | Say it once, hard, then trust the audience |
| Outline-then-retell fluff ("he was not some nobody") | Cut it; just tell what he was |
| Literary connector or announced category | Open on the content itself; a casual signpost or a question turn (§3.4) |
| Cramming every sourced fact in | Select and compress to one vivid beat (§2.2) |
| Essay/moral close, or crafted profundity ("That's a workday") | Stop the way a friend stops: the fates, then a last laugh (§3.5) |
| Dwell — restating a landed point | Say it once, on the sharpest image, then move |
| Triple-build that restates one idea three ways | One clean line |
| Too terse — compressing away the vivid detail | Keep the specific color (§2.5) |
| Credibility padding ("he actually did") | State the fact directly (§1.6) |

## 6. The joke toolbox (reach for these; never force them)

All fact-riding, and used **often** (§1.3 — abundance is the default, flatness is the defect):

- **Anachronistic analogy that teaches the mechanism** (the workhorse): "the Bernie Madoff of the
  1820s, except Madoff sold securities and MacGregor sold a fake country." The same move closes a beat
  as a kicker: "This guy might even put Jordan Belfort to shame."
- **Deadpan undercut / bathos:** "And what does he get in return for all his efforts? 250 customers."
- **Ironic re-label:** "he's turned their savings into his homemade magic money."
- **Comic false precision** (exact numbers for deadpan authority): "a chunk of land the size of
  Maryland for a bar tab and a necklace."
- **Deflate-the-powerful** (the mastermind reduced to an ordinary man in a lucky moment): "It's not
  even that MacGregor was Megamind, you know. He wasn't some super intelligent con man; people just
  didn't care."
- **Viewer-solidarity aside** (the narrator admits they'd fall for it too): "I'd move to a country with
  golden rivers too, you know."
- **Playing out the mark's inner monologue:** "You see that many pages and you just skim through and
  believe the pretty pictures like 'wow, that looks so real. Somebody must know what they're talking
  about.'"
- **Irreverent reaction** (the narrator says out loud what the viewer is thinking): "The balls on this
  guy, huh?"
- **Franchise pull that carries the fact:** "like Doctor Strange with his many sanctums, Doctor Fix It
  ran several other companies at the same time."
- **The knowing build-up before the fall:** "they brought in the man, the myth, the legend: Q.T. Wiles."
- **Scale by modern comparison:** "Accounting for inflation, they were making as much money as Reddit."
- **Narrator at his own expense:** "My Peloton bike is a scam but these guys? Next level."
- **Genre re-label** (name the movie the story has turned into): "This was Ocean's Eleven level stuff."
- **Historical irony where the real fact is the punchline:** the bricks had serial numbers.

**The humor bar:** a joke ships only if it clears the universality bar (§1.4), the words are plain, and
the narrator states the insane fact flat and lets the *viewer* be the one amazed. It also has to
actually land: warm or dry, a swing that misses costs energy and returns nothing, so cut it rather than
keep it for texture. Punch up at power and absurdity, never at the mark or the viewer. Profanity rides
along at the "shit" grade and no further.
