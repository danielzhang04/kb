## Long-form VO

[B-ROLL: a single steel rod threaded through a nut, extreme close-up, the thread stripping under strain] This is the connection that held up two walkways in a hotel atrium. It was never asked to do anything unusual. [PAUSE] And then, one afternoon, it was asked to do exactly twice as much.

[B-ROLL: wide interior of a grand hotel atrium, skywalks crossing overhead, crowd below] July 17th, 1981. The Hyatt Regency in Kansas City. A tea dance fills the lobby. Above the crowd hang two suspended walkways, one directly over the other. To everyone standing there, they look permanent. Engineered. Safe.

This is the anatomy of how a single line on a drawing — a change that made the job *easier to build* — quietly doubled the load on one steel connection, and brought both walkways down into the crowd.

[B-ROLL: clean 3D schematic of the atrium, two stacked walkways suspended from the ceiling by long rods] Start with the original design. The fourth-floor walkway and the second-floor walkway were meant to hang from the same set of long steel rods, running continuously from the ceiling all the way down. [PAUSE] One rod. Two walkways. Each walkway's weight carried straight up to the roof.

[B-ROLL: annotated diagram — a continuous rod passing through both box beams, load arrows drawn to the ceiling] In that design, the connection at the fourth floor holds one thing: the fourth-floor walkway. The rod simply passes through and keeps going. Clean. That was the drawing that got approved.

[B-ROLL: two side-by-side detail drawings, "as designed" vs "as built", the single rod becoming two offset rods] But a continuous threaded rod is a nightmare to build. You'd have to thread the entire lower length of rod through the upper walkway's connection. So a revised detail was proposed: cut the rod in two. Hang the upper walkway from the ceiling — and hang the lower walkway from the upper one.

[PAUSE] It sounds like the same thing. It is not the same thing.

[B-ROLL: force diagram — original connection carries load "W", modified connection carries "2W", the number doubling on screen] In the original, the fourth-floor connection carried the weight of one walkway. In the revised version, that same connection now carried the fourth-floor walkway *and* everything hanging beneath it from the second floor. One connection. Double the load. [PAUSE] Nobody re-ran the number.

[B-ROLL: extreme close-up, annotated — a steel box beam made of two channels welded together, a nut bearing on the underside] And the connection itself was already the weak point. The walkways hung from box beams — two steel channels welded toe-to-toe. The rod passed through, and a single nut underneath held the whole thing up, bearing directly on the welded seam.

--- MID-VIDEO RE-ARM ---

[B-ROLL: the force diagram again, the load arrow now pushing up against the welded seam] So here is the overlooked line, in physical terms. That welded seam was now being asked to hold twice what anyone had calculated — and it was holding it at the exact spot two pieces of steel were only tacked together.

[B-ROLL: archival exterior of the Hyatt Regency hotel, period-correct, no people] Investigators later found that even the *original* connection was under-designed for the building code. The modified one had roughly half the strength it needed. It had been standing, quietly overloaded, since the day it opened.

[B-ROLL: slow push-in on a structural drawing with a revision cloud and initials in the title block] The change moved through on a shop drawing — a fabrication detail. A phone call, a revised sketch, an approval stamp. The kind of routine edit that happens on every large project. [PAUSE] The question the investigation kept circling was simple: who was supposed to check that this change was safe — and did anyone actually run the math again?

[B-ROLL: motion-graphic of the load path failing — the nut pulling through the box beam, the seam splitting] Under the crowd's weight that afternoon, the seam let go. The nut pulled straight through the box beam. The fourth-floor walkway dropped — and because the second-floor walkway was hanging from it, both came down together.

[B-ROLL: aftermath framed strictly on structure — a severed walkway resting at an angle, hanging rods, empty] It remains one of the deadliest structural failures in American history. We are not going to dwell on the crowd below. The forensic point is the steel.

[PAUSE] And here is the detail I saved for last. [B-ROLL: the two side-by-side details again, slow zoom onto the single changed rod] The engineers of record and the fabricator each believed the *other* had verified the connection. The change looked so minor — cut one rod into two — that it slipped through the gap between two people who both assumed it had been checked. The math that would have caught it takes about a minute.

[B-ROLL: the original single-rod schematic, restored, holding — then fading to black] The walkway could have held. The design that would have saved it was the one already on the approved drawing. Someone just made it easier to build.
