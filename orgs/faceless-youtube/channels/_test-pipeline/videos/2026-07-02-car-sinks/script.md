## LONG-FORM VOICEOVER

> Markup: `[B-ROLL: …]` is the shot-list cue for visual-prompt-writer. `[PAUSE]` is a deliberate

### HOOK (00:00)

[B-ROLL: black frame, then first-person POV inside a car — the nose already tipped down toward dark
water, dashboard lights still glowing, the windshield half-swallowed. Water sliding up the glass.]

You're in the water. The dash is still lit, the radio's still playing, and the cold is already at
your ankles.

[PAUSE]

[B-ROLL: on-screen clock element flickers to life in the corner — 00:00 — and starts counting up.]

The clock started the second you went in. It's running right now. So let's watch it, together, all
the way down.

### SECOND GATE (00:22) — cold-open-then-rewind

[B-ROLL: quick cut to over-the-shoulder — hands frozen on the wheel, water beading up the door glass
from outside.]

Here's the part nobody tells you. For the next thirty seconds to two minutes, the car floats. The
engine's dead, the world outside is black water, and yet you're bobbing there like nothing is wrong.
It feels like the worst is already behind you.

[PAUSE]

It isn't. That floating time is the only real time you have. And most people spend it doing the exact
thing that gets them killed — they reach for the door.

[B-ROLL: match cut — the same lit dashboard, rewinding, water draining back down the glass, back to a
dry road at night.]

So let's rewind. Ten seconds, back to the road, back to before. And then we go through this the way
it actually happens. Second by second. No skipping ahead.

### PREMISE / PROMISE (01:06)

[B-ROLL: night road, headlights, a bend, a guardrail. Clean stylized animation — deep indigo, one
hazard-orange reflector on the rail.]

By the end of this, you'll know exactly what the water does to the car, what it does to your body,
and the precise order of moves that gets you out — before the window closes for good.

[PAUSE]

Because there is a window. A real one, measured in seconds. And there's a lock on it. It's just not
the lock you're picturing. The thing holding you inside this car has no key, no latch, and no mercy.
And in about a minute, you're going to meet it. Everything you think you know about escaping this —
everything the movies taught you, everything your gut is about to tell you — is going to be exactly
backwards. So forget all of it. We start from zero.

### THE FLOAT PHASE — first cascade (01:58)

[B-ROLL: the car breaks through the guardrail, drops, hits the water flat with a heavy slap. Cut to
exterior — it bobs, floating, headlights glowing under the surface.]

You hit the water. And the car doesn't sink. Not yet.

The air trapped in the cabin, in the doors, in every sealed pocket of the body, holds you up like a
boat with a slow leak. Thirty seconds. Sometimes a full two minutes. The heavier the engine, the
faster the nose tips — but you're still floating, and floating still feels like safety.

[B-ROLL: interior — water climbing up the outside of the doors, past the sill, past the bottom of the
window. A thin line of water finding its way in around the door seal.]

And this is the exact moment your body turns against you. Cold water hits your skin and your nervous
system does something you don't get a vote on. You gasp — a huge, involuntary breath. Your heart rate
spikes. Your hands start to shake. Your thoughts go loud and useless.

[PAUSE]

[B-ROLL: close on the corner clock — it reads 00:30. A subtle hazard-orange pulse.]

That's the cold-shock response. It lasts about a minute, and for that entire minute you are not
thinking clearly — which is the cruelest possible timing, because this minute is the only one that
counts. Every good decision you will ever make in this car, you have to make now, while your own body
is screaming at you to panic.

[B-ROLL: POV — a hand reaching for the door handle. Pulling. The door not moving. Pulling harder.]

So you do what anyone would do. You reach for the door. You pull the handle. And the door does not
move.

### MACRO RE-HOOK — the named antagonist (03:26)

[B-ROLL: exterior cross-section — the waterline outside the door sitting higher than the water inside
the cabin. Two arrows: a small one pushing out, a huge hazard-orange wall of arrows pushing in.]

Meet your antagonist. It isn't the lock. It isn't a jammed mechanism, or a bent frame, or bad luck.
It's the water itself — and it has already won an argument you didn't know you were having.

The moment the water outside climbs higher than the water inside the cabin, it starts pressing on
that door. Not gently. Flat, even, everywhere at once — hundreds of pounds of force spread across the
whole panel. The deeper you go, the harder it pushes.

[PAUSE]

You are not strong enough to open a door against a swimming pool. Nobody is. Not a bodybuilder, not
two people throwing themselves at it together. The physics simply do not care how hard you try. Think
about the deepest water you've ever swum down into and how your ears ached at the bottom of a pool.
Now put a flat door between you and all of it, and try to push the whole weight of that water out of
your way with one arm. That's the fight you just picked. That's the fight you're going to lose.

[B-ROLL: the hand giving up on the handle. Shoulder throwing against the door — no movement. The
water inside now at the seat.]

And here's the trap inside the trap. Every second you spend fighting that door, the water keeps
climbing, the pressure keeps growing, and the one exit you actually have is quietly closing behind
your back. You are spending your only minute losing an argument with the ocean.

[B-ROLL: slow pan to the side window — still above the waterline, still glass, still air on the other
side.]

Because there is a second way out. And right now — right this second — it's still open.

[B-ROLL: corner clock reads 01:00.]

The window.

### THE WINDOW — the order of operations (05:14)

[B-ROLL: clean animated sequence, almost instructional but tense — a hand unclipping a seatbelt.]

Seatbelt first. Off. This sounds obvious and it is the step people skip. If the belt has you pinned,
you can't turn, you can't reach, you can't do anything else — and belts jam under load, right when
you need them to release. So the belt comes off first, while your hands still answer to you.

[PAUSE]

[B-ROLL: the same hand moving to the window switch. The window sliding down — a beat of relief — then
stopping halfway.]

Then the window. Not the door — the door is gone, forget the door. While the car still has power, the
electric switch may still work. So you press it, and the glass starts to drop, and for one second it
feels like the whole nightmare just handed you a way out.

[B-ROLL: the window stops. Water begins to spill over the half-open glass into the cabin.]

But power doesn't last. Water and a car's electrical system have about sixty seconds of conversation
before the whole thing shorts and dies. And once it dies, that switch is just a dead button under
your finger. The window you were counting on stops halfway, and the water pours in over the top of
it.

[B-ROLL: a small hard object — a headrest post, a spring-loaded punch — pressed to the corner of the
glass. The glass webs, then bursts inward in a sheet of water.]

If the switch is gone, the glass has to break — and this is where knowing one small fact saves your
life. Not the windshield. The windshield is laminated, layered like safety glass, built to survive an
impact and stay in one piece. You will never break it in time. The side windows are different. They're
tempered. Hit the corner — not the center, the corner — and the whole pane turns to gravel in an
instant.

[PAUSE]

[B-ROLL: exterior — water pouring in through the broken window, the car now dropping faster, nose
down.]

And the instant you break it, the whole deal changes. The water that was your enemy at the door
becomes your doorway. It floods in fast and cold, the car loses the last of its float and drops, and
you no longer get to choose your moment. You have to go with the water — now — while there's still a
hole to go through.

### MID-VIDEO RE-ARM (07:22) — 55–65% — the turn

[B-ROLL: interior going dark — dashboard lights flickering out one by one. The cabin lit only by a
last blue glow, then nothing.]

And here's where everything you've been counting on quietly betrays you.

The lights just died. All of them. And that means the one thing you assumed you'd always have —
seeing the way out — is gone. You are now inside a metal box, underwater, in total darkness, and the
box is still filling.

[PAUSE]

[B-ROLL: near-black frame, faint movement, bubbles, the outline of a headrest. Disorientation.]

Down here, in the cold and the black, people lose which way is up. There is no light to swim toward,
no floor, no ceiling, no horizon. The window you were aiming for is somewhere to your left, or your
right, or behind your shoulder — and the whole car is slowly rolling as it fills, turning you with
it, erasing the map in your head second by second.

[B-ROLL: a hand feeling along the door frame, finding the empty window opening — the one landmark.]

So you stop trying to see. You reach instead. The moment you break that window, you put a hand on its
frame and you do not let go — because that jagged empty square is now the only fixed point in a room
that no longer has an up. Lose it, and you are just turning in the dark. Keep it, and you always know
exactly where the door to the world is.

[B-ROLL: corner clock reads 02:00. It stops flickering. Holds.]

The cabin is almost full now. And here's the strange twist the water saved for last: once it's
completely full, inside and out, the pressure finally equalizes. The door — the one you couldn't
budge, the one that started all of this — would open now, easily, like nothing was ever wrong.

[PAUSE]

But that's a plan for a different person, one who did everything else right and had the air to wait.
For you, the window is already broken, your hand is already on the frame, and you are already halfway
through it. You don't need the door anymore. You never really did.

### THE WITHHELD PEAK — final 20% (09:24)

[B-ROLL: the cabin fully flooded, near silent. The POV drifting toward the pale square of the open
window, the last light in the frame.]

And now the part almost nobody talks about. The last ten seconds.

When the cabin fills completely, the noise stops. No more rushing water, no more groaning metal, no
alarms. It goes quiet — deep, total, underwater quiet. And after two minutes of chaos, that quiet
feels like peace. It feels like the moment it's finally over and you can rest.

[PAUSE]

It is the single most dangerous feeling in this entire scenario. Because your body — starved of
oxygen, chilled, completely overwhelmed — will offer you a strange, warm calm. A the-worst-is-over
calm. It is lying to you. That calm is the last thing a lot of people ever feel, and they feel it
while sitting perfectly still, waiting for a rescue that a quiet brain has already decided is coming.

[B-ROLL: the pale window opening, slightly closer. The hand still on the frame. One kick.]

The people who live are the ones who refuse to trust the quiet. Who treat that silence not as a
finish line but as the starting gun. One hand on the frame. One hard kick off the seat. And they pull
themselves toward the last patch of grey light — not because they feel strong, but because they
decided, a full minute ago, that they would. That's the whole secret. Not strength, not luck, not
holding your breath a little longer. The people who get out are the ones who already knew, before the
water ever touched them, that the calm was a trap and the window was the answer.

[B-ROLL: POV rising through the window, out of the car, the dark hull dropping away below into deeper
black, the surface a shimmering ceiling far above.]

[PAUSE]

And then you're out. The car keeps sinking without you, dropping away into a black you don't want to
look at. And above you — impossibly far, but there — is a surface that's still lit. Still the same
night. Still, somewhere behind it, the same radio playing on a road you drove off of two minutes ago.

### EMOTIONAL CLOSE (11:30)

[B-ROLL: from underwater, looking up at the rippling surface light, the shape of the world above
distorted and gold.]

The whole thing took about two minutes. And you spent the first thirty seconds of it completely
certain that you were fine.

[PAUSE]

[B-ROLL: hold on the surface light. The corner clock quietly fades. No resolution graphic, no
recap.]

That's the part that stays with you afterward. Not the cold. Not the dark. The half a minute you sat
still and believed you had all the time in the world.

[B-ROLL: cut to black. Small on-screen line: "Researched from vehicle-submersion survival studies.
Animation is AI-generated." No spoken outro.]

### LOOP-OUT (11:55)

[B-ROLL: end-screen — a single dark thumbnail glowing at the edge of frame: the frozen-lake video,
same corner clock, unstarted.]

If that half-minute of false safety got under your skin, there's another one waiting for you out on
the ice. The clock there starts the moment it cracks.
