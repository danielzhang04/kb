# The Man Who Sold the Eiffel Tower (Twice)

- **Channel:** The Second Take
- **Approx. word count:** ~1,450 words · est. runtime ~9-10 min
- **Register:** casual-friend storytelling; topic-gravity dial hot on the con and the money-absurdity; third-person, one narrator, no quotes, zero em/en dashes.

---

[B-ROLL: the Eiffel Tower being sized up like scrap in a junkyard]
In 1925, a man in Paris sold the Eiffel Tower for scrap metal. He was not the mayor. He did not own it, he had never built anything, and he had no more right to sell it than to sell the moon. [BEAT] And then, a few weeks later, once the money was safely gone, he came back to Paris and sold the exact same tower all over again.

[B-ROLL: the one man behind it, calm in a very good suit]
So who does that? His name was Victor Lustig, and by 1925 he had already been lying for a living for a very long time.

[B-ROLL: a young con man working the first-class deck of an ocean liner]
Lustig grew up in what's now the Czech Republic, and he had one real gift. Languages. He spoke five of them cleanly, with the kind of easy manners that make rich people relax around you. He'd spent years working the ocean liners that ran between Europe and America, the floating palaces full of bored millionaires with nothing to do for a week but gamble. Lustig gave them something to do. And by the time the war shut the crossings down, he'd worked out the one idea that ran under every con he would ever pull. People don't get taken because they're stupid. They get taken because they want the deal to be real.

[B-ROLL: a small newspaper story about the aging tower and its brutal upkeep bills]
It started with a newspaper. In the spring of 1925, Lustig was reading the Paris papers and found a little story most people would have skipped right past. The Eiffel Tower was in trouble. It had gone up back in 1889 as a temporary attraction for a world's fair, it was never really built to last, and now, decades later, the paint was peeling and the repair bills were enormous. The city was quietly wondering whether the whole thing was even worth keeping. Most readers thought, huh, shame. Lustig thought, there's my tower.

[B-ROLL: forged government letterhead coming off a press, looking perfectly official]
So he went to work. He had stationery printed up for the Ministry of Posts and Telegraphs, the government department that actually looked after the tower. Real letterhead, the real names, the works. And then he promoted himself. On paper he became a deputy director-general of the ministry, a man senior enough to be trusted with something delicate and important, which was exactly what he was about to offer.

[B-ROLL: a discreet, confidential invitation going out to the city's biggest scrap dealers]
Then he sent a handful of confidential letters to five of the biggest scrap-metal dealers in Paris, inviting them to a private meeting at the Hotel de Crillon. And the Crillon mattered. It was one of the grandest hotels in the city, the kind of place where actual government business happened, and it told these men, before Lustig had said a single word, that this was real.

[B-ROLL: dealers seated in a private suite, being sworn to secrecy]
He sat them down in a suite and told them a secret. The tower, he explained, was falling apart. The upkeep had become impossible, the repairs cost more than the city could carry, and the government had quietly decided that the Eiffel Tower was going to be torn down and sold off, for scrap. Seven thousand tons of iron. But it was a scandal waiting to happen, because the public loved the thing, so the whole sale had to stay completely secret until it was done. That, he said, was why they'd been called in person, in private, instead of reading it in the paper. Then he loaded them into a limousine and drove them out to the tower to look at it, as their tower, and let them run the numbers on seven thousand tons of metal.

[B-ROLL: one bidder, newer than the rest, desperate to prove he belongs]
Now, five dealers, but Lustig only needed one. And he'd already picked him. A man named Andre Poisson, who was newer to the Paris scene than the others, a little on the outside of the club, and hungry to land the kind of deal that would finally make the older dealers take him seriously. Which made him perfect. Lustig didn't want the sharpest man in the room. He wanted the one who most wanted it to be true.

[B-ROLL: a government official quietly hinting he needs a little something on the side]
So when Poisson started hesitating, and his wife started asking why all of this was so secret and so fast, Lustig did the thing that closed it. He met Poisson privately, dropped the stiff official act for a moment, and confided, one man of the world to another, that a government salary really didn't stretch very far, and that an arrangement like this usually came with a little something extra for the official handling it. He asked for a bribe. [BEAT] And that sealed it. Because a corrupt official asking to be greased was the most reassuring thing Poisson had heard all week. An honest bureaucrat might be a fake. A crooked one had to be the real thing.

[B-ROLL: a case of cash changing hands, then a train pulling out of the station]
So Poisson paid. He handed over the money for the tower, plus the bribe on top, and Lustig handed him a beautifully forged bill of sale. And then Lustig did the only sensible thing a man can do after selling a national monument he does not own. He got on a train to Vienna, with the cash.

[B-ROLL: the con man in Vienna, reading the papers, waiting for a storm that never comes]
And then he waited. He sat in Vienna reading the French papers every morning, waiting for the story to break. The arrest, the outrage, his own face on the front page. And nothing came. A day, then another, then a week. Still nothing. Because Andre Poisson, it turned out, could not bring himself to tell a single soul. Think about what he would have to admit. That he had bought the Eiffel Tower. That he had paid a bribe in order to buy the Eiffel Tower. He would rather quietly swallow the loss than become the biggest joke in France. So he said nothing at all.

[B-ROLL: the con man realizing his crime left no trace, and turning back toward Paris]
And when Lustig worked that out, he understood what he had actually gotten away with. There was no complaint. No police report. No story. Nothing anywhere to say the Eiffel Tower had ever been sold in the first place. So he did the thing almost nobody alive would have the nerve to do. He packed his bags, went back to Paris, and ran the whole con a second time. New letters, new dealers, the same hotel, the same secret, the same tower he still did not own. And it very nearly worked all over again. But this time the mark got a bad feeling before the money was gone, and he went to the police. So Lustig fell back on the trick he was best at. He disappeared, and caught a boat to America.

[B-ROLL: a small polished wooden box with slots and brass dials]
And America was where he found his best trick yet. It was a little mahogany box with a couple of slots and some brass dials, and Lustig called it a money-printing machine. The pitch was simple. You feed a real hundred-dollar bill into one slot, wait six hours for the chemicals to do their work, and out come two identical bills. He would demonstrate it, slowly, and sure enough, out slid a perfect, genuine hundred. What the buyer never saw was that Lustig had loaded two real bills into the box in advance, so the machine could only ever make exactly two before it went back to spitting out blank paper. But by the time anyone figured that out, Lustig had sold them the miracle box for a small fortune and was already three cities away. He sold that box up and down the country, and it worked for the exact same reason the tower had. People wanted the deal to be real.

[B-ROLL: a counterfeiting operation big enough to draw the federal government]
In the end, though, Lustig drifted too close to the one thing his whole life had been dancing around. Instead of selling a box that pretended to print money, he started actually printing it, running a counterfeiting operation big enough to make the government genuinely nervous about the dollar. And that is a completely different level of heat. The law came for him. He was arrested, and, being Lustig, he escaped from the jail in Manhattan by lowering himself out a window on a rope of bedsheets, in broad daylight, tipping his hat to the crowd on the way down. It bought him about a month. Then they caught him again.

[B-ROLL: the paperwork closing quietly on a man who once sold monuments]
Victor Lustig pleaded guilty and was sent to Alcatraz, the one room even he could not talk his way out of. He died there of pneumonia in 1947. And on his death certificate, under occupation, someone wrote apprentice salesman. Which, when you think about the tower, and the box, and the bribe he asked for on purpose, might be the most honest thing anyone ever wrote about the man. He never owned the Eiffel Tower. But for a few weeks in 1925, in a quiet hotel suite, he sold it twice, and the man who bought it was too ashamed to ever say a word.
