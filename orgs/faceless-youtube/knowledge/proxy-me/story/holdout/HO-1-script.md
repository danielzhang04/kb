# Script — The Prince Who Sold a Country That Didn't Exist

- **Source idea:** ST-009 · **Channel:** The Second Take · **Target:** 8–12 min · **Status:** scripted
- **Voice:** The Second Take narrator — dry-smart insider who tells it like a story
- **Estimated runtime:** ~11:00  *(≈1,655 words ÷ 150 wpm)*  ·  humanized (no em-dashes) · short beats only where they land dry wit ("It didn't exist."), no dramatic-portent staccato
- **Leashed to:** `research.md` fact ledger. Hedged per Open-Questions (death toll = "more than half"; no precise second-bond figure; no exact trial date).

## LONG-FORM VOICEOVER

[B-ROLL: a crowded 1822 London dock — families with trunks and children boarding a tall ship] In the autumn of 1822, a few hundred British families packed up their entire lives and boarded ships bound for the other side of the world. [B-ROLL: an elegant hand-drawn map, a lush green coastline in beautiful script] They sold their homes, their furniture, the tools of their trades, all of it, to go and build a new future in a country called Poyais.

[PAUSE] There was one small problem with Poyais. [PAUSE:LONG] It didn't exist.

[B-ROLL: the narrator avatar, flat expression, holding a crisp banknote stamped "POYAIS"] And I don't mean it was rougher than the brochure, or a bit smaller than they'd hoped. I mean the place was never there. Where Poyais was supposed to be, there was a stretch of empty jungle on the coast of Central America, and nothing else. No towns, no farms, no harbour. And yet the man who sold it to those families had given that empty jungle a flag, a capital city, a national bank, its own printed currency, and a ruling prince. [BEAT] The prince was him.

[B-ROLL: the trading floor of the London Stock Exchange, gentlemen in top hats] Here's the detail that still doesn't feel real. That same year, a respectable investor could walk onto the floor of the London Stock Exchange and lend money to the government of Poyais, and the paper he got back changed hands like any other country's. A nation that existed only on paper was being treated, by serious people with serious money, as completely real. [PAUSE] This is the story of how a made-up country ended up trading on the London exchange. And the honest answer isn't "one unusually good liar." It's a whole room of people who badly wanted to believe him.

[B-ROLL: a formal portrait of MacGregor in military uniform, medals] His name was Gregor MacGregor, and the first thing to understand is that he wasn't some drifter with a good story. He was a real soldier with a real reputation. He had gone to South America and fought in the wars of independence against Spain, served under Francisco de Miranda and under Simón Bolívar, and risen to the rank of general. That is the kind of history you cannot fake, because thousands of people watched it happen. [BEAT] So when he came home talking about distant lands, he was already believable.

[B-ROLL: a hand-drawn coastline; a local king in a feathered headdress signing a document] And there was a grain of truth underneath the whole thing. In 1820, a king on the Mosquito Coast, in what is now Honduras and Nicaragua, really did sign over an enormous piece of land to him. Something like eight million acres of it. [PAUSE] So MacGregor was not inventing everything out of nothing. He genuinely held a title and a grant. The grant just happened to be for humid, undeveloped jungle that no European settler could realistically live on. To an honest man, that is a crushing disappointment. [BEAT] To MacGregor, it was raw material.

[B-ROLL: MacGregor draped in a sash, the ornate title "Cazique of Poyais"] Because back in Britain, he stopped describing his grant as a wilderness. He started describing it as a young, civilised country that simply needed good, hardworking people to come and settle it, and he gave himself a title to match: the Cazique, which is to say the Prince, of Poyais. Then he did the thing that separates a con man from a daydreamer. He didn't just tell the story. He manufactured the evidence for it.

[B-ROLL: a thick leather-bound book, camera pushing in on the title page: "Sketch of the Mosquito Shore… Poyais"] There was a full guidebook to Poyais, three hundred and fifty pages of it, describing the country in warm, practical detail for anyone thinking of emigrating, published under the name of a Captain Thomas Strangeways. [BEAT] Captain Thomas Strangeways did not exist either. The book had been produced by MacGregor's own circle, and it painted a picture of a place that was ready and waiting for you.

[B-ROLL: an illustrated town — a domed cathedral, an opera house, a grand bank, tidy streets, a river] It described the capital, a town called St Joseph, with paved streets and grand public buildings: a domed cathedral, a bank, an opera house, even a parliament. It described good farmland and a welcoming climate. And not one single piece of it had ever been built or planted. [B-ROLL: crisp "Poyais dollars" fanned across a table] MacGregor even printed money for his country, real physical Poyais banknotes. And here is the genuinely nasty part of the scheme: emigrants were encouraged to change their solid British pounds into these Poyais dollars before they left, so they would have local currency when they arrived. [PAUSE] Which means the con had already swapped their life savings for souvenir paper, before the ship had even left the harbour.

[B-ROLL: a busy land office; a clerk at a counter, a nervous shoemaker with a coin purse] He opened land offices in London and up in Scotland, and he sold plots of Poyais to ordinary working people: a servant, a shoemaker, a clerk with a little put by. [PAUSE] You can picture how the sale went. A man comes in with his life savings and asks what an acre costs. "Well, it was three shillings last month. But there has been a lot of interest lately, and prices are moving." [BEAT] "Interest from whom?" "Oh, you would be surprised." And of course he buys, right there, because the only thing worse than paying a little too much is standing around hesitating while somebody else grabs the last of it. The price kept climbing as the demand climbed, and the demand, mostly, was just the price climbing.

[B-ROLL: an ornate bond certificate reading "POYAIS", passing between gloved hands] Then MacGregor went after the professionals. He arranged a Poyais government loan on the London market, commonly put at around two hundred thousand pounds, floated through a respectable London banking house, exactly the way a real country would raise money abroad. Sit with what that sentence actually contains: a national loan, for a government, of a country that was not there. [PAUSE] And it did not get laughed out of the room. It traded on the real exchange, at prices in the same neighbourhood as genuine new South American debt. [B-ROLL: a price list of nations — Colombia, Chile, Peru — with "Poyais" slotted in among them] On the list of investable nations, Poyais sat quietly between real ones, and hardly anybody blinked.

[B-ROLL: the narrator avatar, leaning in slightly] Now, the comfortable version of this story is "what a charming liar," and then we all move on. But that version quietly lets everyone else in the room off the hook, and it completely misses how the trick actually worked. [PAUSE] Because London in the early 1820s was not being careful. It was in a full-blown mania. [B-ROLL: newspapers, excited crowds, bond notices for the new South American republics] The colonies of South America had just broken away from Spain, one after another, and London was falling over itself to lend money to the shiny new republics. Colombia, Chile, Peru, all of them borrowing, all of them the talk of the town. Everyone knew somebody who was getting rich on foreign bonds. [PAUSE] In a room like that, MacGregor did not stand out as a lunatic. He looked like one more exciting new country in a year that was absolutely stuffed with them, and almost nobody was doing the boring work of checking whether the countries on the list were real. Selling Poyais in 1822 was like selling shares in a company with no product at the very top of a bubble: the mania does the underwriting for you, and all you have to supply is the paperwork. [BEAT] When that bubble finally burst a couple of years later, plenty of the genuine South American bonds collapsed right alongside his fake one. In the wreckage, Poyais had a lot of company.

[B-ROLL: two ships, the Honduras Packet and the Kennersley Castle, pulling out of a grey harbour] But long before the financial reckoning, there were the settlers, and they were already at sea. At least two ships made the voyage, the Honduras Packet and then the Kennersley Castle, carrying somewhere around two hundred and fifty people out to claim the land they had bought and paid for.

[B-ROLL: settlers on an empty beach, trunks in the sand, a wall of jungle behind, no city anywhere] They arrived on the Mosquito Coast expecting a country, and they found a coastline. There was no St Joseph waiting for them, no cathedral, no harbour, no opera house, nothing but jungle and the rotting remains of an earlier settlement that had already failed on that exact stretch of shore. [BEAT] Some of them reportedly stood there on the sand, holding official deeds to plots in a city that had never been built, and still could not quite make themselves believe it. The paperwork had been that good.

[B-ROLL: rain, a makeshift camp, illness, muted colours] Then the rainy season arrived, and tropical disease arrived with it. They had no real shelter, no supplies that mattered, and no country to turn to for help. Eventually word reached the British settlement over at Belize, and boats were sent to bring back whoever was still alive to be brought back. [PAUSE:LONG] Of the roughly two hundred and fifty people who had sailed out full of hope, more than half never came home. [BEAT] They had paid for a new life with the only thing they had left, which was the old one.

[B-ROLL: London newspapers, a headline breaking; MacGregor quietly boarding a coach] So the survivors come home, the newspapers get hold of it, and the whole thing is exposed. This is the part of the story where the con man finally gets what is coming to him. [PAUSE] Except that is not what happened. MacGregor slipped across the Channel to France. [B-ROLL: Paris streets, a fresh office, new stacks of Poyais paperwork] And in France, almost unbelievably, he set about running the entire scheme a second time: new investors, new hopeful emigrants, the same country that still did not exist. This time the authorities did arrest him, and they did put him on trial for fraud. [PAUSE] And a French court let him walk.

[B-ROLL: the narrator avatar, a small, flat shrug] He was never actually convicted over Poyais, not in France and not in Britain. He kept a few smaller versions of the scheme limping along for years afterward. And then, near the end of his life, he moved to Venezuela, the country he really had helped to liberate as a young soldier. They welcomed him back as a citizen and an army general, granted him a pension, and when he died in 1845, they buried him with full military honours.

[B-ROLL: a single Poyais banknote lying still on a dark surface] We would like to believe that a lie this big has to collapse under its own weight and take the liar down with it. [PAUSE] Poyais is the quiet reminder that it does not always work that way, especially not when enough comfortable people have a reason to look the other way. The families who trusted him lost everything they owned, and a lot of them lost their lives. The man who invented the entire country got a state funeral. [PAUSE:LONG] In the end, the country was the only thing in the whole story that ever paid a price for not being real.
