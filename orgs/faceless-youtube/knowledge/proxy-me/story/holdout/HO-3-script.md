# The Two Brothers Who Tried to Buy All the Silver in the World

**Channel:** The Second Take
**Word count:** ~1,540 words (~10 min)

---

In January of 1980, silver was worth almost fifty dollars an ounce. Four years earlier it had been worth about six. And the reason it climbed that high was not some shortage, and it was not some war. It was two brothers from Texas who had quietly decided to buy up as much of the silver on Earth as money could buy. [PAUSE] They very nearly pulled it off. And when it fell apart, it fell apart in a single afternoon that almost took a chunk of Wall Street down with it.

[B-ROLL: silver bars stacked, cold blue light, slow push-in]

So how do two people end up owning a corner of an entire metal? It starts with their father.

The Hunts were oil money. Serious oil money. Their dad was H.L. Hunt, one of the richest men in America, the kind of rich where the number stops meaning anything. And when he died, he left his sons Nelson Bunker Hunt and William Herbert Hunt a fortune most countries would envy. [BEAT] Bunker, the older one, was the driver here. Heavyset, rumpled, flew coach even though he could have bought the airline. He did not look like a man about to shake the global economy. He looked like a man who might fall asleep in a meeting.

[B-ROLL: 1970s Dallas, oil derricks, grainy archival]

But Bunker had one big worry, and in the 1970s it was a very reasonable worry. Inflation. Prices were climbing and the dollar was losing value every single year, and Bunker looked at all that oil money sitting in dollars and saw it quietly melting. He wanted something real. Something you could hold, that governments could not just print more of. [PAUSE] He wanted silver.

Now, a normal rich person who likes silver buys some silver. Maybe a lot of silver. Bunker was not a normal rich person. Bunker looked at the entire world supply of silver and thought, in effect, what if I just got most of it.

[B-ROLL: a single silver coin spinning, then multiplying across the screen]

Here is the thing that made it possible. There are two ways to buy silver. You can buy the actual metal, the bars, and stack it in a vault. Or you can buy a promise, a contract that says somebody will hand you a pile of silver on a future date at a price you lock in today. That is a silver future, and it is how the big players trade metal without ever touching it. Almost nobody who buys those contracts ever wants the real silver. They cash out before the date and pocket the difference.

Bunker wanted the real silver. Every time one of his contracts came due, he did the thing nobody does. He took delivery. He made them actually hand over the metal. [BEAT] And that is where it gets dangerous, because the whole system quietly assumes you will not do that. There is not enough silver sitting around for everyone to be paid in real bars at once. Bunker figured that out, and he decided to lean on it with everything he had.

[B-ROLL: armored trucks, vault doors, bars being loaded]

He and his brother started buying in the early 1970s and they did not stop. They bought contracts, took delivery, bought more. They flew silver out of the country and stored it in Switzerland, reportedly filling chartered planes with the stuff, guarded by Texas cowboys they hired for the job, because why not. And when people started to notice two Texans hoovering up the market, Bunker did the smart thing and found partners. He brought in a group of wealthy investors from Saudi Arabia, oil money meeting oil money, and together they kept buying.

By the end of the 1970s, the Hunts and their allies controlled a genuinely absurd share of all the silver on the planet that anyone could actually get their hands on. Some estimates put it at more than half. [PAUSE] And when you own that much of a thing, you stop having to guess what the price will do. You are the price.

[B-ROLL: line chart climbing, six dollars crawling up toward fifty]

Silver went from six dollars, to ten, to twenty, to thirty. The more it climbed, the more everyone else wanted in, which pushed it higher, which made the Hunts richer on paper, which let them borrow against that paper to buy even more. It fed on itself. By January of 1980 silver touched nearly fifty dollars an ounce, and on paper the two brothers from Texas were sitting on one of the largest fortunes any private person had ever assembled. Billions. In silver.

And out in the real world, ordinary people were feeling it. This is the part that always gets me. When silver hit fifty dollars, the forks and spoons in people's kitchen drawers were suddenly worth real money. So they sold them. Families lined up outside dealers with the good silverware, the tea sets, grandma's candlesticks, and watched them get weighed and melted down. [BEAT] Whole boxes of heirlooms turned into bars because two men in Texas had made the metal too valuable to keep as a fork.

[B-ROLL: hands emptying a felt-lined silverware box onto a scale]

But here is the trouble with owning half of something. Everyone else can see you doing it. And the people who ran the market were watching two men march the price of silver straight up, and they did not love where it was heading.

The place these contracts get traded had a board of directors. And a lot of the people on that board were, conveniently, people who had bet the other way, people who would lose if silver kept climbing. [PAUSE] So they changed the rules. In the middle of the game.

[B-ROLL: a boardroom, empty chairs, a rulebook on the table]

They passed something that got nicknamed Silver Rule 7. And the plain-English version is this. They made it so you basically could not buy any more silver on borrowed money. You could sell, but you could not build the pile any bigger. For a man whose entire plan depended on borrowing against his silver to buy more silver, forever, that was the floor giving way. The buying stopped. And the second the buying stopped, everyone finally exhaled and started to sell.

The price did not drift down. It fell off a cliff.

[B-ROLL: the chart line collapsing, fifty plunging back toward ten]

Which brings us to the part with the fun name. March 27, 1980. Silver Thursday. In a matter of days silver had lost more than half its value, and on that Thursday it just came apart, dropping to around ten dollars. [BEAT] And remember how the Hunts had borrowed against their silver to buy more of it. That works beautifully on the way up. On the way down it is a nightmare, because now the lenders want their money, and the thing you promised them is worth a fraction of what it was last week.

The brothers got a margin call. That is the phrase for when your lender calls and says the collateral has cratered, we need cash, today. The Hunts got one for over a billion dollars. [PAUSE] And they did not have it. Not in cash. Their money was the silver, and the silver was the problem.

[B-ROLL: a phone ringing on an empty desk, unanswered]

And this is where two rich guys losing a bet turns into everybody's problem. The Hunts owed that money to a brokerage, and that brokerage owed money to banks, and those banks were tangled up with other banks, and all of a sudden the people in charge realized that if the Hunt brothers went down, they might drag a piece of the whole financial system down with them. Wall Street had a genuinely bad few days wondering if it was about to get pulled into a hole two Texans had dug chasing silverware.

So they did the thing you do when the fire is too big to let burn. A group of banks got together and arranged an enormous loan, something like a billion dollars, to keep the Hunts standing and stop the whole thing from unraveling. Not a rescue for the brothers so much as a rescue for everyone standing next to them.

[B-ROLL: bank headquarters at night, lights on in one floor]

The Hunts survived Silver Thursday. But the empire did not really recover. They spent years in courtrooms and hearings, answering for the corner they had tried to build, and slowly the fortune bled out through the debts and the lawyers and the interest on that giant loan. By the late 1980s Bunker Hunt, a man who had once tried to own most of a precious metal on Earth, filed for bankruptcy.

[PAUSE]

Somewhere out there, a lot of family silver never came home. It got weighed, and melted, and poured into bars during the few strange months when a fork was worth more than the meal, all because two brothers from Texas looked at the entire world's silver and genuinely believed they could hold it in their hands.
